### EDITION PAGE

This document is a procurement market study prepared for the acquisition of exterior
lighting poles in the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago. It is a working commercial
instrument and is intended to be used to solicit formal quotations. It is not a
priced tender return and it is not a substitute for supplier quotations.

All figures presented in this document are separated into two classes, and the
distinction is binding on any reader using this document:

**CLASS V — VERIFIED.** A published price traced to a named source and a live URL,
recorded with the date of verification.

**CLASS D — DERIVED.** An arithmetic output of a cost model built on Class V inputs
plus stated assumptions. A Class D figure is a planning number. It is not a quotation,
it is not an offer, and it must not be entered into a bid, a budget submission or a
contract without substitution by a supplier quotation.

No price in this document has been fabricated. Where a price could not be verified,
the entry reads **PRICE ON REQUEST** and the supplier contact required to obtain a
quotation is supplied in its place.

# SECTION 0 — CRITICAL FINDING: DATA AVAILABILITY

This finding is placed first because it governs every number that follows.

**The Trinidad and Tobago light pole market does not publish prices.**

An exhaustive search across local hardware suppliers, electrical wholesalers, lighting
retailers, steel fabricators, solar integrators, business directories, social commerce
channels, utility publications and government procurement portals returned **no
published TT$ price for any street lighting pole, from any Trinidad and Tobago supplier.**

This is not a gap in the search. It is the structure of the market. Lighting columns in
Trinidad and Tobago are sold as project supply, not as shelf stock. Pricing is released
against a drawing, a height, a wind loading and a quantity, and it is released by
quotation only. The suppliers who hold this product are importers and fabricators who
price per enquiry.

Three consequences follow, and they are stated plainly rather than worked around:

**One.** The brief calls for a table of suppliers ranked from lowest price to highest
price. That table cannot be built from public sources today. What is delivered in its
place at Section 4 is every price that could be verified, ranked as instructed, with the
Trinidad and Tobago entries carrying PRICE ON REQUEST and a contact route.

**Two.** The brief calls for cross-checking each price against at least two independent
sources. Two independent sources do not exist for any Trinidad and Tobago pole price.
Cross-checking has instead been applied to the import benchmark tier, where multiple
independent published sources do exist.

**Three.** Technical constraint of this working session: outbound access to Trinidad and
Tobago domains, including ttec.co.tt, lighthousett.com, bhsil.com, trifactorsolar.com,
findyello.com and mpu.gov.tt, was blocked at the network layer. Supplier identity,
location and contact data were recovered through search indexing rather than direct page
retrieval. Every contact number in Section 3 should be confirmed on first call. This
constraint did not change the finding at Section 0, because the same finding was returned
independently through every other channel searched.

**What this document therefore is.** It is the instrument that converts an unpriced
market into a priced one. It fixes the specification, names the suppliers who can meet
it, supplies their contact routes, models the cost floor from verified import data so
that incoming quotations can be judged rather than merely received, and appends a
Request for Quotation ready to be issued.

The question in the brief was: *what lengths of light poles can I buy in Trinidad and
Tobago, and what should I realistically expect to pay for them?*

The lengths are answered from verified data at Section 2.

The price is answered at Section 5 and Section 6 as a modelled band, and the model is
shown in full so that every assumption in it can be challenged, replaced or defended.

# SECTION 1 — SCOPE AND CLASSIFICATION

## 1.1 The distinction that governs this document

The brief instructs that lighting poles must not be confused with T&TEC electrical
distribution poles. That distinction is fixed here and applied throughout.

### LIGHTING POLE (also: lighting column, light standard)

A standalone structural column whose only function is to carry a luminaire at a designed
mounting height. It terminates at a machined base plate which is bolted to a cast
concrete foundation through a pre-set anchor bolt cage. It carries no distribution
conductor. Its internal cable is a single low voltage feed to the luminaire, entering
through a cable door in the shaft and terminating at a fused cut-out mounted inside the
door aperture.

Typical construction in the Trinidad and Tobago market: hot dip galvanized steel, Q235
or S235JR grade, octagonal or round tapered profile, 3mm to 6mm wall, base plate with
four anchor bolt holes, 65 to 85 micron zinc coating.

Ownership: the developer, the property owner, the Regional Corporation or the Ministry.

### UTILITY / DISTRIBUTION POLE

A pole owned and installed by the Trinidad and Tobago Electricity Commission to carry
medium voltage or low voltage distribution conductors, transformers, cut-outs and
service drops. A street lamp bracket is frequently attached to it, which is the source of
the confusion this section exists to remove. The lamp is a secondary attachment. The pole
exists to carry the network.

Typical construction in Trinidad and Tobago: treated timber (imported), and pre-stressed
spun concrete. Direct buried, not base plated.

Ownership: T&TEC.

**Procurement consequence.** A developer cannot buy a distribution pole. It is not a
purchasable article in the ordinary market; it is network plant installed by the utility
under its own standards, and the developer's route to it is an application to T&TEC, not
a purchase order to a supplier. A developer *can* buy a lighting column, from any of the
suppliers at Section 3. Where a scheme requires both, they are two separate procurement
tracks running to two separate counterparties. Confusing them is the most common and most
expensive error in Trinidad and Tobago lighting procurement, because it produces a budget
that funds columns and a programme that waits on a utility application nobody filed.

**Finding on the imperial lengths in the brief.** The brief prioritises 10, 12, 15, 20,
25, 30, 35 and 40 ft. Of these, 10 ft, 20 ft and 30 ft map cleanly onto the 3m, 6m and 9m
production standards. The others (12, 15, 25, 35, 40 ft) do not sit on a metric production
step. They are obtainable, but they are cut to order rather than drawn from a standard
line, and that carries a cost and lead time penalty of consequence.

**Recommendation.** Specify in metric. A 26 ft requirement specified as 8m buys from the
standard line. The same requirement specified as 26 ft may be quoted as a custom cut at a
premium of roughly 15 to 25 per cent with four to six weeks added to lead time, for a
mounting height difference of 0.08 m that no lighting design will notice. This single
drafting decision is worth more than most of the negotiating leverage available elsewhere
in the purchase.

**Most commonly sold height in Trinidad and Tobago.** *Class D — inferred, not verified.*
No published sales data exists. The inference from application mix, T&TEC street lighting
practice and standard production lines is that **6m (20ft) and 8m (26ft) carry the volume**,
with 6m dominant in residential and car park work and 8m dominant in street and commercial
work. This should be confirmed against the first three quotations received, because every
supplier will disclose which height they hold or have run most recently.

**T&TEC tariff structure, verified.** Two street lighting rates operate. Under **S1**,
T&TEC furnishes, installs and owns the poles, overhead wiring, circuits and fittings, and
bears the full cost of installation including fittings. Under **S2**, a lower rate, the
customer contributes the cost of the fittings. Charges are annual, rendered monthly at
one twelfth. The numeric rates are published on the T&TEC tariff schedule and must be
obtained directly; they were not retrievable during this study.

**Why this matters to a developer.** S1 removes the pole from the capital budget entirely
and converts it to an operating charge. For a residential development handing over to a
Regional Corporation, an S1 application may be commercially superior to buying columns at
all. That comparison should be run before any purchase order is raised. It is the single
highest-value enquiry on the call list at Section 10.

**Sensitivity warning.** The duty rate and the freight allowance are the two unverified

runs at US$130 per pole rather than US$65 the landed cost rises by roughly 40 per cent at
the low FOB end. Both are resolved by two phone calls, and both should be resolved before
this model is used for anything that binds.

## 8.5 What changes at 200 units

200 poles is not 100 poles twice. Seven things change, and they change the procurement
rather than the price.

**One. This is a tender, not a purchase.** At roughly TT$1.0M to TT$1.5M installed, this
crosses into formal tender territory. If any part of the scheme touches public money, a
Regional Corporation adoption, or a state agency, the Public Procurement and Disposal of
Public Property Act applies and its thresholds govern. **Confirm the applicable threshold
before issuing any enquiry.** A scheme of this size procured informally and later adopted
into public maintenance can be challenged retrospectively.

**Two. Local fabrication moves from parallel option to primary comparator.** 200 identical
columns is a genuine production run. Rosscon Ltd and Steel Force Caribbean Ltd can jig a
repeated design at this quantity, which changes their economics completely. Local
fabrication also removes the eight to fourteen week shipping lead time, removes the duty
exposure on finished goods, keeps the foreign exchange onshore, and puts the warranty
counterparty on the same island as the poles. **At 200 units this route must be priced,
not merely considered.**

**Three. Split the award.** Do not single-source 200 columns. Two suppliers at 100 each,
or 150 and 50 where one is clearly stronger, keeps pricing honest through the delivery
programme and removes the single point of failure. A supplier who knows they hold the
whole order behaves differently from one who knows the balance is contestable.

**Four. Stage the delivery.** 200 galvanized columns landed at once must be stored. Stacked
in the open in this climate they develop wet storage stain, the white powdery zinc
corrosion that forms when galvanized surfaces sit in contact with trapped moisture. It is
cosmetically severe and on a thin coating it is structurally consequential. Match the
delivery programme to the installation programme, take the columns in two or three
tranches, and specify that the supplier stack them spaced and angled for drainage.

**Five. Pre-shipment inspection becomes affordable.** At 200 units a third party factory
inspection costs a small fraction of the order and verifies galvanizing thickness, weld
quality and dimensional compliance before the containers sail. At 25 units this is hard to
justify. At 200 it is negligent to skip. Make it a condition of the final payment
instalment.

**Six. You have payment terms leverage.** At this quantity do not accept 100 per cent
telegraphic transfer in advance. A letter of credit, or 30 per cent deposit against 70 per
cent on presentation of shipping documents and a clean inspection report, is achievable
and it is the only real protection an importer has.

**Seven. Buy spares in the same run.** Order 3 to 5 per cent spares, so six to ten
additional columns, in the same production batch. A replacement column ordered in two
years is a minimum order quantity, a fresh freight charge and a colour and finish that
will not match. Spares bought now cost roughly TT$10,000 to TT$16,000 excl VAT and will
save a multiple of that the first time a column is struck by a vehicle.

## 9.1 Cheapest supplier

**Cannot be named. Zero verified T&T quotations exist.**

The cheapest *channel* is direct import against the FOB evidence at Section 4.1, at a
modelled 31 per cent below local distributor purchase. The cheapest *supplier* will be

## 9.2 Best-value supplier

**Recommended for first approach: Electrical Industries Group (Agos Lighting).**

*Basis, Class D.* It is the only entity in the register that is a Caribbean lighting
manufacturer rather than a reseller, with over eighty years of manufacturing history and
a dedicated industrial lighting brand. Manufacturer status means engineering support, a
warranty counterparty inside the region, and the ability to quote against a specification
rather than a catalogue page. On a lighting scheme those are worth more than a low unit
price, because the failure modes that hurt are photometric non-compliance and premature
corrosion, and neither is caught by a cheap quotation.

*Confirmation test.* Ask whether they supply the column or only the luminaire. If column
supply is outside their scope, the recommendation transfers to Lighthouse Ltd on the
strength of its four-branch national footprint and established import lines.

## 9.3 Best option for bulk purchase

**At 50 to 100 units: direct import via consolidated container.**

Below 50 units the freight penalty and the import overhead erode the advantage. At 100
units the buyer is in two full 40ft containers, freight per unit falls, and FOB volume
discounting becomes available. Run the enquiry through at least three Chinese
manufacturers with SGS and ISO certification and demand a mill certificate and a
galvanizing thickness certificate as a condition of payment.

**At 200 units: run direct import and local fabrication as a genuine two-horse race, and
split the award.**

This is the central recommendation for the 200 unit case and it differs from the
recommendation at every smaller quantity.

Direct import at 200 units models at **TT$324,355 excl VAT** for Package C hardware, against
**TT$461,271** from a local distributor project desk at a 15 per cent trade discount. The
modelled gap is **TT$136,916 excl VAT**, and that gap is what justifies carrying the import
risk.

But 200 identical columns is also the quantity at which **Rosscon Ltd** and **Steel Force
Caribbean Ltd** can jig a repeated design, and local fabrication at that point is not a
courtesy enquiry. It removes the shipping lead time, the duty exposure on finished goods,
the foreign exchange outflow and the absent warranty counterparty, all at once. Neither
fabricator publishes a rate, so the comparison cannot be made from this desk. **It must be
made from their quotations, and at 200 units both must be asked.**

Then split the award. 100 and 100, or 150 and 50 where one supplier is clearly stronger.
Single-sourcing 200 columns hands the supplier the whole programme and removes every
lever you hold once the deposit is paid.

Three conditions on any 200 unit award, each of which is cheap now and expensive later:
third party pre-shipment inspection as a condition of final payment; staged delivery
matched to the installation programme rather than one 200 column drop; and 3 to 5 per cent
spares produced in the same batch. Section 8.5 sets out why each of these earns its place.

## 9.4 Best option for a residential development

**First action: file an S1 enquiry with T&TEC before buying anything.**

Under the verified S1 tariff, T&TEC furnishes, installs and owns the poles, overhead
wiring, circuits and fittings, and bears the full installation cost including fittings.
For a residential estate that will hand its roads to a Regional Corporation, this removes
the entire capital cost of the lighting from the development budget and converts it to a
tariff charge. Section 8.3 models a 50-pole residential scheme at roughly TT$318,000 excl
VAT purchased and installed. An S1 route may reduce that capital line to near zero.

If S1 is refused or unsuitable, specify **6m (20ft) octagonal hot dip galvanized, 85
micron, single 1.5m arm at 5 degrees, 60W to 80W LED at 3000K**. The 3000K warm colour
temperature matters in residential work; 5000K white reads as institutional and generates
resident complaints.

## 9.5 Best option for roadway and street lighting

**8m (26ft) for distributor roads, 10m to 12m (33ft to 39ft) for highway.**

Specify octagonal tapered hot dip galvanized to 85 micron minimum, wall thickness 4mm at
8m rising to 6mm at 12m, base plate with four M24 anchor bolts, and a stated wind loading.

**Wind rating is the specification most often omitted and it is the one that matters most
here.** Trinidad and Tobago sits at the southern margin of the hurricane belt and is not
exempt from it. Require the supplier to certify the column against a design wind speed
and to state the standard used. A column supplied without a wind certificate is a column
supplied without an engineer behind it.

Any roadway scheme intended for public adoption must additionally be cleared against
T&TEC's *Wiring for Light and Power* (8th edition) and, for major roadways, routed
through MOWT.

## 9.6 Most common pole height sold in Trinidad

**Class D, inferred.** 6m (20ft) and 8m (26ft). See Section 2. Confirm against the first
three RFQ returns, each of which will disclose held stock.
