## 3. PRINCIPAL FINDINGS

### 3.1 There is no published pole price in either country, and that is commercially meaningful

Across every search term in the brief, in both countries, **zero** published prices for galvanized lighting poles were returned. Every priced pole found was from a United States retailer or a Chinese or Indian export platform.

The correct reading of that silence is not that the market is hidden. It is that **200 galvanized lighting poles is a made-to-order transaction in both Trinidad and Guyana, not a shelf purchase.** Neither country has a retail SKU for this item because neither country sells it off a shelf. Every serious route to 200 poles is either a fabrication order or a container import, and both are priced by quotation against a drawing.

The practical consequence: the procurement does not begin with price comparison. It begins with issuing a specification. Section 8 provides one.

### 3.2 No dedicated lighting-pole manufacturer was identified in either country

No firm in Trinidad and Tobago or Guyana was found advertising street-lighting poles as a product line. What exists is the capability to make them:

- **Trinidad has the galvanizing.** Varma Iron and Steel operates hot-dip galvanizing at Claxton Bay, and its own site markets that service into Guyana, Dominica and the wider Caribbean (SEARCH-INDEX FACT). Damus Limited is described as among the largest mechanical fabrication firms in the country, already serving Guyana and Suriname (SEARCH-INDEX FACT).
- **Guyana has fabrication but unproven galvanizing at scale.** E. C. Vieira Investment Ltd. reports over 100 welders and fabricators (SEARCH-INDEX FACT). No commercial hot-dip galvanizing kettle in Guyana was identified in any search. UNKNOWN whether one exists.

This asymmetry is the single most important technical finding in the study. A pole fabricated in Guyana would, on present evidence, still have to travel to be galvanized — most plausibly to Trinidad. A pole fabricated in Trinidad is galvanized in the same country, arguably on the same industrial estate.

### 3.3 CARICOM origin is the decisive commercial lever

Goods of CARICOM origin move duty-free between member states under the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas, subject to the origin rules and a preferential Certificate of Origin, which exporTT issues in Trinidad and Tobago (SEARCH-INDEX FACT). Guyana applies the Common External Tariff, spanning roughly 0 to 20 per cent, to extra-regional goods, and levies VAT at 14 per cent calculated on the CIF value **plus** duty and other charges (SEARCH-INDEX FACT).

A pole fabricated and galvanized in Trinidad, properly certified, therefore lands in Guyana at **zero duty**. An identical pole from China pays the CET on the tariff line, and then pays 14 per cent VAT on a base inflated by that duty.

This does not automatically make Trinidad cheaper — a Chinese pole can start from a very much lower ex-works figure — but it narrows the gap by the full duty amount plus the VAT charged on that duty, and it is the reason the Trinidad route deserves a genuine quotation rather than dismissal.

The exact CET line for lighting columns (HS 7308.90 *columns, pillars, posts* versus HS 9405.99 *lamp-post parts*) could **not** be confirmed; the CARICOM tariff schedule was unreachable. **This must be settled with a Guyana Revenue Authority ruling before any import decision.** The classification is worth real money and it is contestable.

### 3.4 Duty relief may be available regardless of origin

Where the poles serve an approved project, an Investment Agreement facilitated by GO-Invest can waive Customs Duty, Excise Tax and VAT on relevant imports, with the Guyana Revenue Authority ruling on eligibility (SEARCH-INDEX FACT). If the project qualifies, the CARICOM advantage at 3.3 is neutralised and the comparison reverts to ex-works price plus freight alone. **Establish the project's concession status before choosing a route** — it changes the answer.

Two points follow, and both matter to the quotation:

**A 20 ft container is unusable.** Its internal length is about 5.9 m. A 6 m pole will not fit. Every height in this brief requires 40 ft equipment.

**The 25 ft option is the efficiency sweet spot.** At 8 m, 200 poles land within a whisker of a single container's payload. Specifying 7.5 m rather than 8 m, or shaving the wall to 3.5 mm where the wind calculation permits, would bring the whole order inside **one** 40 ft container instead of two. On a short regional leg that is close to a halving of the freight line. This is the one specification decision in the study with a large and immediate cost consequence, and it should be tested with the structural engineer before the enquiry goes out.

## 7. PROCUREMENT RECOMMENDATION

Answering the brief's questions with the evidence that exists:

**Cheapest 20 ft option** — UNKNOWN. No price exists publicly in either country.
**Cheapest 25 ft option** — UNKNOWN.
**Cheapest 30 ft option** — UNKNOWN.

**Best Trinidad supplier** — **Varma Iron and Steel Co. Ltd.** It is the only firm identified in either country combining structural steel supply with in-house hot-dip galvanizing, and it already markets that capability into Guyana. On a galvanized-pole order, moving the steel to the zinc is a real cost and a real schedule risk; Varma removes that leg.

**Best Guyana supplier** — **E. C. Vieira Investment Ltd.**, on fabrication depth. Qualified: no Guyanese hot-dip galvanizing was identified, so their galvanizing route must be the first question asked. If the answer is "we send it to Trinidad," the Guyana-local option is not local, and its economics collapse into the Trinidad option plus two sea legs.

**Supplier most capable of 200 units** — UNKNOWN on present evidence. Note that 200 poles is a modest fabrication run, roughly 15 to 34 tonnes of steel. It is not a large order for any of T1, T2 or G2. Capacity is unlikely to be the binding constraint; galvanizing kettle length and schedule are the more probable ones. A 9 m pole needs a long kettle or double-dipping, and double-dipping shows.

**Strongest locally manufactured option** — Trinidad fabrication with Trinidad galvanizing, exported under CARICOM origin. It is the only route that is simultaneously regionally made, duty-free into Guyana, and three days from the port.

**Strongest imported option** — An established extra-regional octagonal-pole manufacturer shipping direct. Lowest probable ex-works cost by a wide margin, offset by CET plus VAT-on-duty, a long lead time, and no recourse if 200 poles arrive wrong.

**Best technical specification** — Section 5.1, with hot-dip galvanizing to ASTM A123 and the zinc thickness stated, not left to the fabricator.

**Best overall value** — Not determinable without quotations. The honest statement of the position: the Trinidad route is **structurally advantaged** by CARICOM origin and three-day transit, and the extra-regional route is **structurally advantaged** on ex-works price. Which wins depends on the size of the duty, and the duty depends on a tariff classification that could not be confirmed here.

**Is Trinidad-to-Guyana export cheaper than buying locally in Guyana?** On present evidence, **probably yes, and possibly by a wide margin** — because "buying locally in Guyana" appears not to mean local manufacture. No Guyanese pole manufacturer and no Guyanese galvanizing plant was found. A Guyanese distributor's price would carry an import that already paid CET and VAT, plus their margin. The Trinidad route pays no duty and no intermediary. This finding is stated with moderate confidence and should be tested by quotation, not assumed.

**Estimated total budget for 200 poles** — Not stated. A budget figure invented without a single supplier price would be worthless for the purpose it would be used for, which is committing money.

**Potential saving between cheapest and most expensive credible options** — UNKNOWN.

## 9. IMMEDIATE ACTIONS BEYOND THE FIVE

1. **Obtain a GRA tariff ruling** on the classification of octagonal lighting columns — HS 7308.90 against HS 9405.99. The rate difference is money and the classification is arguable.
2. **Establish the project's GO-Invest concession status.** If duty and VAT are waived, the CARICOM advantage disappears and the extra-regional route likely wins outright.
3. **Have the structural engineer fix the wind loading** and confirm whether an 8 m pole can be specified to fit 200 units into a single 40 ft container (Section 5.3). Settle this before the enquiries go out, not after.
4. **Decide the outreach arm, door, and luminaire scope now.** Poles quoted bare against poles quoted complete are not comparable, and the difference will be discovered late if it is not fixed early.
