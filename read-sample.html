BOOK I

Prologue

Tarrid

Movement I: Before Tarrid

Movement II: A Vision

He had been dreaming her for three years.

Not continuously. Not every night. It came unbidden and clear, and no use to him, because what it showed was never the situation you were in.

In the dream she was very small. She was the size of a thing that had not yet learned what size meant. He was carrying her in the crook of his left arm (the arm that had, in nine years of campaigning, learned to carry a blade at the correct angle for the longest possible sustained engagement) and the arm made no complaint. She fit easily. This was the part that stayed with him when he woke: the exact grain of the weight, never her face, which he could never quite resolve. The set rightness of it.

She was looking at something he couldn’t see, her grey eyes (and he knew, in the dream, that the eyes were grey) fixed on a point at the edge of his sight that moved whenever he tried to look at it directly.

He did not know what she was looking at.

He had the sense that she did.

· · ·

In the second dream she was older. Seven years, perhaps. Perhaps eight. She was standing in a chamber he recognised without having been in it, the lower foundries of the southern crag, the dark stone, the heat, the exact grain of light that came off metal at sustained temperature.

She stood very still at the centre of the chamber.

The Vah’Sumir surgical college was doing something to her that he could not see directly. It happened at the edges of the dream. He could see the result. The result was the stillness. The stillness of a child who has learned, at an age when most children are still learning to run, that the appropriate response to pain is not to move. Not to make a sound. Not to give the pain the satisfaction of acknowledgement.

He could see her hands.

Her hands were flat against her thighs. The knuckles were white. The copper line at her inner wrist (already there, already burning) was the brightest thing in the room.

He tried to go to her. In the dream, he always tried to go to her.

In the dream, he was always on the wrong side of the chamber.

· · ·

In the third dream she was winning.

He saw it in fragments, as campaign dispatches come when the relay stations are overwhelmed: out of order, missing context, the most important details carried in the gaps between what is stated. A horde moving at dawn across a plain he didn’t recognise. A flag going up on a wall that might have been New Terra. The chant (and he knew it was the chant, though he had never heard it, because in the dream he knew everything except its price) rolling across the ground below a ship’s keel.

She was at the centre of all of it, eight feet tall and every centimetre of that height, and the hordes behind her moved as the sea moves when something large passes beneath it, answering a presence rather than a command.

He had commanded armies. He knew the difference.

· · ·

In the last dream she stood on the steps of the Spire of New Terra, speaking.

He could not hear all of it. He could hear the grain of it (the register she used, which was the Vah’Sumir rolling register that carried across open ground without effort) and he could hear fragments:

“I take the Dragon Throne under the protocols of the Trinitarian succession.”

And then, after a silence that the dream gave him in full:

“I take it as the daughter of the line. I take it as the inheritor of the Black Throne. I take it as the first of either. I will not be the last.”

The chant answered. He couldn’t hear the word. He knew, as you know things in dreams, that it was one syllable.

He knew it was hers.

· · ·

He came back to himself in the command cabin of the Aurex-Tertia on the morning that changed the succession.

A bowl of cold barley sat in front of him. A wooden spoon lay in his right hand. The lamp at the table’s far end put out the same low amber light it always did, steady now after hours of burning.

He was twenty-five years old. He had not yet been to Hesh-Kar. The wrongness inside him (the inheritance that Sono’Rah would press into his sternum on a basalt ridge in the fourth year from now) was not yet there. He was, at this moment, a Crown Prince with a battle beginning somewhere beyond the hull of his ship.

He set down the wooden spoon.

The dream was already going.

He let it go. He had learned, in three years of dreaming her, not to try to hold it. The details dissolved as things do when they are not ready to be used yet. The face he could never quite resolve. The weight in the crook of his left arm. The coronation steps and the one syllable and the chant that was hers.

He turned the slate over.

He read.

Tarrid: Wars Make Fathers

Before Asha Kers I was conceived, before the Black Throne was cut from the voidstone of the Black Cavern in the lower foundries of the southern crag of Vahharoon, before the Goliath broke orbit at New Terra at the head of the Wretch and the Apex, the Star Wolves under Khuvius Augustus Pierre von Care fought five wars across the rim of the Trinitarian frontier.

The Star Wolves burned the slaver cartels off the basalt deserts of Tessek-Minor in the Prince’s twenty-third year, broke the Threxian secessionists at the Khotai pass in his twenty-fourth, and put down the heretic admiral Senec at the orbital battle of Tarrid in his twenty-fifth, the Janissary mutinies on the inner shipyards in his twenty-seventh, and the Caspari border raids on the trans-Belt corridor in his twenty-ninth.

Each campaign tightened the eleven commanders who stood at his shoulder. Each campaign carved one more ridge of scar across the gauntlets of Mercurio of the Third Wing, and Nayra of Kithoun, and Vor’Dressal of the First Horde, and Saksai of the Black Steppe, and the seven others who would, in time, be called the Star Wolves by every body of troops that marched under the Prince’s standard.

Three of those wars are recounted here in their proper order, because they make the Prince. The fourth and fifth are recorded in the Khanate’s Greater Annals. The sixth war was Hesh-Kar, and Hesh-Kar made his daughter.

Movement III: The Orbital Battle of Tarrid

The Prince was twenty-five years when the heretic admiral Senec broke for the open dark.

Senec had been Imperator of the Sixth Fleet for nine years. He had drunk at the Emperor Livius’s right hand in the great hall of the Spire on six successive Coronation Days. He had stood at Khuvius’s own confirmation as Crown Prince and had laid his palm flat against the boy’s sternum and kept it there for a beat, the customary touch of an admiral pledging his fleet to the line of succession.

In the autumn of the Prince’s twenty-fifth year, Senec turned the entire Sixth Fleet (four hundred and eleven hulls, three battle stations, and the orbital refinery at Tarrid) against the Trinitarian central admiralty.

He did so on the morning the Crown Prince’s flagship Aurex-Tertia was already fourteen hours into a routine rotation through the Tarrid sector.

Khuvius received the alert in his command cabin. He was seated at the long obsidian table that ran the length of the cabin, eating a bowl of cold barley with a wooden spoon. The barley was salted heavily. He was alone in the cabin. The lamp above the table burned at three-quarters intensity. The deck under his bare feet was warm.

The runner who brought the alert did not speak. The runner placed the slate face down on the obsidian beside his bowl and stepped back two paces and waited. The runner was a girl of perhaps fourteen years, in the dark grey of the Prince’s household, with the small wolf-head at her collar that marked her as Mercurio’s. Her hands hung at her sides. Her eyes did not meet his.

He set down the wooden spoon. He turned the slate over. He read.

The text was eleven lines. The eleventh line was the order of battle Senec had filed with the central admiralty four hours earlier, a public declaration of war against the throne to which he had pledged his fleet.

The Prince read the eleven lines twice.

He set the slate down. He took the wooden spoon back into his right hand. He returned to the bowl of cold barley. He ate three more spoonfuls. The salt scoured the soft tissue of his cheeks. The barley was cooler in the centre of the bowl than at the edges, and he registered the difference without comment.

When the bowl was empty, he set the spoon across the rim. He pushed the bowl one hand’s breadth toward the centre of the table. He looked up at the runner.

“Mercurio.”

“In the war room, my Prince. He has held the bridge for the past hour without your authorisation because he believed you would wish to eat first.”

“He was correct. Tell him I will be there in four minutes. Tell him to begin the deployment cycle on his own seal. Tell him I will sign the seal when I come.”

He is the Mercurio de Rothschild of the Cosmic Sagas records, born on Runeus, one of the Four Captains of the Thunderborn Niccolò Kerron von Hapsburgi during the era of God Emperor Augustus Rex (#73). He was about ten years the senior of the Emperor Livius, forty-seven years the senior of the Crown Prince Khuvius, and eighty-four years the senior of Asha Kers I. He did not appear any of these things. The Ixorian Lattice treatment (a cellular stabilisation process available only to those of extraordinary wealth, of which the de Rothschild quantite empire produced more than enough) had held his physical presentation at the approximate decade of his first treatment since early adulthood. He aged at a fraction of rate. The face that entered the war room of the Aurex-Tertia at Tarrid appeared twenty-six. It was not twenty-six. The hands on the tactical table had been on tactical tables for thirty years before Khuvius was born. The Lattice treatment produced, in many long-term carriers of Pierre von Care blood, a progressive degradation of cognitive affect known in the records as Lattice madness, a narrowing of emotional range, a flattening of moral imagination, an increasingly mechanical relationship to other persons. Mercurio did not carry Pierre von Care blood. He was de Rothschild on both sides, a line with no recorded Lattice susceptibility. The treatment extended his life without the byproduct. He remained, across a century and a half of service, precisely as human as he had been at the beginning: no more detached, no less capable of the exact grief that appears throughout his private papers.

The runner bent her knee to the deck. She rose. She turned and was gone.

Khuvius rose. He crossed the cabin to the small alcove where his armour stood on its frame. The frame was bare wood, oiled, marked at the joints by a hundred prior dressings. The armour was the field plate of the von Care line, black armanite, no ornament along the breast, the heavy shoulder mantle of woven Threxian fibre folded across the left arm of the frame.

He dressed himself. The dressing took six minutes.

He did not call for an attendant. He had not, in nine years of campaigning, called for an attendant in the dressing of his own armour. The under-sleeve fitted to the bone of his upper arm. The greave clipped at the heel and the side of the calf with two small reports of metal on metal. The breastplate seated against his sternum and the latches at the shoulder closed under the pressure of his own thumb.

When he was dressed, he stood in front of the small mirror set into the back wall of the alcove. The mirror was the size of his face. He looked at his own face in it for a beat. He did not adjust anything. He turned and left the cabin.

He reached the war room four minutes and twenty seconds after the runner had left.

The war room of the Aurex-Tertia was a hexagonal hall set into the upper spine of the cruiser. The walls of the hall were dark armanite. The ceiling was vaulted in three concentric arches, each arch carrying a band of recessed lamps that lit the hall in three tiers of brightness.

At the centre of the hall stood the tactical table. The tactical table was a circular slab of dark glass eight meters in diameter. Above the slab, suspended on a single articulated arm from the central arch, hung the holographic projector. The projector was already lit when Khuvius entered. It showed the Tarrid sector in three dimensions at one-to-one-million scale, the orange disc of the Tarrid primary at the centre, the four orbiting worlds drawn in their current positions, the orbital refinery as a small pale ring at the second world’s L2, and the Sixth Fleet in red contour overlay arrayed in three battle groups across the upper, middle, and lower planes of the Tarrid system.

Mercurio stood at the table.

He appeared twenty-six years. He stood six foot eight. He was, by true count, about seventy-two (ten years Livius’s senior, forty-seven years Khuvius’s senior) but the Ixorian Lattice treatment he had sustained since early adulthood held his presentation at the decade of his first treatment and did not advance it. He wore the field uniform of the Third Wing, dark grey, no insignia other than the small wolf-head at his collar. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow. His forearms were bare and lean. The tendons of his right wrist worked once as he traced a contour on the surface of the table.

He did not look up when Khuvius entered.

“Three groups,” Mercurio said. “Northern at the upper plane. Centre at the equatorial. Southern at the lower. He has split his battle stations one to each group. He has pulled the refinery garrison and added it to the centre. He has six hours of fuel for the heaviest hulls in the centre group and twelve for the rest. He is committed.”

“He is committed,” Khuvius said.

“He sent the declaration to the central admiralty four hours ago. He sent a parallel declaration to the Khanate’s Greater Council two hours ago. He has made it impossible for any of his sub-commanders to retreat without being branded apostate themselves. He has burned the bridge for them as well as for himself.”

“Which sub-commanders.”

“Aenar of the Sixth Fleet’s Twelfth Squadron is wavering. He has not transmitted his own confirmation of Senec’s declaration. He has not transmitted his refusal either. He is in the lower group. His squadron is at the rim of the lower group, closest to the open dark.”

“Then he can be peeled.”

“He can be peeled.”

“Saksai.”

“Already moving. Her wing is cycling out of the upper hangar in eleven minutes. She will engage Aenar’s flank with a soft signal, give him the option to break off, and if he does not break she will gut him.”

“And the centre.”

Mercurio’s jaw moved once. He set both palms flat on the surface of the tactical table. The contour overlay rippled under the warmth of his hands.

“The centre is where Senec is,” he said. “He has put his own flag aboard the Tessekai. He has surrounded the Tessekai with the heaviest cruisers in his line. He intends to draw us into the centre by holding the refinery as the obvious target. He believes we will not allow the refinery to fall. He believes we will commit our heaviest line against his heaviest line in the middle plane and he will, in the second hour of the engagement, drop the upper and lower groups onto our flanks in pincer.”

“And you intend?”

“To let him.”

Khuvius looked at him. The lamp above the table caught Mercurio’s dark eyes and held them. Mercurio did not turn from the table.

“Tell me,” Khuvius said.

“We commit the Aurex-Tertia and the eight cruisers of the Third Wing into the centre on his terms,” Mercurio said. “We accept the engagement at the equatorial plane. We take the casualties of the first hour. We do not allow the refinery to fall, we hold it with a screen of light cruisers from the Eighth Wing, which Nayra is moving into position now from the L4 trojan. When Senec drops his upper and lower groups onto our flanks at the start of the second hour, we have already pulled the upper and lower groups of his own flanks into open vector toward our centre, and at the moment they commit, Vor’Dressal takes the Second Horde out from behind the third world (where they have been drifting under cold-burn for the past sixteen hours) and they hit the rear of Senec’s centre with twenty-eight thousand Vah’Sumir boarding on heavy armour.”

“His centre will collapse on itself.”

“His centre will collapse on itself. The flanks, already committed to our flanks, will not be able to wheel back in time. We will take Senec on the bridge of the Tessekai before the third hour.”

Khuvius set his own palm flat on the surface of the table beside Mercurio’s. The dark glass was warm. The contour overlay rippled around both their hands.

“The price.”

“Twelve to fifteen hundred dead in the first hour from the Third Wing. Perhaps two thousand from the Eighth Wing if the screen of the refinery does not hold cleanly. Perhaps five hundred from the Second Horde at the moment of boarding.”

“Senec’s losses.”

“All of them.”

Khuvius held the contact with the table for a beat. He lifted his palm.

“Sign the seal,” he said. “Begin the deployment. I want every commander in every wing to know, by the time the first round is fired, that they are fighting under my hand and not under the central admiralty’s. I want them to know that this is a Star Wolf engagement and that the Star Wolf doctrine applies, no quarter to officers, full quarter to enlisted men who lay down arms before the second hour.”

“Signed already, my Prince. The deployment began on my seal twenty-six minutes ago, when the runner left for your cabin.”

“Then I confirm it now.”

Khuvius pressed his thumb to the small biometric pad at the edge of the table. The pad blinked once. The seal authenticated. The contour overlay above the table shifted as the orders propagated through the Aurex-Tertia and out to the eleven other capital ships of the Third Wing currently in the Tarrid sector.

“How long until the first round?”

“Forty-one minutes.”

“Then I have time to walk the lower decks.”

“My Prince, ”

“I have time.”

Mercurio did not argue. He bent his head a fraction. He returned his attention to the table.

Khuvius left the war room.

He walked the lower decks for thirty-four of the remaining forty-one minutes.

He took no escort. He carried his sword at his hip in its plain dark sheath. He wore his helmet under his left arm. The corridors of the Aurex-Tertia hummed under his boots with the rising vibration of the cruiser’s primary igniters coming up from cold standby to full engagement burn. The air carried the layered smell of fresh coolant, hot lubricant, and the sour edge of human sweat as the lower-deck crews ran their pre-engagement checks at the forward weapon batteries.

He walked the gun deck first. The gun deck of the Aurex-Tertia was a long armoured corridor that ran the full length of the cruiser’s spine, from the forward batteries to the aft. Six hundred and eighty crew worked the deck at full readiness. They worked in teams of four around each gun mount, two loaders, one targeting officer, one sergeant. They wore the dark grey of the Third Wing with the small wolf-head at the collar.

Khuvius walked the deck from forward to aft.

He did not speak to the crews collectively. He stopped at the seventh gun mount from the forward and laid his palm flat on the chestplate of the sergeant who commanded that mount. The sergeant was a woman of perhaps thirty years, dark-skinned, with a heavy ridge of scar across the bridge of her nose where a previous round had cooked off in the loading chamber. Her name was Tessera n’Holt. She had served under his command for six years. He knew her by sight.

He held the contact for a beat.

He moved on.

He laid his palm on the chestplate of the sergeant at the twenty-third gun mount. He laid his palm on the chestplate of the sergeant at the forty-first. He laid his palm on the chestplate of the sergeant at the sixty-second. The full deck had eighty-four mounts. He touched eight of them. He did not touch them in any pattern that the crews could anticipate. He stopped, walked on, stopped, walked on.

Each crew whose sergeant he touched stood a fraction of a degree straighter after he passed. The crews whose sergeants he did not touch worked harder for the next forty minutes than they had worked at any point in the prior week, in the slim chance that he might come back along the deck and choose them on the second pass.

He did not come back along the deck. He took the lift from the aft of the gun deck to the boarding bay.

The boarding bay was three decks below the gun deck. It was a wide, low chamber, fitted with the racks and the harnesses and the assault corridors that would, if the engagement required it, disgorge eleven hundred Vah’Sumir boarders of the Aurex-Tertia’s own complement onto whatever hull the cruiser closed alongside.

Vor’Dressal was not on the Aurex-Tertia. Vor’Dressal was on the Hammer of Khoth, behind the third world, holding cold burn with the rest of the Second Horde. The Vah’Sumir of the Aurex-Tertia’s own boarding complement were under the command of a centurion named Vor’Tesai, who stood at the head of the central assault corridor when Khuvius entered.

Vor’Tesai was eleven feet tall. He wore the heavy boarding plate of the First Horde, the green sash of his subcommand wrapped twice around his upper arm. His amber eyes tracked Khuvius from the moment the lift doors opened.

He did not bow. The Vah’Sumir did not bow to the Trinitarian princes. He grounded his percussion maul against the deck plating with a single hard report that carried the length of the bay and brought the eleven hundred boarders behind him to attention in one synchronised motion.

“My Prince.”

“Vor’Tesai.”

“The Hammer of Khoth signals ready. The Second Horde is under cold burn at the third world. They will warm at the start of the second hour. They will engage at the start of the third.”

“And your own complement.”

“Will not be deployed in the first three hours. Mercurio’s plan does not call for them. They will hold readiness in the racks and they will be available if the situation requires.”

“Good. Hold them sharp. Walk the racks. Tell them they may not see action today. Tell them they must be ready to see action tomorrow, or the day after, or the day after that. Tell them the campaign does not end with Tarrid.”

“My Prince.”

Vor’Tesai grounded the maul a second time. The eleven hundred boarders behind him shifted by a single degree of stance. The boarding bay carried the resonance up into the bones of Khuvius’s ankles through the deck plating.

He left the bay.

He returned to the war room with seven minutes remaining before the first round.

The first round of the orbital battle of Tarrid was fired by the heretic admiral Senec.

Senec opened with a long-range plasma strike from the Tessekai’s primary spinal mount. The strike crossed eighteen thousand kilometres of open vacuum in eleven seconds. It struck the forward shielding of the Aurex-Tertia at an angle of seven degrees off perpendicular and dispersed across the upper hull in a wash of yellow-white fire that lit the war room’s tactical display in a single bright pulse.

The Aurex-Tertia’s shielding held. The hull beneath the strike heated to twelve hundred degrees standard within the first second of contact and was cooling under the cruiser’s emergency thermal protocols within the third.

In the war room, Khuvius watched the strike land on the tactical display. He did not move from his position at the table. Mercurio, beside him, traced a small arc with his finger across the surface of the dark glass and the contour overlay shifted as the Third Wing’s eight other cruisers rotated their flanks toward the centre group of Senec’s line.

“Return fire?” Khuvius said.

“At the second mark,” Mercurio said. “Let him fire again. Let him think we are slow. He will fire three more long-range strikes in the next ninety seconds. Each strike will reveal the position of one of his secondary mounts. We return fire at the second mark with a coordinated salvo from all eight cruisers, targeted on the secondary mounts he has just exposed.”

“He will lose three secondary mounts.”

“He will lose three secondary mounts. He will then close the range. He will commit to the engagement at the equatorial plane. He will not be able to do otherwise without losing the morale of his own line.”

The second strike landed on the Aurex-Tertia’s lower hull at fourteen seconds past the first. The third struck the Hammer of the West, the Third Wing’s fourth cruiser, at twenty-two seconds. The fourth struck the Aurex-Tertia again at thirty-one seconds.

At the second mark (eighty-two seconds after the first strike) the Third Wing returned fire.

The coordinated salvo lit the equatorial plane of the Tarrid system in a single sustained pulse that lasted four seconds. Eight Trinitarian capital ships opened their primary spinal mounts and three of their secondary mounts each, every barrel targeted on the small windows of vulnerability that Senec’s three opening strikes had opened along the centre group’s outer hulls.

Three of Senec’s secondary mounts went dark in the same second.

The Tessekai itself took a glancing hit along the dorsal shielding. The hit did not penetrate. It scoured a long, dark furrow across the painted Trinitarian seal that ran the length of the Tessekai’s upper hull, a seal that Senec had ordered painted himself, three years earlier, in the colours of the Sixth Fleet and the colours of the von Care line interlaced.

In the war room of the Aurex-Tertia, Khuvius watched the seal scour. He did not smile.

“He will close now,” Mercurio said.

“He will close now.”

The Tessekai’s main engines lit. The contour overlay above the table shifted as the centre group of Senec’s line broke from station and burned toward the equatorial plane at half-acceleration.

Behind him, the Third Wing held station.

Forty kilometres above the equatorial plane, Nayra’s screen of light cruisers from the Eighth Wing took position around the orbital refinery. The screen comprised eleven hulls. Each hull was small, fast, lightly armoured but heavily gunned. Nayra herself was on the Khotai-Veth, the lead hull of the screen. She did not transmit. She held station and waited. She kept a single dried flower pressed flat against the inside of her wrist guard, from a plant that did not grow anywhere near the equatorial plane. No one aboard the Khotai-Veth had ever asked her about it, and she had never explained it to anyone who did not already know.

Below the equatorial plane, the lower group of Senec’s line (Aenar’s Twelfth Squadron at its rim) held its position for the first six minutes of the engagement. At the seventh minute, Saksai’s wing emerged from cold burn at the L5 trojan and began a slow approach to the lower group’s flank. At the ninth minute, Saksai transmitted on Aenar’s personal channel, a short signal, three words, in the Black Steppe dialect that Aenar’s grandmother had spoken at the kitchen fire of his birth-house.

Aenar broke off two minutes later.

His Twelfth Squadron (eleven hulls, three thousand crew) pulled out of formation and began a slow, controlled drift toward the open dark at the rim of the Tarrid system. Saksai’s wing did not pursue. Saksai had given Aenar his terms and Aenar had taken them.

Senec, on the bridge of the Tessekai, learned of Aenar’s defection at the eleventh minute. He ordered the lower group to fire on the Twelfth Squadron’s drives as it pulled out. The lower group did not comply. The lower group’s commander (a man named Korrin, who had served under Aenar for fourteen years and would not fire on his own former commander’s drives) gave the order to hold fire and was shot at his console by his own first officer six a moment later. By the time the first officer had assumed command of the lower group and reissued the order to fire on Aenar’s drives, the Twelfth Squadron was forty thousand kilometres past effective range.

The lower group’s first officer was a young man named Galleth, promoted to lieutenant only nine months earlier, who had not expected on the morning of the engagement to be commanding a battle group by the eleventh minute. He held the lower group in formation for another seven minutes before committing to the upper-flank pincer that Mercurio had predicted from the start.

By the time Galleth’s lower group committed, Vor’Dressal’s Second Horde was warming behind the third world. Its hulls were visible on the long-range optics of the Aurex-Tertia by the start of the second hour. They were not visible on the optics of the Tessekai because Senec had concentrated his sensor sweep on the upper and lower planes and had not, in the rising heat of the engagement, allowed his sensor officers to widen the sweep to the inner planets.

At the start of the third hour, Vor’Dressal’s Second Horde struck the rear of Senec’s centre.

The Second Horde was twenty-eight thousand Vah’Sumir on heavy boarding armour. They carried with them seventy-two heavy assault sleds and twelve hundred light boarding skiffs. They did not engage at distance. They closed to grappling range and they boarded.

The boarding of Senec’s centre took ninety-six minutes.

The Tessekai itself was boarded at the second hour and forty-one minutes of the engagement. Vor’Dressal led the boarding party personally. He took the bridge of the Tessekai in the third hour and twenty-second minute. He took Senec at the navigation console where the heretic admiral had retreated when the bridge was breached. He did not kill Senec. He grounded his percussion maul against the deck of the bridge and waited for the order from the Aurex-Tertia.

The order came nineteen minutes later. The order was: bring him.

Vor’Dressal brought Senec.

Senec was delivered to the war room of the Aurex-Tertia at the fourth hour and fourteenth minute of the engagement. He was unbound. He was unhelmeted. His face was the face of a man who had not slept in the prior thirty hours. The white plumage of his admiral’s mantle was crusted along the right shoulder with the dried blood of a junior officer who had stood beside him on the Tessekai’s bridge at the moment of the boarding and had been removed from him by Vor’Dressal’s percussion maul before Senec could intervene.

Senec stood three meters from the tactical table. Vor’Dressal stood at his right shoulder. Mercurio stood at the left. Khuvius stood at the table. The contour overlay above the table showed the dispersal of Senec’s surviving line, perhaps ninety hulls of the original four hundred and eleven, scattered across the Tarrid sector in attitudes of surrender or cold burn.

Khuvius did not look at Senec immediately. He finished tracing a line on the surface of the table with his finger. He tapped twice. The contour overlay updated. He raised his head.

“Imperator.”

“Crown Prince.”

“You touched my chest at my confirmation.”

“I did.”

“You held the contact for a beat.”

“I did.”

“You took the throne’s pledge. You took it from me, with my mother and my father and the Khanate’s Greater Council watching. You took it from me a second time in writing the following morning. You took it from me a third time, in person at the steppe of my birth-world, on the day I rode out for my first command at the age of nineteen.”

“I did.”

“And then you turned the Sixth Fleet.”

Senec did not answer. His eyes (pale blue, set close, weathered along the lower lid) held Khuvius’s. He did not look down.

“Tell me why,” Khuvius said.

Senec spoke. His voice was thin from disuse. He had not spoken in the prior nine hours, since the moment Vor’Dressal had grounded his maul on the bridge of the Tessekai.

“Because your father is dying,” Senec said. “Because you are not yet the Emperor. Because the Sixteenth Branch is moving on the throne and I will not bend my knee to a Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi. Because if I had moved with you, the central admiralty would have called me apostate within an hour and stripped my fleet from me before I could protect my own household. Because the only way to remain free of Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi’s hand was to break for the open dark with my fleet intact and with a declaration that placed me beyond the reach of any subsequent succession.”

“You broke for the open dark by turning your fleet against me.”

“I broke for the open dark by turning my fleet against the central admiralty. I did not, in my own counsel, turn against you.”

“I am the throne, Imperator. The central admiralty is the throne. There is no distinction.”

“There will be. Within the year. When your father dies. When Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi moves. When the central admiralty lines up behind Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi and you find yourself standing on a lower deck in the dark counting your own loyal hulls. There will be a distinction then. I have made it early. I have made it cleanly. I have made it where I could carry my fleet with me.”

“You have made it where you have lost your fleet.”

“I have made it where you have taken my fleet. There is a difference. Take it as a gift, my Prince. Take it as the gift of a man who saw, before any other, that the throne would not pass cleanly. I have made you stronger by losing to you here. I have given you four hundred hulls and three battle stations and the orbital refinery at Tarrid. I have given you a victory the Khanate will record. I have given you something to point to, when Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi moves, when your father dies. I have given you legitimacy.”

Khuvius looked at him for a beat.

He turned his head a fraction toward Mercurio.

“Take him to the lower deck. Hold him in the brig. Do not harm him. He is to face the Khanate in writing. He is not to face the Khanate in the body. Make sure his household survives. Make sure his line continues. He has given me a victory and I will not give the Khanate a corpse.”

“My Prince.”

Mercurio bent his head. He raised one hand. Two of the household guard, who had stood silently at the door of the war room throughout the exchange, crossed to Senec’s elbows.

Senec did not resist.

At the door, he stopped. He turned his head to look back over his shoulder at Khuvius.

“My Prince.”

“Imperator.”

“When Salman al Sa’ud von Hapsburgi moves.”

“He will move.”

“You will not have time to assemble a fleet of your own. You will need a fleet that is already assembled. You will need a fleet that has already broken from the central admiralty. You will need, in short, a fleet that is mine.”

“You are giving me your fleet.”

“I am giving you my fleet. I am also giving you the eleven other admirals of the rim, who will follow what I have done if you handle this correctly. I am giving you a beginning. Use it.”

He turned and walked through the door under the hands of the household guard. Vor’Dressal followed him.

Khuvius stood at the tactical table for a long count after the door closed. He did not look at Mercurio. He looked at the contour overlay above the table. The dispersal of Senec’s surviving line resolved, hull by hull, into the small amber dots that signified surrender or cold burn under his command.

“He is right,” Mercurio said quietly.

“He is right.”

“You will not have time to assemble a fleet.”

“I will not have time to assemble a fleet.”

“Then we begin assembling it now. Quietly. Under cover of the campaigns. One commander at a time.”

“Yes,” Khuvius said. “We begin now.”

He raised his palm from the table.

The orbital battle of Tarrid had ended at the fourth hour and the fourteenth minute. The first quiet movement of the Star Wolf assembly began at the fourth hour and the seventeenth.

There is one other record of that day that the compiler includes here, drawn not from the central admiralty’s battle logs but from the personal papers of Mercurio de Rothschild.

At the end of the fourth hour and the fourteenth minute, after Senec had been escorted from the war room and before the Aurex-Tertia had completed its post-engagement sweep, Khuvius left the bridge.

He went to the lower decks. He went to the medical bays, all eleven of them, in order, from the lowest to the highest. He spent three hours and eleven minutes doing this. He spoke to every man and woman who was conscious enough to be spoken to. He knew, according to Mercurio’s papers, more than half their names before he was told them.

In the ninth bay, he sat on a crate of replacement coolant canisters next to a Third Wing gunner named Vell Caerai who had taken a bulkhead fragment through his left shoulder and was going to lose the arm. Vell Caerai was seventeen years old. He was from the third district of the outer Belt, a mining family, and he had been in service for eight months.

Khuvius sat on the crate and talked with him for thirty-five minutes. About the third district. About the outer Belt. About what the mining of the Belt produced and where it went and whether Vell Caerai had ever seen the stuff that came up from the really deep seams, the stuff that ran blue when it was fresh-cut.

He had. His uncle worked the deep seams.

Khuvius had been to the third district once, in his twentieth year, for reasons that were in the central admiralty’s classified records and would not be made available to this compiler. He described the district by name: the eastern landing, the long transit corridor, the smell of the air handlers in the morning when the overnight shift came off.

Vell Caerai confirmed it. That was the smell. He had grown up with that smell.

When Khuvius rose from the crate, he set his palm on Vell Caerai’s right shoulder (the shoulder that was intact) and held the contact for a beat.

“Vell Caerai.”

“My Prince.”

“You held your post.”

“I did what the formation required, my Prince.”

“The formation required it. You did it. Both things are true and the second one is yours.”

He moved to the next bay.

Mercurio de Rothschild, who followed him through all eleven bays and wrote twelve pages about the experience afterward, notes that Khuvius had memorised more than the names and ranks of every crew member aboard the Aurex-Tertia but the distinguishing characteristics of their service records (the campaigns, the commendations, the families) and that he used this knowledge not as command information but as human information: the way you know something about a person because you considered it worth knowing.

He notes also that Khuvius ate nothing in those three hours and eleven minutes. He notes that by the time the last bay was visited, the Crown Prince’s hands were shaking slightly with fatigue, the fine tremor of a body that has been at battle readiness for nine hours and has not been fed. He notes that Khuvius returned to the command cabin, ate the cold barley that had been there since the morning, and was at the tactical table with the deployment review running within four minutes of finishing.

He notes, finally: “I had served under eleven commanders before him. I have not served under one since who understood that the people who hold your formations are not the formations. They are the people.”

This is the thing about Khuvius Auguste Pierre von Care that the battle records do not carry. It is the thing that made the Star Wolf assembly follow him into a succession crisis that by every structural analysis they should have observed neutrally. It is the thing that made ten rim admirals pledge their fleets to a man who could not tell them exactly what he was asking them for, only that he was asking them for it.

The battle logs carry his operational decisions. Mercurio’s papers carry the rest.
