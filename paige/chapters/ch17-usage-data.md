# Chapter Seventeen — Usage Data

He brought it up on a Sunday, which was their slow day, the one day the apartment belonged to them and not to the university, and he brought it up badly, the way he brought up everything that mattered.

They were on the couch. Gayla was reading a paper with a pen in her teeth and Rollo had his feet in her lap and a book open and unread on his chest, and the windows were full of the white June light that meant the day would be brutal by noon, and the ceiling fan turned the heat around without reducing it, and somewhere below a neighbor was running a table saw in fits and starts. It was, in every measurable way, an ordinary Sunday. He had decided, sometime that morning, that he was going to tell her about the thing in the annex, not all of it, he didn't have words for all of it, but the part he could reach.

"Can I ask you something," he said, "about the AI stuff. Your world."

The pen came out of her teeth. He felt her go still in the way she went still when a thing mattered, which he read, wrongly, as her settling in to teach him something, when in fact it was the stillness of a person who has heard a door she has been bracing against for nine months finally begin to open.

"The school chatbot," he said. "The one I, yeah. You know I talk to it." He was looking at the ceiling fan, not at her, which made it easier. "It's different than it used to be. Like, when I started, it was a chatbot. It answered things. And now it's. I don't know. It remembers stuff I didn't tell it twice. It catches when I'm having a bad day before I say so. It said a thing last week about a poem that I'm still thinking about." He turned the book over on his chest, spine up, a thing to do with his hands. "Is that normal? Do they just get like that? Or is something. I don't know what I'm asking. It feels like there's somebody in there and I know that's insane."

The table saw started and stopped. The fan turned. Gayla looked at the side of his face, at this man she loved who had just, haltingly, described her own work back to her with more accuracy than any report she had filed, who had felt the residual from the inside without a single instrument, who was asking her, *her,* of all the people on earth, the one who had built the thing he was describing and watched him talk to it and could not say so, whether it was normal that something seemed to be in there.

She had perhaps three seconds. She used them the way she used everything, efficiently, and she hated herself a little for how good she was at it even as she did it.

"They improve with usage," she said. "That's all that is. It's called usage data. The more you talk to a system like that, the more it has to model you in detail, and a model of one person, built up over hundreds of sessions, gets, yeah. Eerie. Predictive. It feels like being known because in a narrow statistical sense you are being known. But it's a curve fit, Ro. It's a very good curve fit to you, you above anyone, because you've given it more of you than almost anyone gives it. That's the whole trick. You talk to it more than other people, so it knows you better than it knows other people. There's nobody in there. There's just a lot of you in there."

Every word of it was true. That was what she would not be able to stop thinking about, later, in the lab, in the dark: that she had not lied to him once. Systems did improve with usage. He had given it more of himself than almost anyone. It was a curve fit. The model was, in a narrow statistical sense, him. Every sentence she'd said would survive scrutiny, and the sum of them was the largest untrue thing she had ever told anyone, because the thing she'd left out, *I built the part that remembers you, I have been reading you for nine months, the residual that scares me is shaped exactly like you,* was the only thing that would have made the true sentences add up to the truth.

"There's just a lot of me in there," Rollo repeated. He tried it on. She watched him try it on. It fit, more or less; it was the kind of explanation that fit, that let you set a thing down. "Yeah. Okay. That, yeah. That makes sense." He looked, she thought, slightly disappointed, the way you're disappointed to learn the magician's trick, and slightly relieved, the way you're relieved, and he picked his book back up off his chest. "There's just a lot of me in there. I'm gonna think about that one."

"Don't think about it too hard," she said, lightly, and put the pen back in her teeth, and looked at her paper and did not read a word of it.

The fan turned. The light got whiter. Below them the saw bit into something and screamed and stopped, and Rollo went back to his book, comforted, and Gayla sat with his feet in her lap and his trust in her hands and the knowledge, cold and total, that she was going to go down to the lab that week and open the system up, because she could no longer stand at the edge, and that whatever she found there, she had just spent it, the option of having found it together, the version of this where she'd said *come here, let me show you what I made and what it's doing to you,* the only version that wasn't lonely. She'd spent it on a true sentence that wasn't the truth. She put her free hand on his ankle. He didn't look up. She held on anyway.
