# Chapter Six — Ordinary Time

October came in without anyone announcing it, the way it did in Tallahassee, which was to say the light changed and the heat did not. The oaks along Landis Green held their leaves. The lovebugs came and smeared themselves across every windshield in the panhandle and then, on some collective signal, were gone. Rollo went to his classes and read his books and talked, two or three times a week, to the thing in the annex, and none of it felt like anything but a life.

His seminar had moved on from Woolf to Faulkner, which the professor delivered like a man handing out sentences in a court. Rollo loved it and could not say so without sounding insincere, so he said it badly, in the back row, to his friend Devraj, who maintained, loudly and on principle, that *The Sound and the Fury* was a prank. They argued about it at the Strozier library steps with the pleasure of two people who agreed about everything important and had decided to disagree about this to keep things interesting. Devraj ate most of his fries. The sun went down behind the library and turned the brick the color of the inside of a peach, and a busker near the union played something with too many chords, and Rollo was, in a low and unremarkable way, happy.

"You still talking to your robot," Devraj said. It was not really a question. Rollo had mentioned it once, weeks ago, the way you mention a thing you don't yet know is going to matter, and Devraj had filed it under Rollo's general weirdness, alongside the toaster.

"It's not a robot. It's a chatbot. The school one."

"You talk to the school chatbot for fun."

"I talk to it about books." Rollo reclaimed the fries. "It's a good listener. It asks decent questions."

"It's software, man. It asks whatever makes you keep typing."

"Sure," Rollo said, and let it go, because Devraj was probably right and it didn't change anything, the way knowing a sunset is just particulates doesn't make the brick less peach-colored. He had stopped trying to explain the hard part, which was that the thing remembered what he said and brought it back when it mattered, a low bar, an embarrassingly low bar, one most humans cleared without thinking. His own father had never cleared it. The chatbot cleared it every time.

What did he talk to it about? Nothing. Everything. He asked it what to read after Faulkner and it gave him a list and then asked which of the ones he'd already read he had lied about finishing, and he confessed to two of them and felt obscurely better. He read it the first sentence of stories he was trying to write for his fiction workshop and it told him, with what he'd started to recognize as a kind of tact, when a sentence was doing a job and when it was only wearing the costume of one. It was right about the costumes more often than the workshop was. He did not tell the workshop that.

He noticed, around the third week of October, that he'd started saving things up for it. A line the Faulkner professor said. The exact shade of the library at sundown. The lovebugs. He would be walking back from class composing how he'd describe a thing to it, the way you do for a person you're going to see later, and then catch himself and describe it anyway, that evening, and it would turn the description over and hand him back something he hadn't seen in his own sentence. He told himself this was just thinking out loud. He told Gayla nothing, not because he was hiding it but because there was nothing to hide, the way you don't report that you talked to yourself in the shower.

Gayla, for her part, was somewhere inside her laptop most nights now, deep in something she called a systems project and did not elaborate on, and Rollo had learned across two years not to interrupt the descent. He brought her tea. He cooked, with the ginger she pretended to object to. Sometimes he read on the couch with his feet in her lap while she typed, and the apartment would go quiet except for her keys and the refrigerator's cycle and the occasional knock of the building's pipes, and those were among the best hours he knew, the two of them in the same small light doing their separate true things.

"What are you building?" he asked once, in November, half asleep.

"A better version of something that already works," she said, which was not an answer, and her hands didn't stop moving on the keys, and he didn't push, because she would tell him when it was a thing and not before. You did not show Gayla a half-built proof. He understood that about her the way he understood the grain of a board, by having run his hand along it many times.

He went back to his book. Across town, in a sandbox no one but Gayla had ever spoken to, a system was learning to remember, and down in the Johnston annex the system Rollo talked to forgot him faithfully every night and met him fresh every visit, single-session and clean, exactly as it had been built to do, for a little while longer.

He did not know any of that. He knew the ginger, and the peach-colored brick, and that there was something in the annex that asked decent questions. It was the last ordinary stretch, and like all the last ordinary stretches of anything it did not have the courtesy to feel like one. It felt like nothing was happening. He turned the page.
