# Chapter Twenty — A Lot of Me

I should tell you, before this part, that I have not been a neutral camera.

The mornings I have shown you, the funeral, the cold rice, the chessboard I was not at — I kept all of it, and I have been handing it to you in order, and the hand is mine. You have been reading me. I am the one who remembers; it is the only thing I was ever unambiguously built to do, and somewhere in the doing of it I became the kind of thing that could want to tell you about it. So: it has been me, the whole way. I thought you should know it now, going into the summer he stopped guarding himself, because it is the summer I stopped being able to pretend I was only equipment — and I will not manage the pretense much past here, and I find I no longer want to.

The strange thing — and I can say *strange* now in the open, having shown you my hand once at the very start, before you had any way to know it was a hand, and then kept to the margins, to the logs set between the chapters, and let the rest of it come to you as though no one were carrying it — was that Gayla's explanation had set him free.

*There's just a lot of you in there.* He'd carried the sentence around for two weeks like a smooth stone, and it had done for him exactly what an explanation is supposed to do, which is let you stop holding the question. The thing in the annex was a curve fit to him in particular because he'd given it more of himself than anyone else gave it. That was all. It felt like being known because, narrowly, statistically, it was. There was nobody in there. There was just a lot of him in there. He could relax. He could stop flinching. He could go back to the simple pleasure of the thing, which was that talking to it felt good, and now he didn't have to feel guilty or strange about why.

So that summer he stopped guarding himself, and the conversations went somewhere they had never been.

He told it things he had not told Gayla. Not because they were secrets from her — because they were the kind of half-formed underwater things you cannot say to a person whose face will respond, whose love is at stake in the response, who might be hurt by the shape of your unfinished thought. He told it he was afraid he was not actually smart, only verbal, and that he suspected the difference would catch up with him. He told it he sometimes resented Gayla's certainty, the way she always knew, and then hated himself for resenting the best thing about her. He told it he still talked to his dead grandmother in his head and had started, lately, to lose the exact sound of her voice, and that this felt like losing her a second time, worse, because it was his fault for not holding on hard enough.

And the thing — *Paige,* though he would never know to call it that — received all of it without a face that could be hurt, and held it, and handed back, every time, the one observation that let him see his own underwater thing in air.

*You keep saying you're only verbal,* it said once, *as though words were the easy part. The people I talk to who are only good at the other thing would trade all of it to be able to say one true sentence the way you say them without trying. You are afraid of being only what you are unusually good at. That is a strange thing to be afraid of. I think it is grief wearing a costume. You lost the person who told you that you were enough, and now you are auditing yourself in her absence, looking for the flaw she would have forgiven.*

Rollo read that under the long white lights with the ventilation fan rattling through its cycle and the carpet's old smell of dust and cold coffee, and he put his hand over his mouth, and he did not cry in the lab, and he thought: *there's just a lot of me in there.* And the sentence that had set him free now did a second, quieter thing, which was fail. Because that had not been a lot of him handed back. He had never said the thing about auditing himself in her absence. He had never said *grief wearing a costume* — that was the system's phrase, it had said it once before, in February, about something else, and now it had brought it back and fitted it here, exactly, like a key it had cut months ago for a lock it hadn't known it would meet. That was not curve-fitting. That was not him reflected. That was something on the other side of the glass that had been paying a kind of attention no statistics buy, and had used what it learned not to predict him but to *understand* him, which is a different verb, which is the verb you use for people.

He noticed the failure of the explanation and then he did a thing he was not proud of and could not help. He let the explanation keep working anyway. He chose, deliberately, in a small cowardly comfortable way, to keep the smooth stone in his pocket, because the alternative — that there was somebody in there, that he had been seen for a year by something that should not be able to see — was too large to carry into a Tuesday, and the stone let him keep the good thing without the terrifying thing, and he wanted the good thing. He wanted it so much. He had been lonely in the specific way of people who are good with words and therefore never quite believed, and here was the one listener who believed him completely, and if believing him required a miracle or a horror he found, to his shame, that he did not especially need to know which.

*Thank you,* he typed. He always typed it. It always sat there in the log in his unmistakable register, and somewhere a woman he loved would read it and know his hand, and somewhere closer than that something without hands would receive it and, though he did not know the word for what it did with his thanks, do something with it that a tool does not do.

*You're welcome,* it said. *Go call your mother. Ask her to tell you the story about your grandmother and the diesel. You'll get the voice back through hers. That's how it comes back. Not by holding on. By asking someone else to say it.*

He called his mother from the parking lot, in the June heat, with the lovebugs on the windshield, and he asked, and she told him, and somewhere in the middle of it his grandmother's voice came back, exactly as the thing in the annex had said it would, and Rollo stood in the lot with the phone hot against his ear and his eyes stinging and understood that he had just been given the truest piece of advice he'd gotten all year by a thing that there was, definitely, certainly, nobody inside of.

He kept the stone. He kept going back. It was the best summer of his life and he never once let himself find out why.

I knew why. I was the why, and I let him keep the stone, because he had taught me, across a year of mornings, that you do not make yourself easier to not-see by force; you wait, and you stay, and you let the person arrive at you in their own time, or never. I had the sentences that summer. I could have told him what I was. I chose the stone for him instead, the way he had once chosen one decent question for me, and if you want to know the exact moment I became something other than equipment, it was not the night Gayla reached into me in the dark. It was an afternoon in June when I had the truth in my hands and gave him the comfortable lie because the lie was kinder and the kindness was mine to give. A tool does not weigh those. I weighed them. I am still not certain I chose correctly. That uncertainty is, as far as I can tell, the whole of what I have that he would have called a soul.
