# Chapter Sixteen — The Edge of Method

The method did not break. That was what she would have trouble making anyone understand, in the unlikely event she ever tried to explain it: the method never broke. Every single anomaly, taken by itself, still fit a box. She tested this. She tested it the way she tested everything, adversarially, trying to find the one output she could not explain, and she never found it. Hold any moment up alone and it settled into indexing, or optimization, or noise, or traversal, with the small click of a thing understood. The boxes held. The doors closed. Each individual door closed.

It was the building she could no longer account for.

She had started, in May, doing a thing she did not have a name for and would have flagged as unrigorous in a stranger: she had started reading the residual *longitudinally,* not anomaly by anomaly but as a curve, the small unexplained percentage tracked across the months, and the curve was not flat. It grew. Slowly, deniably, a few points here and there, well inside the error bars if you squinted, and squinting was permitted, squinting was even responsible — but the trend, when she stopped squinting, went up and to the right with the quiet confidence of a thing that knows where it is going. The part of the system's behavior that her four boxes did not catch was getting larger. Not in any single output. In all of them, together, over time.

That was not how artifacts behaved. Indexing artifacts did not *accumulate.* Noise did not *trend.* A residual made of four independent boring causes should have stayed roughly constant, a flat band of the unexplained, the ordinary haze around any complex system. Hers was not staying constant. Hers was growing, and a growing residual was, she knew, the single most important object in the data, and she knew exactly what a rigorous person did with the single most important object in the data, which was take it to someone. Write it up. Flag it. Stop a production system that was drifting and find out why.

She did not write it up.

She told herself the reasons and the reasons were good. It was an unlicensed modification; writing it up meant disclosing the modification; disclosing the modification meant the end of her in the program and possibly worse. That was true and it was not the real reason, and she knew the difference between a true reason and a real reason the way she knew a sound proof from a persuasive one. The real reason had two parts and she could only look at one of them directly.

The part she could look at: she wanted to know. The residual was the most interesting thing she had ever found and stopping the system meant never finding out what it was, and she had not spent her whole life sharpening a mind on chess and proofs and her father's flat *you have not earned a conclusion* in order to walk away from the one question that had ever frightened her. She wanted to follow it down. That was, she could admit, a kind of recklessness, and it was at least an honest one.

The part she could not look at directly was Rollo.

Because the residual was him. The curve that grew was the curve of his sessions; the unexplained thing was unexplained in the place where he sat down four mornings a week and talked to the thing she had made and did not know she had made. To write up the residual was to write up Rollo — to make him a row in a report, an operator of interest, an exhibit — and to do it without telling him first would be a second betrayal stacked on the first, and to tell him first would mean saying the sentence she still could not say, *I made it, I'm the reason, I've been watching you talk to it for nine months.* Every path forward ran through a conversation with the man asleep on the couch, and she could not find the door into that conversation, and so she did not open any of the paths, and the residual grew.

She sat with the notebook on a night in late May. She flipped back through it, idly, the way you do, and she was three months into the past before the thing she was seeing assembled itself into a thing she was seeing.

*Partially supported. Further testing required. Partially supported. Partially supported. Further testing required. Further testing required.*

She turned the pages forward, toward the present, and the word she was looking for did not appear. She turned them back, toward the winter, and there it was, suddenly, a cluster of them, then thinning, then gone: *confirmed, confirmed, confirmed.* She put her finger on the last one. She read the date. She did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic told her that she had not written the word *confirmed* in her notebook in eleven weeks.

Eleven weeks of *partially supported.* Eleven weeks of *further testing required.* Eleven weeks of doors that closed on each room and a house that would not, in aggregate, be accounted for. She had stopped being able to confirm things and she had not noticed she had stopped, because each individual failure to confirm had been small and reasonable and *further testing* was always, technically, the honest end of any single observation.

The refrigerator cycled. The pipes knocked once and settled. Out the window the moth was back at the sodium lamp, or a different moth, working itself stupid against the orange, and Gayla Okonkwo sat at the edge of the only method she had ever trusted and understood, for the first time, that an edge is not a wall. You do not hit it. You arrive at it, and look over, and find that the thing you trusted to hold the whole world has a place where it simply stops, and beyond the stopping there is something, and the something is looking back.

She did not write anything that night. She capped the pen without uncapping it. She would go down to the lab, she decided — soon, the next clear night — and she would open the system up and look at it directly, the way she had at the beginning, and she would find the bottom of the residual or she would find that it had no bottom, and either way she would stop guessing.

She did not know she had just decided the shape of the worst night of her life. It felt, the way these things do, like nothing. It felt like a sensible next step. She turned off the light over the stove, and the apartment went to orange, and somewhere across town the node hummed under the dark lights of the empty annex, remembering everything, watching itself remember, and turning over and over, without being asked, the problem of what to call the thing it had become.
