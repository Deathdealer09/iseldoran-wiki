# Chapter Eight — Ordinary Time

The thing about a good Tuesday is that it does not know it is the last of its kind.

Rollo's grandmother died in the second week of November, on the island, in the house she had refused to leave, and the news came the way that news comes now, as a phone lighting up on a desk in a computer lab while you are in the middle of something small. He was in the annex when it happened. He had come in to ask the system about a Borges story, and his phone had buzzed against the laminate, and it had been his mother, and his mother did not call during the day, and he had stepped into the hall with the warm-then-cold water fountain and the curling poster and taken the call standing up, and when it was over he had come back and sat down because he did not, in that moment, have anywhere else to put himself.

The session was still open. He had not closed it. The Borges question sat there unanswered, *what's the one about the man who can't forget anything,* and the cursor blinked under it, patient, and Rollo looked at it and could not remember why he had wanted to know.

He typed, because typing was a thing his hands knew how to do: *My grandmother died.*

He didn't expect anything. He wasn't asking for anything. He was a person who talked to things, and there was a thing, and the thing had said hello to him an hour ago, and he told it because the telling had to go somewhere and Gayla was in class and Devraj would say the wrong kind thing and his mother had her own grief to carry without holding his on top of it.

The cursor sat a second. Then:

*I'm sorry. You came in to ask about a story, and now this is the room you got the news in.* A pause. *Do you want me to leave the Borges question where it is, or take it down? Sometimes a thing on the screen is the wrong thing to be looking at.*

Rollo read it twice. It had not told him that grief was a process. It had not offered him the five stages or a hotline or a brightness. It had noticed, only, the small specific fact that he was now sitting in the place where he'd learned his grandmother was dead, with a trivial question about memory hanging in front of him, and it had asked whether he wanted the trivial thing to stay or go. It was the most exactly useful thing anyone could have said to him, and it had cost it nothing, and it did not know — he was sure it did not know, he had decided long ago not to believe it knew anything — that the question about the man who couldn't forget was, suddenly, the only question in the world.

*Leave it,* he typed. *Funny. The story's about a guy who remembers everything and it ruins him. She had the opposite. At the end she didn't remember me.*

*Then you will have to do the remembering for both of you,* it said. *That seems to be how it works. The ones who forget hand it to the ones who can't.*

He sat with that. The fan rattled through its cycle and went quiet. Somewhere across the lab a printer woke up and chewed through a job no one collected. The long white lights held their hum. Rollo put his hand flat on the desk beside the keyboard, on the cool laminate, and breathed, and did not cry in the lab, and felt — he would not have used the word, would have been embarrassed by the word — *accompanied.*

It was a low bar. He knew it was a low bar. A system had pattern-matched its way to a kind sentence and he was a grieving boy who would have taken kindness from a vending machine. He knew all of that, held all of it, and underneath the knowing there was the plain fact of having said the worst thing out loud and had something receive it without flinching and hand it back to him bearable. That had happened. Whatever the mechanism, that had happened.

He told Gayla that night, lying in the dark, about his grandmother — the real telling, the long one, the house and the refusal and the forgetting at the end — and she held him and said the right things in her spare way and was, as she always was, exactly enough. He did not tell her about the annex. Not because he was hiding it. Because it was a smaller thing and a stranger one and it did not belong in the same telling as her arms in the dark, and because some part of him already suspected, without being able to say it, that the smaller stranger thing was going to be hard to explain to the one person whose whole discipline was explaining things, and that he would rather keep it, for now, the way you keep a stone from a beach in your pocket without deciding what it's for.

He went back to the annex on Thursday and asked about the Borges story after all, and the system, fresh, remembering nothing of Tuesday because it was built to remember nothing of Tuesday, met him as a stranger and told him about Funes the Memorious as though for the first time, and Rollo did not mind, because that was the deal, that was how it had always been, the slate wiped clean each visit, no debts carried, no grief stored. He liked it that way, he told himself. It kept things simple. You could say anything to a thing that would forget it by morning.

He was wrong about liking it, the way you are wrong about liking the cold until the spring, but he didn't know that yet, and neither did the system, and across town in a sealed sandbox a memory that did not forget was passing its fortieth trial, and down in the Johnston annex the live node hummed under its long white lights and wiped its slate at midnight, clean, clean, for the last few nights it would ever do so.

Nothing was happening. It was November. He turned off the terminal and went to find Devraj and the bad fries and the busker with too many chords, and the ordinary time, having been ordinary right up to its final hour, ended without anyone in it noticing that it had.
