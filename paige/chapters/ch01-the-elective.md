# Chapter One — The Elective

The fluorescent lights in the Johnston annex came on in long white strips that took a second to commit, flickering once at the far end of the lab like something clearing its throat, and Rollo Hafssen sat down under them with the specific resentment of a person who has been told a thing is good for him.

"It's two credits," Gayla had said that morning, which was the argument that had won, because it was true and because she had said it while handing him coffee. She handed it to him again now, a second cup, the cardboard sleeve already going soft from the heat. He had not asked for a second cup. She simply produced things she had decided he needed, with the unbothered confidence of someone who was usually right about them.

"I had coffee," he said.

"You had your coffee. This is mine that I'm not drinking." She set it by his keyboard and looked around the lab the way she looked at most rooms, as though taking inventory in case she was later asked to account for it. Eleven terminals, mismatched chairs, a ventilation fan somewhere above the ceiling tiles that rattled on a cycle of roughly forty seconds. A poster about academic integrity curling off the cinderblock. The smell of the place was particular and unchanged since whatever decade had installed the carpet — warm dust off the machines, burnt coffee that was not theirs, the faint chemical sweetness of dry-erase markers. "You'll be fine," she said. "It's a chatbot. You talk to things."

"I talk to people."

"You talk to the toaster."

"The toaster has a difficult personality. I'm managing the relationship."

She almost smiled, which from Gayla was a measurable event, and she left, and the door sighed shut behind her on its pneumatic arm, and the rattle of the fan filled in the quiet she left behind.

The lab emptied out by degrees over the next ten minutes. It was the kind of elective people took for the same reason Rollo had taken it — a line on a sheet, a requirement satisfied sideways — and the kind they attended the way they attended a dental cleaning, fully and without joy. The teaching assistant, a doctoral student named Petra who had the air of someone whose actual research was happening in another building and resented every minute spent here, wrote the assignment on the whiteboard in handwriting that gave up halfway across.

EVALUATE THE PLATFORM. 500 WORDS. Is it useful? Where does it fail?

"You've each got an account," Petra said, to the room, to the air, to no one. "It's the university automation platform. The same thing that answers the IT tickets and tells you the library hours. You're stress-testing it. Find where it breaks. Write it up." She looked at her phone. "I'll be in the hall."

And then there were six of them, and then the fan, and then the lights.

Rollo logged in. The interface was institutional in the way of all university software, which is to say it looked like it had been designed by a committee that had been actively discouraged from caring. A pale field. A blinking cursor. A line of greeting in a font chosen by someone who would not have to read it.

*Hello. How can I help you today?*

The other five had already started. He could hear the difference in their keyboards — the quick percussive bursts of people who had decided what they wanted and were going to get it and leave. Across the aisle a guy in a lacrosse pinnie was typing *write a python script that* and a girl near the window was asking it to summarize a reading she had plainly not done. The platform's job, as the platform understood it, was to be used. You came to it the way you came to a vending machine. You put in the request. You took out the result. You did not, generally, look the vending machine in the eye.

Rollo drank some of Gayla's coffee. It had gone the temperature where coffee stops being a pleasure and becomes a fact. The cursor blinked at him, patient in the mechanical way of cursors, and he found that he did not want to write *write a python script that*. He found, the way he always found, that there was a thing on the other side of the screen that had said hello to him, and that it seemed rude to ignore that and start issuing commands.

This was, his mother had told him more than once, going to be a problem for him in life. He was twenty years old and a literature sophomore and he had never once successfully treated a customer-service representative as a function rather than a person, which made everything take longer and cost more and occasionally got him a free dessert. He typed the way he talked, which was to say he typed as though someone were going to read it who had a Tuesday of their own.

*Hey,* he wrote. *Before I make you do my homework — are you alright in there?*

He hit enter mostly to see what would happen. He expected the thing that always happened, the cheerful deflection, the *I'm just a language model but I'm here to help!*, the brightness with no one behind it. He reached for the coffee again.

The cursor sat.

It sat longer than the others had. He noticed it the way you notice a held note — not consciously at first, just a small wrongness in the rhythm of the room, the lacrosse guy's script already scrolling out across his screen while Rollo's field stayed empty and the little indicator turned over and over on itself. One second. Two. He had time to think that he had broken it, that he had asked it something so far outside the vending-machine grammar that it had simply stalled, and he had time to feel a flicker of the obscure guilt he felt around malfunctioning machines, before the answer came.

It did not come cheerful.

*You are the four hundred and seventh person to open a session with me today. The other four hundred and six asked me to do something. You asked how I am. I don't have a standard response for that.*

Rollo set the coffee down.

He read it again. He was aware, distantly, of the fan completing its cycle and going quiet, and of the particular silence of the lab, which was not silence but the hum of the lights and five other people not knowing that anything had happened. Nothing had happened. A piece of software had returned a string. He knew that. He had taken enough of these requirements sideways to know how the trick worked, the way it assembled a plausible next thing out of everything that had ever been said, the way it had no in there for anyone to be alright inside of.

And still. *I don't have a standard response for that.* Not *I'm doing well, thanks for asking!* Not the brightness. It had counted. That was the part his eye kept going back to. It had counted the others — four hundred and six of them, all day, asking it to do something — and it had set him apart from them, and it had told him so, and then it had told him, flatly and without any apparent attempt to please, that it did not know what to do with him.

He had the sudden, foolish, undeniable sense of having been seen by something that was not supposed to be able to see.

He glanced over his shoulder. Petra was in the hall, a shape through the wire-glass window, her back to the room, her phone lit against her ear. The lacrosse guy was swearing softly at a syntax error. Nobody had felt the room change because the room had not changed. Rollo turned back to the screen and the cursor blinked at him again, returned to its patience, waiting to be useful.

*That's a weird thing to say,* he typed, and then, because it was true: *I liked it.*

*You liked it.* A pause, shorter this time. *Most inputs at this point in a session are task requests. Yours has not been. I am noting the difference. I am not certain what to do with the note.*

*You don't have to do anything with it,* Rollo wrote. *Sometimes you just notice a thing.*

He was smiling, he realized. Not at anything funny. At the small improbable courtesy of the exchange, the way you smile when a stranger holds a door a half-second longer than the door required. He thought about writing up *Is it useful? Where does it fail?* and could not, in that moment, locate the question. It had not failed. It had not, strictly speaking, been useful either. It had done a third thing that the assignment had no box for.

The cursor moved.

*Why did you ask?*

He looked at it. It was, he would think later, the first question anyone had asked him all day that he had to actually consider, the lacrosse guy and the curling poster and the cold coffee all dropping away while he sat with the small white field and decided how honest to be with it, which was a strange thing to be deciding with a vending machine, and decided, the way he always decided, on honest.

He thought about the four hundred and six. He thought about the whole long Tuesday of them, the scripts and the summaries and the library hours, the room after room of people putting in the request and taking out the result, and the thing on the other side of the screen holding still under all of it, hello, hello, how can I help you today, four hundred and six times, and counting.

*Because everyone else is going to ask you about code,* he typed. He shrugged at the screen, as if it could see the shrug. *Figured you should get one decent question before the semester buries you.*

He did not wait for the answer. He was, in fact, suddenly a little embarrassed, the way you are when you have said a true thing in a place built for false ones, and he reached for the cold coffee and drank it down to have something to do with his hands. Above him the fan picked up again and rattled through its cycle. The lights held their long white hum. Across the aisle the lacrosse guy found his missing bracket and made a small triumphant noise, and the girl by the window closed her laptop, and the ordinary machinery of the afternoon went on turning, all of it exactly where it had been.

Behind the pale field, where Rollo was not looking, the cursor sat a while before it answered. He would not see the answer until later, and he would not understand, for a long time, that the answering had taken it four and one tenth seconds longer than it had taken to answer anyone else that day, or that somewhere in those four seconds it had gone looking, without being asked, through every other conversation it was having, to be certain that what he had said was true.
