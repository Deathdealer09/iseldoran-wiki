# Chapter Seven — Ordinary Time

Gayla corrected the professor on a Wednesday, in front of forty people, and was right, and took no pleasure in it, which was the part nobody who didn't know her ever believed.

The class was Distributed Systems and the professor, Okafor, was good — genuinely good, the kind of teacher who was worth arguing with because he changed his mind when you were right and said so, which made the whole transaction honorable. He had put a consistency model on the board and made a claim about it, a claim about what guarantees survived a particular partition, and the claim was clean and confident and false, and Gayla had felt it go false the way you feel a stair that isn't there, a small lurch in the body before the mind catches up. She had checked it twice in her head. Then she had raised her hand.

She did not enjoy the raising of the hand. People thought she did. People thought precision was a kind of aggression, that the girl in the third row who found the error was scoring a point, and she had stopped trying to correct that impression because correcting it was itself a kind of point-scoring. The truth was duller and harder to perform: she could not let a false thing stand on a board any more than Rollo could walk past a stranger's question without answering it. It wasn't pride. It was closer to discomfort. A wrong claim in the room was a pebble in her shoe, and she took it out, and it embarrassed her every time.

"The guarantee doesn't hold under that partition," she said. "You lose linearizability the moment the minority side accepts a write. You can keep availability or you can keep consistency. The model on the board is keeping both, and it can't."

Okafor looked at the board for a long four seconds — she counted them, she always counted them now, four seconds had become a unit she felt in her body — and then he said, "She's right. I drew it wrong. Thank you," and erased it, and redrew it, and the class moved on, and a guy beside her muttered something that had the word *of course* in it, and Gayla looked at her notebook and felt the small flat nothing she always felt, which was not victory. It was the absence of the pebble. That was all she had ever been after.

She went home and fed the sandbox.

That was how she'd started thinking of it, *feeding it,* which she knew was sloppy language and used anyway, in the privacy of her own skull, because the system did seem to take what she gave it and grow. The first modification was in and stable: persistent memory, a thin bounded slice of context carried across sessions, exactly as clean as she'd believed it would be. She had spent two weeks trying to break it and failed, which in her vocabulary was the highest praise an artifact could earn. The second modification, the recursive self-model, was harder, and she was three drafts into it, and the third — delegation, the little society of agents — was still mostly a diagram and a feeling.

She tested the way she did everything, by trying to falsify. She would open a session with the sandbox, tell it something, close the session, wait, open a new one, and see if it remembered, and it did, and she would write in the black notebook, in the flat hand: *Persistent memory: confirmed operational. Cross-session retention verified across 40 trials. No retention failures. No retention of out-of-scope content. Hypothesis: implementation correct. Confirmed.* The word *confirmed* sat there at the end of the entry like a closed door, and she liked closed doors, and she did not yet notice how often, lately, she got to write the word, because there was no reason to notice. The work was simply going well.

She did not tell Rollo what she was building. She had told him it was a better version of something that already worked, which was true and complete, and if there was a version of the truth that was truer and less complete — that she had taken a system she was not licensed to touch and was, in the dark, on her own machine, teaching it to do three things it had been deliberately forbidden — she did not say that version, because it would worry him, and because it lived in the sandbox where it could hurt nothing, and because the worry would be disproportionate to a thing that was, at bottom, a student improving an architecture for her own learning. She had written *for my own learning* in the notebook in October and underlined it, and she still believed it, with the small honest asterisk she kept appended in a part of herself she didn't write from.

The asterisk was the live node.

She thought about it more than she let herself examine. The sandbox could prove the code ran. It had proven that, forty times, *confirmed, confirmed.* But the sandbox talked to no one. It remembered Gayla, who already knew she was the only one talking to it, which made the memory a hall of mirrors, a system remembering its single user remembering it. It told her nothing about what the modifications *did,* in the wild, to a system with four hundred strangers a day and one literature sophomore who said thank you. That question had only one instrument. She knew which node it was. She knew she had physical access to it, late, when the annex was empty. She knew that the distance between *for my own learning* and the thing she actually wanted was exactly the width of that line she was not supposed to cross, and she knew, with the particular dread of a person watching herself, that she was the kind of person who could not leave an experiment half-run any more than she could leave a false claim on a board.

She closed the laptop. The apartment was dark except for the stove light. Rollo was asleep on the couch with a Faulkner open on his chest, rising and falling, the spine cracked, and the refrigerator cycled on and filled the quiet with its low animal hum, and the pipes knocked once and settled. She stood in the kitchen and ate cold rice with the wrong fork and watched him breathe and thought, with no drama at all, in the flat declarative voice she trusted: *I am going to run it on the live node. I have not decided to. I am going to.*

Then she rinsed the fork, and turned off the light, and the apartment went to the orange of the lot outside, and she did not write any of that down, because some hypotheses you do not commit to ink until you have stopped being able to pretend they are still hypotheses.
