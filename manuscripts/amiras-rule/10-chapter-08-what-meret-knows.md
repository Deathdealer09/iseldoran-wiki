# Chapter 8 — What Meret Knows

Meret had asked for the room at the second hour, before the compound came to itself, and Asha had granted it the way she granted most things Meret asked for, without question and with the understanding that Meret did not ask for much. The room sat at the end of the assessment corridor, the small one, built so that a voice raised to its ordinary pitch did not reach the wall and a voice dropped below that reached nothing at all. The walls were doubled and the door hung on oiled hinges that gave back no sound, and there was a table and two chairs and nothing on the walls, none of the columns of initials that hung in the larger rooms, because the room had been built for the kind of conversation that left no record until an officer decided to make one. Asha came down to it cold from her own quarters, the corridor still dark, the duty lamps burning low at their stations, and she let herself in and sat in the chair that faced the door, as she always did, and waited.

The compound at this hour had a smell it lost by the fourth, before the kitchens woke and the day's oil went onto the hinges. It was the smell of stone that had cooled all night and the faint mineral edge of the wash-water the night crew ran through the lower corridors, and over it, very thin, the cold ash of yesterday's lamps not yet relit. Asha had spent more mornings than she could count in this stillness before the building filled, and she knew that a person who asked for a room at the second hour had been awake in it a long time before the asking.

Meret came in at the second hour exactly. She closed the door with the flat of her hand, felt it seat, and sat in the chair across the table without being told to, square to it, her forearms coming to rest on the worn wood. She had a folder with her. She set it down and kept her hands on it, folded, the way she kept her hands on things, and did not open it.

"You have had this a while," Asha said. It was not a question. Meret did not ask for the small room to bring something a day old.

"Six weeks," Meret said.

Asha let that sit between them. Six weeks was a long time for Meret to carry a thing without sending it up the chain, and the length of it was the first piece of information in the room, before the folder, before a word of what was in it. Asha did not ask why Meret had waited. She would hear the why in the shape of what came next, or she would not, and either way the asking would have been an officer wanting to be told she was owed an explanation. Meret had her reasons and they had a discipline to them, and that was enough to begin from.

"Then tell me what it is," Asha said, "and tell me how it came to you, in that order."

Meret nodded, once, and began the way she did everything, from the bottom, with the parts that had dates on them.

"I keep the periphery summaries that Silva sends up," she said. "Not her field notes; the clean summaries, after she has cut them down for the file. I log them in and cross them against the transfer registers from the outer waystations, the arrivals and departures the stations post up the standard channel. That is the routine work, the reconciling. I have done it three years and I know its grain." She turned the folder a quarter on the table, not opening it, only squaring it nearer her own hands. "Six weeks ago I logged a summary out of a waystation on the Sennar feeder, and there was a face in it Silva had noted in passing, a man at a document counter, no flag on him, just a description she put down because she puts everything down. A courier's satchel. The loose look of a man waiting for nothing in a place where everyone waits for something. She moved on. It was not her matter; she was working a different line that day."

"Go on."

"I had seen the description before." Meret said it flat, without weight, the way she would have read a register entry aloud. "Not the man. The description. Three weeks earlier, in a summary out of a different waystation, a different jurisdiction, on a feeder that does not connect to the Sennar line and has no traffic that crosses it. Same satchel. Same loose attention. A man at a transfer point with no onward booking the station could post, present long enough to be noted and gone before the next register cycle closed." She paused, and Asha understood the pause was Meret deciding how much of her method to put into the room and choosing to put all of it. "I do not trust a description twice. A satchel is a satchel and a man waiting is half the people in a waystation. So I did not flag it. I laid the two summaries beside each other and looked for the thing that would tell me it was the same man or the thing that would tell me it was two, and there was not enough on the page to do either. So I left it open and kept logging, and watched the registers as they came up, and waited to see whether the description came a third time."

"And it came a third time," Asha said.

"It came four more times in six weeks." Meret opened the folder now. She lifted the cover and turned it back and laid her hand flat on the top sheet, one she had ruled herself, columns down it, dates and station codes and a short line of description against each, the same phrases recurring down the column because she had used the same words each time on purpose, so that the eye could catch the repetition. "Six instances, six weeks. Five waystations. Every one a transfer point, where a thing moves from one line to another and the register has to be re-cut by hand. Every one on a feeder that touches a jurisdiction where the field work has flagged something in the appointment records this season. I did not put that last part together myself. I keep the summaries, not the appointment files. But I keep the jurisdiction codes, and the codes on my six are the ones I have heard senior officers ask the analysis hall about in the last two months, and I am not deaf in a corridor."

Asha drew the folder a hand's width across the table and read the ruled sheet without touching it, top to bottom, the way she read everything the first time, for the shape and not the argument. Six rows. Six dates, the earliest seven weeks back, the most recent eleven days. Five station codes, one of them appearing twice. A column of descriptions in Meret's square hand, the same four or five phrases worked across all six rows so that they stacked when you ran your eye down them. It was the cleanest kind of work the Quarter did, the kind no examination on a page would teach a person to do, because the doing of it was patience and the patience could not be coached.

"You are reconciling registers," Asha said, reading still, "and a face you have no name for keeps standing up off the page in the places the appointment work is hot. And you have laid it out so that if you are wrong, the wrongness shows as plainly as the rightness."

"If I am wrong it is because six men in six waystations carried the same satchel and waited the same way and booked nothing the station could post," Meret said. "I do not think that is what happened. But I have built it so that it could be, because the day I build it so that it cannot be is the day I have stopped reconciling and started to decide."

Asha looked up at her then. The room was small enough that there was nowhere to look but at the other or at the table, the closeness the point of it, two people set near enough that nothing passed between them the room would let escape. Meret met the look and held it, plain and unhurried, and Asha saw in it none of the thing she watched for in the assessment chair, none of the wanting to be right, only a woman who had carried a finding carefully for six weeks and decided this morning she had carried it as far as one person should.

"This is why you waited," Asha said.

"I waited because three instances is a coincidence I have seen before and been wrong about," Meret said. "Four would have been a pattern, but two of the four I could not stand behind; the descriptions were thin and I would not pad them. I waited until I had six I could stand behind. I have six." She closed the folder over her own sheet, gently, her hand flat on the cover. "I did not wait because I was afraid to be wrong in front of you, Officer. I waited because if I bring you a thing before it will hold, and you act on it, and it does not hold, I have spent the Quarter's reach on my unease, and there is an operation in the field this season the unease could foul. I do not know what it is. I know there is one, because the analysis hall has not slept and a senior was woken for a roster eleven days ago and Kaviel has been in her office past the tenth hour more nights than not. I keep the night log. I see who burns the lamp. I did not need to be told there was something running to know that a wrong move from me could put a foot through it."

Asha sat with that. It was the longest she had heard Meret speak in a year, and Meret had spent it telling her, in operational language and no other, a thing a lesser officer would have dressed as conscience and a worse one as apology. There was no apology in it, only a woman accounting for a decision the way she would account for a register, and Asha respected the accounting more than she would have respected any speech about duty. She did not say so. Meret had not brought the finding to be told she had done well with it.

"You were right to wait," Asha said instead, the same thing said as a finding rather than a comfort. "And you are right to bring it now. Six holds. Three would not have. You know the difference and you held the line at it. That is the work."

Meret inclined her head a degree and said nothing, which was the correct amount to say.

Asha opened the folder again and read it the second way, slower, against what she carried that Meret did not. Meret kept the summaries and the registers and not the appointment files, and so had brought a pattern in transport and could not see the pattern in the records it lay against. Asha sat high enough in the analysis chain to have heard the jurisdiction codes spoken in the hall and to know which had gone hot and why, and she laid Meret's six station codes against that knowing now, row by row, and watched them fall into the hot jurisdictions one after another with the regularity of a thing that was not chance.

"Tell me about the man," Asha said. "Not the satchel. You have given me the satchel. What does this face do, in your reading, that puts him in five waystations in six weeks at the transfer points and nowhere the register can post."

"He carries," Meret said. "He does not travel. A traveler books an onward leg and the station posts it; this man has no onward leg the station can show, which means he is handing off. A transfer point is where a thing leaves one line and joins another and the register is re-cut by hand, and a thing handed across at the moment the register is open can be moved without a clean line from where it started to where it went. He is at the seam every time, and the seam is where the record can be made to lose the thread." She said it without heat, a register entry still. "I think he moves paper, because a courier's satchel carries paper and the jurisdictions are the appointment jurisdictions, and a record-trouble that runs across jurisdictions needs a hand to carry the false thing from where it is made to where it is filed, by a route that does not post. I cannot prove the satchel holds appointment papers; I have never seen inside it. I am telling you the shape my reading takes and where it stops being something I can stand behind."

It was the same discipline Kaviel kept, the line drawn hard between what the page held and what the mind reached past it for, the thing the Quarter selected for above all the rest, the thing she had watched a competent soldier fail to have in the assessment chair weeks back. She set the thought aside.

"You have a face and a method and six instances and no name," Asha said.

"I have no name," Meret agreed. "Silva did not have one. The registers do not carry one for him, because a man who hands off at the seam does not give the counter a name it logs. I have a description and a route and a rhythm. I do not have a man I could put a hand on."

Asha read the rightmost column again, the one Meret had not spoken to, a narrow column at the edge where she had set down, against four of the six rows, a cluster of marks that were not description and not date. Asha had taken them for register codes on the first reading. They were not. They were initials, partial ones, a single letter standing alone in three of the rows and a letter and a half-formed second in the fourth, set down small and tight against the rule.

"What are these," Asha said, and put her finger to the column without touching the page.

"That is the thing I am least sure of," Meret said, "and the reason I almost brought you only the pattern." She did not reach for it; she let Asha hold it. "Three of the waystations keep a hand-signed transfer chit when a thing is moved across by a person rather than down a posted line. It is an old practice, kept in the outer stations and let go in the central ones, a man putting his mark to say the thing passed through his hand. It carries no legal weight. No one reconciles it; it sits in a box at the station until the box is full and then it is burned. Silva does not collect it because it tells you nothing on its own. But three of my six stations keep it, and on the days my face was in those three, a chit was signed across the seam, and the mark, where I could read it, began with the same letter."

Asha was very still.

"Which letter," she said.

"B," Meret said.

The room held it. Asha did not move and did not let her face move, the not-moving a discipline she had kept for forty years and kept now, because a single letter set down in a small room at the second hour was either the first true edge of a thing the Quarter had been reading the shape of for two months or a coincidence of a courtesy practice in three outer stations, and her face would not decide which before her reading had.

"Where you could read it," she said. "Not all three."

"Two I read plainly. The third the chit was smudged and I would not swear past the letter, which is why the half-mark is in the fourth row and not a full one. The fourth station did not keep chits; that row has no mark and I left it empty rather than carry the letter across to it, because the letter belongs to the chits and not to the man, until I have a reason to join them." Meret's hands had come back to folded on the closed folder. "It is the first letter of a name signed on a courtesy chit by a man who handed a thing across a seam, on the days my face was in the station, at three stations of the five. It is not the man's name. It could be the man. It could be a station hand who signed for a transfer that had nothing to do with him. The chit says nothing past the one mark. I laid it at the edge of the sheet on purpose, so that it sits beside the pattern and not inside it. If you take the letter into the pattern you are deciding the courier's name begins with it, and I have not earned that, and I will not put my hand to it until it is earned."

Asha read the column one more time, the lone B set tight against the rule in three rows, the empty fourth where a lesser hand would have filled the letter in to make the page look finished. She thought of a roster pulled from below the active line eleven days back and a reconciliation review stood up under an authority no clerk would name, and of the appointment work that ran across exactly the jurisdictions whose codes were stacked in front of her in Meret's square hand. She held enough of their shape to know that Meret had brought her, without being able to see the far side of it herself, the first thread in two months that ran from the records into a hand, a hand that moved at the seams where the record lost its thread, in the jurisdictions where the false appointments surfaced, leaving a single letter on a courtesy chit no one was meant to read and that Meret had read because Meret read everything.

It was the most incomplete thing she had been handed in weeks, a letter and a satchel and a rhythm, and the incompleteness was the part she would have to carry now, because she sat where the deciding was. Act on a letter as though it were a name and the operation already running in the field, the one whose lamp Meret saw burning past the tenth hour, could turn on the wrong man, or on the right man too soon, before the thing behind him had shown its reach. Wait for the name and the man kept handing off at the seams while they waited, and the longer he handed off the more the record lost. Asha had sat with that choice a hundred times and it did not get lighter with the hundredth.

This was not a day to move on the man. A courier handing off at the seams was a node and not a head, the kind of figure a process used and replaced, and to reach for him now was to spend the surprise on the part of the machine that mattered least and warn the part that mattered most. But the letter was a true edge, and a true edge logged did not have to be found again, and the finding of it had cost Meret six weeks she had spent well.

"You will keep doing exactly what you have been doing," Asha said. "Log the summaries, reconcile the registers, change nothing in how you do it, because the value of what you brought me is that it came up out of the routine and not out of a man looking for it. If you start looking for him, the looking will change the shape of what you see, and I would lose the one reading in this building that is not bent toward an answer. You watch. You log. The next time the face comes up, you note the station and the date and whether a chit was kept, and you bring it to me here, to no one else, and you put it in no standing file. A thread this thin breaks if it is handled by more hands than need to hold it, and right now the hands that need to hold it are yours and mine."

"Understood," Meret said.

"The sheet stays with me."

"I made it to give to you."

Asha drew it the last of the way across the table and squared it to the folder's edge. "Kaviel is working a shape of this from the records side. She does not have what you have; you do not have what she has; neither of you is to be told what the other holds until I decide the two halves are worth joining, and that decision is mine and it is not made this morning." She said the name once and let it lie, because Meret kept the night log and knew whose lamp burned and would have her own shape forming of why Asha named her, and she would keep that shape to herself the way she kept everything. "You have not spoken Kaviel's name in connection with any of this. You will not."

"I have not," Meret said. "I will not."

Asha rose and went to the low cabinet against the doubled wall, unmarked, locked with a key she carried and not one the building held. She took out a ledger that was hers and not the Quarter's, narrow, bound in plain dark board, the log a senior officer kept of threads too thin to file and too true to lose, and carried it back and uncapped her pen. Meret had risen when Asha rose, and stood at the far side of the small table with her closed folder under her hand, waiting to be released, watching without watching the way she did.

The room had warmed a half-degree with the two of them in it, the ash smell of the dead lamps thinning, and somewhere far down through the doubled walls the first sound of the kitchens reached them as a vibration more than a noise, the day beginning to assemble itself two floors below while the small room held its silence a moment longer. Asha turned to a clean page near the back of the ledger, past the older threads in her own cramped hand, and set the date at the head of it, and the station codes from Meret's sheet beneath, the rhythm, the absence of an onward booking, the courtesy chit and what it did and did not prove. She wrote it short, because a long entry was an officer arguing with herself on the page and she was not arguing; she had decided. At the foot of the entry, in the narrow space she left for the thing each thread came down to, she wrote a single letter, and looked at it a moment in her own hand, the lone B sitting at the bottom of the page the way it had sat at the edge of Meret's sheet, an edge and not a name, true and not yet enough.

She capped the pen. She closed the ledger over the letter and set her hand flat on the cover for the length of a breath, and then she stood.

"We work from what we have," she said.
