# Chapter 21 — The Faction Moves

The challenge entered the administrative registry of the Bureau of Establishments at the seventh hour of the morning, and it entered well.

Kaviel had the first word of it within the half-hour, which was the whole of the margin she had built for herself and most of what would save the day. She was in her office under the north wall when the channel tray took the slip, a single line in the hand of the registry liaison the Quarter kept inside the Bureau, a clerk named Otieno who had passed paper to the Lionesses for nine years and had never once been told why and had never once asked. The line said that a petition of invalidation had been lodged against the findings of an unnamed inquiry touching the carry-forward record, on grounds of procedural irregularity in the gathering, and that it had been entered under a priority docket that required the registrar of record to rule on it within the working day. Kaviel read it once. She set it flat on the desk and put her two hands on the edge of the wood and read it again, and then she did the arithmetic, which she had done many times in the abstract over the past three days and now did with the figures real and the clock running, and the arithmetic told her she had until the registrar closed the docket at the end of the working day to keep the Empress's notation from being entered against a finding that no longer formally existed.

She had cracked the window an inch when she came in, as she did every morning, and the air off the courtyard came in cold and carried the wet-stone smell of the gutters and, under it, the burnt-grain warmth rising from the kitchens two floors down. She did not notice either. She was reading the slip a third time, not for what it said, which she had taken whole at the first reading, but for the shape of the office that had sent it, the choice of docket, the choice of hour, the choice to file against findings without naming the inquiry that had made them, which was the move of people who knew exactly what the inquiry was and meant the court to be able to say, afterward, that they had not.

They had moved before the notation. That was the thing she sat with for the length of one slow breath. Amira had written the emergency authority in her own hand the night before and given it to Kaviel to carry and enter, and the carrying and the entering were a process with its own hours and its own desks, and the faction had reached into that gap and filed a thing that, if the registrar ruled it sound, would strike the findings out of the record as improperly gathered before the authority that protected them could attach. A finding struck from the record was a finding the emergency prerogative had nothing to protect. The Empress would be holding a warrant over an empty page.

Kaviel had known the gap was there. She had told Asha a week ago where the danger lay. The gathering was not the dangerous hour, and the judging was not the dangerous hour. The danger was the narrow passage between them, when the work was complete and the authority not yet entered, and that a careful opponent would file in that passage if he understood it, and that this one understood everything. She had not been able to close the gap. The notation could not be written before the findings were complete and the findings had been complete only the previous night. So she had done the other thing. She had built, against this morning, a set of replies that did not yet have a challenge to answer, and she had put her people where the replies would have to be made, and she had told them what they were waiting for without being able to tell them when it would come.

It had come at the seventh hour. She took the channel and wrote three lines, one to Asha, one to Silva, one to Meret, each saying the same thing in the form each woman would act on fastest, and she put them in the tray and rang the floor runner, and the runner came and took them and went, and the morning began.

The procedural irregularity the petition named was authorization. Kaviel had it from Otieno's second slip by the eighth hour, and it was the move she had thought most likely, because it was the strongest. The petition did not argue that the findings were wrong. To argue that the findings were wrong was to engage the findings, and to engage them was to give them more life, and the people who had filed this did not want the findings to have any life at all. So the petition argued instead that the inquiry had reached, somewhere in its gathering, past the line its mandate drew, into a record or a category it had no charter to open, and that everything the inquiry had touched after that reach was gathered under a void authority and could not stand. It was clean. It was the argument Asha had refused to give them six weeks ago, on her knees on the floor of the small room with the code table open under her hand, when she had hit the category she could not investigate and had logged the wall and not crossed it. The faction did not know she had refused. The faction had filed the petition on the assumption that an inquiry which had got this far must have crossed the line, because the faction's own people would have crossed it, and the whole of the petition rested on a reach the Lionesses had been disciplined enough not to make.

That was the first thing Kaviel needed proven, and it was Asha's.

---

Asha had the request before she had finished the morning intake, and she set the intake aside, which she did for almost nothing, and went down to the room where the work had lived.

The room was as she had left it at the sixth hour two nights before, the disbursement registers banded and ranked along the wall, the appointment files squared on the table, the floor clear now because the floor-work was done. She did not need the registers this morning. She needed the log. She had logged every finding of the inquiry in the cramped exact hand that had filled a thousand intake ledgers, and she had logged beside each finding the authority under which it had been gathered, the channel, the mandate clause, the desk it had crossed, because she had spent forty years teaching that a finding without its provenance was a rumor and that the day would come when the provenance was the only thing that mattered. The day had come. She drew the log to the table edge and opened it to the first entry and began to read it the way the registrar of record would read it if the registrar were honest and the way the petition's authors would read it if they were given the chance, which was to say against itself, hunting for the single place where the gathering had reached past the line.

The light in the room was the flat artificial light of a space with no window, a thing she had stopped noticing decades ago and noticed now because the day outside was running and she could not see it run. She read with the cold of the banded registers still banked in the corners and the dry mineral smell of the old paper close around her, and she read for an hour without rising, entry by entry, the carry-forward trace and the disbursement match and the message route and the protected witness, each finding set against the authority that had reached it, and at the head of every entry that touched the category above the line she found the same notation in her own hand, set down weeks ago, that the reach had stopped at the wall, that the issuing record had not been pulled, that the request to pull it had not been written, that the finding past the wall rested on inference from facts gathered below it and not on any record gathered above.

She had built the case so that it did not need the thing it could not reach, because a case that needed the crossing was a case that fell the day someone asked whether the crossing had been authorized. Someone had asked. The asking was in the registry under a priority docket with a clock on it. And the answer Asha had spent six weeks making sure she could give was that the line had held, that every finding stood on ground the mandate covered, that the inquiry had reached the floor above the working bureaus by inference and never by reach, and that there was no void authority in the gathering because there was no gathering above the line to be void.

She wrote it out. She wrote it the way she wrote everything, short, the provenance of each finding stated and the mandate clause cited and the wall marked plainly at every place the inquiry had stopped at it, and she wrote at the head of the thing the one sentence that was the whole of the reply, that the inquiry's findings touching the protected category were findings about that category and not findings gathered from within it, and that the distinction was the distinction between reading a locked door and going through it, and that the inquiry had read the door. She did not argue. She stated. She trusted the registrar of record to read the argument out of the statement, and she trusted Kaviel to put the statement where the registrar would have to read it before the docket closed, and she folded the reply and set it in the channel tray and squared it and rang the runner, and the runner came, and it went up.

It was the ninth hour. She sat back in the chair, the first time she had used it that morning, and she did not rest, because the reply she had written answered the petition only if the petition stood on its own authentication, and a petition of invalidation under a priority docket had to be authenticated by an officer of the court whose seal said the grounds were colorable and worth the registrar's day, and the question of who had set that seal was not Asha's. It was Silva's. Asha had marked it in the log as the second thing the morning would turn on, and she had no word yet that it had turned.

---

Silva had been on the registry steps since before the petition was filed, because Kaviel had put her there the night before on the strength of nothing but the dangerous hour, and Silva had spent her life being where a thing would happen before there was a thing.

She was not on the steps as herself. She was a factor's clerk with a satchel of someone else's paper, one of the forty such clerks who waited each morning on the broad shallow steps of the registry tower for the doors to take the day's business, and she stood among them in the cold with the smell of the vendor's grill at the foot of the steps coming up on the wind, charred fat and burnt grain and the sharp green of the herb the vendor cut over the meat, and she watched the doors and the people who went through them and she did the work she was best in three Thrones at, which was to see who carried what to whom and to remember it without appearing to have looked. She was not waiting for the petition. The petition would go in through the clerks' door like everything else and she would never see the sheet. She was waiting for the authentication, because an authentication was not paper that moved through a clerk's window. It was an officer of the court setting a seal, and an officer of the court setting a seal on a priority docket had to do it in person at the authenticating desk on the registry's second floor, and Silva had spent the previous afternoon learning, the way she learned things, which window of the second floor was the authenticating desk and where on the steps a clerk could stand and see the people who climbed to it.

She saw him at the eighth hour and a quarter. He came up the steps not hurrying and not slow, a man in a councillor's coat with the river-country cut to it, broad in the shoulders and going soft at the jaw, and he carried under his arm a folder banded in the red the court used for matters it meant to move, and he climbed the steps among the clerks and the factors without looking at any of them and went in through the principals' door, which the clerks did not use, and Silva let him go and did not follow and marked the time and the coat and the red band and the principals' door, and she knew him. She had watched him for six weeks in the spring from peripheries he never knew were peopled, and she had filed the watching in the field notes Kaviel kept, and she did not need the face turned toward her to name it. She let him reach the second floor. Then she crossed the steps to the vendor and bought a round of the grilled meat she did not want, and while the man cut it she stood with her back to the tower and her face to the street and took from the satchel the small flat field-set the Quarter issued and sent up the channel the one line Kaviel was waiting for, the time and the coat and the red-banded folder and the principals' door and the name, Hadi Marun, climbing to the authenticating desk.

She put the field-set away and ate the meat she did not want, because a clerk who bought food and did not eat it was a clerk a watcher remembered, and she stayed on the steps, because the man would come down again when the seal was set and she wanted the time of his coming down as much as she had wanted the time of his going up. The grease was cold on her fingers and the herb was sharp on the air, and Silva stood among the clerks and watched the principals' door and waited for a councillor to come back down a flight of stairs.

---

Kaviel had Silva's line at the eighth hour and a half, and it told her the petition was Marun's to authenticate, which told her the rest of it.

She had known there was a courtier. She had not known which, and she had not let herself guess, because a guess carried into the work was a finding spoiled at the root. Now she did not have to guess. Hadi Marun, who had heard a river-prefecture matter ruled against him by the throne and ruled fairly, who had owed in two directions for eleven years the way every councillor of his grade owed in two directions, had set his seal to a petition that named no inquiry and argued a void authority, and in setting it he had told the Quarter the one thing the petition had been built to keep hidden, which was that the people who had filed it were close enough to the court to reach a councillor and turn him to the authenticating desk in a single morning. The petition was clean. The hand that had moved Marun to authenticate it was not, because a clean petition did not need a councillor walked up a flight of stairs under a priority docket inside two hours of the dangerous gap opening. Someone had been waiting for the gap as carefully as Kaviel had. Someone had had Marun ready.

That was the thing past the day, and Kaviel set it down and did not follow it, because the day was not yet won and a person who chased the second thing while the first ran loose lost both. She wrote it into the running log in two lines for Asha, that the authentication was Marun's and that Marun's reach to the desk was the faction's reach into the court, and she left it there. The morning's work was the petition, and the petition would fall on Asha's reply if the reply reached the registrar before the docket closed, and the reply had gone up, and now there was the registrar himself, and the registrar's day, and what the faction would do to that day to keep Asha's reply from being read in it.

She did not have to wonder long. The registrar of record was a magister of the court named Faraj Oduya, exact and slow and proof against pressure of the ordinary kind, and the faction would not try the ordinary kind. They would try the docket. A priority docket that required a ruling within the working day was a docket that could be lost in a working day, buried under other priority matters filed the same morning so that the registrar reached the petition with an hour left and ruled on it tired and fast and on the face of the thing, which was clean, rather than on the reply, which had to be read slow to be believed. Kaviel had the morning's docket count from Otieno, eleven priority matters filed before the ninth hour where a busy day saw three. They were drowning the registrar. They meant Asha's reply to arrive at a desk too buried to read it.

So Kaviel did the thing she had built for. She did not fight the docket. She moved the reply out of it. Asha's reply had gone up as a response to the petition, to be read in the petition's hour at the bottom of a drowned docket. Kaviel sent a second copy of it by a different channel, away from the registrar's docket and into the registrar's chambers, addressed to Magister Oduya by name and carried by the Quarter's own runner under the standing arrangement by which the Lionesses could put a matter directly before a court officer when the matter touched the security of the imperial record, which this one did, and which Kaviel had been careful to phrase so that it did. It was a narrow door and she had used it perhaps four times in nine years and she used it now, and the using of it told Oduya, before he read a line, that the Quarter considered the morning's petition a matter touching the security of the record, which was a thing a magister of long service would weigh before he buried it under the docket. She wrote the cover line herself, short, and put the copy in the runner's hand, and sent it, and then there was nothing to do but the thing she was worst at, which was to wait while other people's hours ran.

---

Sael Mokoena's hour was running too, four hundred miles south in the city of Adwa, and it was running out from under him though he did not yet know it.

He had a role in the morning and he performed it, because he had been told to perform it and because the people who told him things had been right for four years and he had no ground left to doubt them from. The petition that argued a void authority needed, somewhere beneath the court's clean language, a fact to argue from, and the fact was Mokoena's. He was the deputy registrar of the Bureau's Outer Prefectures office, the man whose carry-forward initials sat at the head of the schedules the inquiry had unwound, and the petition's authors had built into it a sworn attestation from his office that the inquiry's investigators had, in the course of their gathering, requested and received records that lay above the working line, records his office held under the protected category, and that the request had carried no authorization the office could find. It was the spine of the thing. If a working registrar of the Bureau swore that the inquiry had reached above the line and taken protected records without authority, the petition's clean argument had a fact under it, and Asha's reply, which said the line had held, would stand against a sworn attestation from the office that kept the line, and a registrar choosing between a Lioness's word and a Bureau registrar's oath about the Bureau's own records would have to weigh the oath.

Mokoena's task was to swear it. He had been given the attestation drafted, the records named, the dates set, and all that was asked of him was the oath of an officer that the request had crossed his desk and that no authorization had accompanied it, and he had sat with the draft at the desk worn pale at the front edge where two generations of registrars had rested their forearms, with the bitter red tea cooling at his elbow and the smell of the lamp he lit each morning though the window gave him light, and he had read the draft for the third time and known that it was not true.

He knew it was not true because he kept his own pigeonholes and trusted no one to touch them, and he had gone to them the moment the draft arrived, the night before, and pulled the correspondence log of his office for the period the attestation named, and the inquiry's investigators had not requested the protected records from his office, because the inquiry had never reached above the line, because Asha had stopped at the wall. There was no request that had come to him without authorization, because there was no request at all. The attestation asked him to swear to the absence of an authorization on a thing that had never been asked of him, and a man could perhaps swear to an absence if he did not look, but Mokoena had looked, because looking was the only thing he had ever been good at, and now he knew, and the knowing sat on the desk with the cooling tea.

He had inserted false appointments through the carry-forward provision for four years, taking by other means a seat he believed he was owed, and across four years he had told himself a long quiet string of true-sounding things about what he was and was not doing, and not one of them had required him to set his name under an oath that he knew at the moment of setting it to be false. The frauds had been done in the gaps the provision left, in the spaces between what was true and what could be proven, and a man could live a long time in those spaces and call himself an honest registrar who had made one arrangement. The oath was not a gap. The oath was his name and his hand and the word sworn, and the word was false, and the people who had sent him the draft had not asked whether he could swear it. They had assumed he would, because he had done everything else, and the assuming was the place their understanding of him ran out.

He sat with it through the morning while his hour ran. He drank the tea cold. He read the correspondence log a fourth time, hunting for the request that would have made the oath true, and it was not there, and a floor-keeper drew the wide mop across the corridor outside his door and the wet trail of it shone and faded and the transcription engine clicked through its backlog somewhere above him, patient as rain on a roof, the sounds of the office he had loved for nineteen years before he had begun to use it. And at the eleventh hour, with the attestation still unsigned and the messenger waiting in the outer office for the sealed oath the petition could not stand without, Sael Mokoena did the thing that collapsed one floor of the structure that had protected him, which was nothing. He did not sign. He could not bring his hand to set his name under a sworn falsehood he had checked and knew to be false, and he could not bring himself to refuse outright and tell the people who had been right for four years that he would not, and so he did the thing a careful frightened man does, which was to delay, to send word that the records were being verified, that the attestation would follow within the day, that he wished to be exact. The day did not have within it. The petition needed the oath by the docket's hour and the oath did not come, and the messenger waited in the outer office past the hour it could be useful, and four hundred miles north the petition went before the registrar of record with its clean argument and its void authority and no sworn fact beneath it, because the one man who could have sworn the fact had read his own correspondence log and could not make his hand move.

He had not chosen a side. He told himself that, sitting at the pale-edged desk with the lamp lit and the tea cold and the unsigned draft in front of him, that he had only waited to be sure. But the waiting was the thread, and Asha would have it within the week, because a registrar who delayed an attestation his principals needed by the hour was a registrar whose principals would now move to protect the structure from him, and a man being moved against by the people who had protected him was a man with a reason to be found by the people on the other side, and Asha read threads like that by the place where the friction stopped. Mokoena did not know any of that yet. He knew only that he had not signed, and he sat in the office that had been his for nineteen years and listened to the mop and the engine and waited for an instruction that was not going to come, because the people who gave him instructions had already understood, faster than he had, what his silence meant.

---

The registrar ruled at the close of the working day, and Kaviel was in the registry to watch it, because she had earned the right to be in the room and because she did not trust a thing she had not seen with her own eyes.

She stood at the back of the registrar's anteroom where the parties to a docket waited for the day's rulings, among the factors and the clerks and the one councillor's man sent to carry the result back to whoever had sent the petition, and she watched Magister Faraj Oduya come out to the long desk where the rulings were read and set down his folio of the day's work, heavy with the eleven priority matters, and read them out in the order he had taken them, slow and exact and proof against the weight of the docket in a way the petition's authors had wagered he would not be. He came to the matter of invalidation last, as a buried matter came last, and Kaviel stood very still at the back of the room with her hands folded and gave the watchers around her nothing to read, and Oduya read the ruling.

He had read Asha's reply. Kaviel knew it from the first line of the ruling, because the ruling took up the petition's argument about void authority on the ground Asha had built, the distinction between reading a locked record and gathering from within it, and found, the ruling said, no showing that the inquiry had gathered from within the protected category, the attestation that would have made such a showing not having been received, and the petition resting therefore on an assertion of reach unsupported by any record of reach. He ruled the grounds not colorable. He returned the petition without prejudice to refiling on a proper showing, which was a magister's exact way of saying that the people who had filed it could come back when they had a fact, and he set his seal to the ruling and closed the docket, and the working day ended with the findings of the inquiry standing in the record exactly where they had stood at the seventh hour of the morning, and the Empress's notation, when it was entered, would have its page to protect.

The smell of the anteroom was sealing wax and lamp oil and the cold stone of a building emptying for the night. The councillor's man heard the ruling and turned at once and went, fast, to carry it back. Kaviel did not watch him go. She watched Oduya gather his folio and rise from the long desk, an old exact man at the end of a long drowned day, and she watched the petition returned to the clerk's hand to be filed away without prejudice, and she felt the day settle out of her shoulders the way the work always settled when it was done, slow and incomplete, because a day won was only a day.

Asha had come into the anteroom at the last ruling and stood beside her, having walked up from the Quarter to see the thing land, and the two of them stood at the back of the emptying room while the clerks banked the lamps along the long desk and the wax cooled on the day's seals, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"The attestation never came," Asha said at last. It was not a question. She had built the reply on the chance that it would not and she had watched the ruling turn on its absence, and she was marking the fact aloud the way she marked a finding, to set it where it could be acted on.

"No," Kaviel said. "Mokoena did not sign."

Asha took that and turned it once and set it where it belonged, which was at the head of the thread she would follow, the registrar in Adwa who had been given a thing to swear and had not sworn it and was now, this evening, a man his own people would be moving against. She did not say any of that. She had it, and Kaviel knew she had it, and the knowing passed between them without a word spent on it.

The clerks banked the last of the lamps and the anteroom went to the dim it kept for the night, and the returned petition lay on the clerk's desk in its red band, struck off the docket, waiting to be carried down to the files where matters went that had not stood. Kaviel watched it lie there, the clean fast elegant thing that had nearly worked, and she did the arithmetic again, the new arithmetic, the one that began the moment the old one closed.

"They have another move," she said. Quiet, to Asha, not turning from the desk.

"I know," Asha said.
