# Chapter 28 — Amira Alone

The household had withdrawn at the tenth hour, as it withdrew on every night she did not keep someone with her, and the quarters had gone over to the quiet they kept after the people left, which was a quiet with a floor under it and not a true silence. Amira had learned the floor of it in her first year. The building breathed. Somewhere below the private wing a furnace ran its long even labor against the cold that came up out of the New Terra stone after dark, and the heat of it climbed through the walls and the floors as a faint travel, a thing felt in the soles before it was heard, and over that ran the smaller sounds, a shutter taking the wind on the north face, the watch changing at the foot of the stair with the soft exchange of two men who had done it a thousand times and no longer needed words for it. She had stopped, years ago, expecting the quarters to be quiet. A throne did not buy quiet. It bought a better grade of noise, and a door no one would open without her leave, and those were not the same thing, and she had made her peace with the difference the way she had made her peace with the weight of the overrobe.

The overrobe was off. It hung on its stand by the inner door where the dresser had left it, holding its own shape without her inside it, the metal thread catching the lamp in short dull lines down the seams. She had worn it eleven hours. The marks of it were still on her, the pressure of the collar along the back of her neck, the place at the shoulders where the weight gathered and sat, and she rolled the neck once, slowly, and felt the day let go of it by a degree, and did not pretend the rest of the day would let go so easily.

She had eaten. She had eaten alone, at the small table by the cold hearth, the plain late meal the kitchen sent up when she dined out of the hall, and she had read while she ate, which her physician disliked and which she did anyway, because the reading was the work and the eating was the maintenance of the thing that did the work, and she had long since stopped granting the maintenance a ceremony of its own. The plate was cleared to the side now. A cup of the bitter southern tea sat at her hand, cooling, the steam off it gone thin. The taste of it was on the back of her tongue, the resin-and-ash edge the eastern prefectures cured into the leaf, and she drank it for no reason of pleasure and none of wakefulness. It was the cup she drank at this hour, and the hour wanted its cup.

Tomorrow she would seat the first appointments under the new oversight.

She had come to the desk to read the form of it one more time, the document her own office had drafted under her hand and the council had not yet seen, the mechanism that would stand where the carry-forward reconciliation had stood, and she had read it twice through and found nothing in it she would change tonight, and now she sat with it under her palm and did not read it a third time, because reading it a third time would have been a way of not thinking about the thing under it, and she had not held a throne for five years by letting herself not think about the thing under it.

The thing under it was the cost.

She turned the lamp down a quarter. The room came in closer around the smaller flame, the painted ceiling going up into a dark she could no longer make out, the shelves along the western wall losing their edges, and she sat in the close warm circle of the light with the document under one hand and the cooling cup beside the other and let herself, for the first time since the chamber on the third floor of the Hall of Establishments, count the bill from the top.

She had invoked the emergency prerogative of the Throne. No sovereign had done it in thirty-one years, and the last to do it had spent the rest of a long reign explaining the doing, and Amira had read every line of that explaining when she was a girl being made into what she now was, and she had read it again three weeks ago in the windowless briefing room with Kaviel across the table and the evidence between them, before she put the pen to the notation in her own hand. She had known what the act would cost when she committed it to paper. A prerogative used was a prerogative no longer asleep. Every Throne that came after her would govern in a court that had seen this one reach past the ordinary administration and take a case out of the channels and into a court of her own constituting, and some of those later Thrones would reach for the same lever for worse reasons, and would point at her when they did. She had handed the future a weapon to use against itself. That was the first line of the bill, and it did not get smaller for being looked at squarely, and she looked at it squarely, because the alternative had been to let the channels adjudicate a fraud the channels were compromised to protect, and that was not an alternative, it was a slower surrender dressed as procedure.

She had done it in open court. That was the second line. She could have suspended the proceeding against Eleanor in chambers, quietly, on paper, and let the transfer reach the public record after the fact in the dry notation such things wore. She had chosen the chamber, the people in it, the witnesses, the Arbiter standing to receive the jurisdiction from the throne's own mouth. She had chosen to be seen doing the most exposed thing of her reign, because a thing done in the dark could be undone in the dark, and a thing done in the light had to be undone in the light or not at all, and she had wanted Marek's faction, and the men around it who had not been named and might never be, to understand that the throne had not flinched from the seeing. It had been the right choice. She did not revisit it to second-guess it. She revisited it to hold it, the way she held everything she had decided and could not afford to forget she had decided, because a ruler who forgot the weight of her own past acts would mistake the next one for the first.

The wind found the north shutter again and worked it, and she heard the watch settle it, the scrape of the latch and the man's low word to the cold, and then the building breathing on under that.

She thought about Eleanor.

She did it without sentiment, because there was no sentiment in it to begin with, and she had never once let herself import any, not in five years of the most consequential professional relationship of her life. She and Eleanor De Grosvenor were not friends. They had met privately three times in the small reception room with the two chairs and the window over the garden neither of them looked at, and the three meetings were the whole of their intimacy, and the intimacy was the intimacy of two people who carried, between them, the load that the compact existed to carry, and who trusted each other to carry it and not one inch further than that. Amira had never wanted it further. The compact bought the throne an apparatus it could not have built and could not control, and it bought the house a sovereign who would let it serve without bending it into a court faction, and the price of the bargain was that neither party ever pretended the bargain was love. Amira had kept the price exactly. She kept it still.

And Eleanor had gone out past the frame of the compact, alone, to a neutral room she had arranged, to give Marek a choice before any formal action, and she had not told the throne why, only asked for the three days, and Amira had given them without the why, because that too was the compact, the throne extending the trust the house had not formally earned, as the throne before hers had extended it, and the one before that. Amira knew what the three days had cost. She had read it in the chamber when Eleanor sat silent through the transfer, present and not active, and she had read it again in the throne room three mornings ago, in the chair behind the screen, in the stillness with no current under it. The crisis had taken something out of the old woman, and it had not given it back, and it would not, and Amira had seen this the way she saw everything she could not act on in session and could not let herself forget, and she had put it where she put such things, and it sat there now, in the close light, refusing to be filed.

She knew the other thing too. She knew what Kaviel had seen.

It had reached her by no channel anyone could name, the way the worst-weighing things reached a throne, in the half-beat Kaviel's circuit of the western wall had held on the band of shadow behind the screen a fraction longer than a column's eye would hold, in the earliness with which the old woman now left the hall, in a hundred small administrative facts that added to one fact a daughter had read before the throne read it and would carry whether or not she ever set it on the gray dispatch paper in the four fixed sentences. Kaviel knew her mother was failing. Amira knew that Kaviel knew. Neither of them had said a word of it, and neither would, because there was no use in the words and a great deal of cost, and the two of them, the Empress and the senior Lioness who carried the house's name without performing it, understood the economy of that cost in the same currency. It was the nearest thing to fellow feeling Amira permitted herself about the De Grosvenors, this shared refusal to spend on what could not be bought back, and she permitted it tonight, alone, with the lamp down and the tea going cold, because there was no court to read her permitting it.

She drank the last of the tea. It had gone cool and the resin had settled to the bottom and the last mouthful carried the grit of it, bitter, and she set the cup down on its saucer with the small flat click of porcelain on porcelain that was the loudest sound the room had made in an hour.

Was it worth it.

That was the question she had come to the desk to answer, under the cover of reading a document she had already mastered, and she made herself put it plainly, in the words a petitioner would use, because a throne that dressed its hardest questions in the soft language of statecraft was a throne already lying to itself. Was the bill worth the thing it had bought. She had spent a prerogative that would not sleep again in her lifetime. She had exposed the house's apparatus and the house's head. She had bought herself a court that would, for the rest of her reign, measure every reach of the throne against the morning it had reached this far, and some of them would never trust the reach again, and she would govern the rest of her life among people who had watched her do the largest thing she would ever do and could not unsee it.

Against that, she had seated a fraud's dismantling. The carry-forward clause was closed. Marek was before a court that could not be reached from inside the Bureau he had run, and the financial channels were pinned where the prerogative could hold them, and the appointments of three Thrones' worth of jurisdiction would be made, from tomorrow, under an oversight that answered past the Bureau, by people who put their names where offices had been, and the prefectures that had been governed by magistrates seated foully would be governed, going forward, by magistrates seated in the light. She had not fixed the empire. An empire was not a thing that got fixed. She had closed one wound that had been left open for seven years because closing it required a hand willing to be seen reaching, and she had been the hand, and that was the whole of what a throne was for, and the men who came after her with worse reasons would have to find their own justifications, because she was not responsible for the uses bad sovereigns made of good precedents, she was responsible for the case in front of her, and the case in front of her had been a fraud protected at the height of the administration, and she had ended it.

The answer was not a triumph. She felt no lift in it, none of the heat she had felt as a younger woman when she imagined doing large things and being right to have done them. What she felt was something flatter and more durable, the thing a person feels who has weighed a load correctly and lifted it and set it down where it belonged and knows the weight will be back tomorrow in another shape. The answer was settled. She had done the thing that the position required of the person who held it, and she had paid what it cost, and she would pay the rest of what it cost across the years it would take to come due, and she would have done it again, knowing the price, because the price was the price of being the one who held the position and not merely the one who occupied it. She had stopped, somewhere in these five years, being able to tell those two apart in herself. The crisis had finished the work of joining them. She did not name the change. It did not need naming. It was in the way she had set the cup down, and in the document under her hand that she would seat in the morning, and in the fact that she had asked the question at all instead of letting the success answer it for her.

She rose from the desk.

She had not slept more than five hours a night since the briefing room, and she would not sleep much tonight, and she had stopped fighting the arithmetic of it. The body did the work the position needed and the position did not consult the body about the hours. She crossed the close warm circle of the lamp and out of it into the cooler dark of the room, where the floor gave the furnace's heat back faintly through the soles of her feet, and she went to the tall window on the eastern wall that gave out over the imperial complex, and she drew the inner curtain aside.

The complex lay below her in the dark, the great administrative city within the city, and it was not dark. It was never dark. The night office of an empire that spanned what this one spanned could not afford the dark, and so the windows burned, scattered and clustered across the black bulk of the wings and the halls and the bureaus, a hundred rooms and more where the work that did not stop for the hour was being done by people whose names she would never know, the night clerks and the duty registrars of a machine that ran on after every individual hand let go of it. She stood at the glass and looked at the lights. The Hall of Establishments was among them, somewhere in the middle distance, where the new oversight would be entered into the record within the month under her hand, where tonight some clerk was probably still moving paper that the crisis had displaced. The Lioness compound was beyond it, low and lit, running as it always ran. She could not see Eleanor's wing from here. She knew where it was. A window or two in that quarter would be burning, and one of them might be the office with the west-facing glass and the cold tea, where a daughter had sat an afternoon that the throne had heard about through no channel it could name, and the throne did not look for that window, because there were things a throne had no business seeing even when it knew exactly where to look.

The glass was cold. She felt it before she touched it, the chill standing off the pane into the warmth of the room, and she raised her hand and set her palm flat against it, and the cold came into her hand the way the cold came up through the marble a stride to either side of the warm band in the throne room, the cold that was always there at the edge of the warmth and that the warmth was borrowed against. Her breath fogged a small bloom on the pane below her hand and shrank and went. Below her the lights burned in their hundred rooms, the work running, the empire keeping its long even labor against the dark the way the furnace kept it against the cold, with no single hand on it and every hand part of it, hers among them, hers most of all and least of all at once.

She held her hand on the glass a moment longer.

Then she turned from the window, back into the room and the warmth and the small flame of the turned-down lamp, and went to put out the light and take what sleep the night would give her, because the appointments would be seated at the fourth hour and there was still tomorrow.
