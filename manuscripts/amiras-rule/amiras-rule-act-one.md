# AMIRA'S RULE

### The Iseldoran Sagas — Act One

_Prologue, Chapters 1 through 8, Interludes 1 and 2. Draft._

---

# Prologue

I have set down the date in the upper margin of this page out of habit, the way I have begun every official record for forty years, and then I have sat looking at it long enough that the lamp oil has burned down a finger's width. The Imperial Record Office is quiet at this hour. The clerks are gone. The stone holds the cold of the courtyard outside and gives it back slowly, so that even in the warm months the lower vaults stay chilled, and a man writing here past midnight learns to keep his hands moving.

My name is Adulfus Khan. I hold the rank of Captain, retired from the field and assigned now to the keeping of records, which is a posting men of my service tend to regard as a courtesy extended to the old. I have stopped resenting it. There is more danger in a room like this one than most of the court would credit, because what is written here becomes the thing that happened, and what is left out stops having happened at all. I descend from the line of Harlow Khan. I mention this so that whoever reads it understands the discipline I was raised to, and for no reason of vanity in the name itself. A soldier does not embellish his report. He states what he saw, what he was told, and which of the two it was.

What follows is the history of the first crisis the Empress Amira faced without anyone above her to absorb the cost of failure. I have spent the better part of eleven years assembling it. The testimony fills four shelves of the east vault. I have read all of it more than once, the depositions and the duplicated minutes and the field reports that came in folded so many times the creases had worn through the ink, and I have set my hand against each page the way one sets a hand against a wall in the dark, to learn the shape of a thing you cannot yet see whole.

I will tell you now what I will not do. I will not give you the end of it here. I have read records that announced their own conclusions in the first lines, and a reader who knows the ending reads everything afterward as a man already braced for the blow, which is no way to understand how the people inside it lived through the not-knowing. They did not know. For a long time they did not even know there was something to be known. That is the truth I am most concerned to preserve, and it is the hardest, because I sit here with all four shelves behind me and the temptation to make them wiser than they were is a constant pressure against the stylus.

There is the matter of the De Grosvenor house, which this record places, perhaps for the first time in the sanctioned archive, under direct historical inquiry. I am aware of what that means. Houses of that standing are accustomed to being described, not examined. I have examined it. I have done so because the events I am recording cannot be understood without it, and because Eleanor De Grosvenor, whatever else may be said of her in the chambers where such things are said, built an apparatus over six decades that held the administrative spine of this empire upright through seasons that should have broken it. I met her three times. I will not pretend the meetings were warm. I will say that I have never in my life sat across from a mind that wasted so little, and that the respect I record here is owed, not granted.

The wax on the older depositions has gone hard and dark, and when I break a seal to copy a passage it gives off a smell like a doused candle, faintly bitter, that has come to mean work to me the way the smell of cut metal once meant the field. My fingers know the difference between the imperial vellum and the cheaper stock the prefectures used for their daily traffic before I have looked down to see which I am holding. I tell you these small things because they are the texture of the work, and because a record that admits its own making is harder to lie with.

I want to be plain about why this account exists at all, since the reign survived. It survived. The Trinitarian Throne stands, and the woman who holds it holds it still. But there was a stretch of months, the months this record concerns, when that outcome was not yet decided, and the thing that nearly undid it was not an army and not an assassin. It was a process. A clause in the minutes of a routine commission, a column of dates that did not agree, a confirmation entered before the body that confirmed it had met. Small things. The empire is administered by small things, and so it can be unmade by them, quietly, with everyone's signature in the proper place.

I have decided how to record this. That decision took me longer than the writing will. I have chosen to begin where the people in it began, with no warning of what they were standing on, and to let the floor go out from under the reader at the same pace it went out from under them. It is the only honest way I know to do it, and I have spent eleven years deciding it was honest enough.

The lamp wants trimming. Beyond the vault door the long hall runs empty toward the stair, and there is no sound in this building but the stone settling and my own stylus against the page, which is the sound I will leave you with before I begin. Turn the leaf. You have already agreed to know this, by reading even this far, and I would not have you stop now and carry only the half of it.

# Chapter 1 — The Weight of the Throne Room

The incense had been lit at the fourth hour and would burn until the court adjourned, and by the time the second petition came forward the smoke had settled into the high vaulting and given up trying to rise. Amira sat in it. The ceremonial overrobe across her shoulders carried the weight of a small grown person, the brocade stiffened with metal thread along the seams so that it held its own shape whether she moved or not, and she had learned in the first year of her reign to let it hold her rather than fight it. She kept her spine against the back of the throne. She kept her hands where the court could see them. The marble in front of the dais ran in long bands of polish, and through the polish, if the light from the high windows struck it at the angle it struck it now, a person could read the old repair lines, the seams where the floor had been broken and laid again across four hundred years of audiences. The light came down in cut columns from the eastern clerestory. Where it touched the floor the stone was nearly warm. A stride to either side and the cold came up through the soles of the feet like water.

The Clerk of Petitions stood at the foot of the dais with the day's register, a tall man named Obasi whose voice had been trained to carry the length of the hall without seeming to rise. He read the first matter into the room. A waterworks dispute out of the river prefectures, two guilds and a question of who had paid for a cistern and who now owned the right to charge for it. Amira heard it through. She asked Obasi for the date of the original grant, and when he found it she asked who had witnessed it, and when the witness proved to be a man both guilds had since buried she ruled the grant lapsed and ordered the cistern held in common under the prefect's seal until a new instrument could be drawn. The guildmasters bowed and withdrew, neither pleased, neither cheated. That was the work. She did not expect to please. She expected to be exact, and to be seen to be exact, because a court that believed the throne was paying attention behaved as though the throne was always paying attention, and that belief did more governing than any decree she could sign.

The forms between matters were their own small labor. An usher came forward with the closing of the first file and laid it on the register table to the Clerk's left, and the under-clerk who kept the running minute dipped his pen and wrote, and the soft scratch of it carried up into the smoke and was lost. The court breathed in the interval. Somewhere along the southern aisle a child of one of the standing delegations coughed and was hushed. A censer-bearer crossed below the dais on soft feet to feed the nearest brazier, and the incense thickened for a moment, sweet and resinous over the flat mineral cold of the stone, and Amira felt it catch at the back of her throat the way it caught every morning by the fifth hour and made no sign of it. Her water cup stood untouched on the small shelf inside the right arm of the throne. She had learned not to drink in court. A ruler who reached for her cup gave the hall a thing to watch that was not the ruling, and the hall would watch it, and remember that she had been thirsty, and forget what she had decided.

Obasi read the second matter while the under-clerk still wrote the first. A criminal review out of the coastal customs courts: a shipping factor convicted of falsifying weights against the duty, sentenced to the loss of his license and a term of labor, appealing the term as beyond the statute for a first offense. Amira asked for the statute and Obasi read it. She asked whether the man had stood a prior conviction under any name, and the customs advocate, a brisk woman in the green sash of the revenue courts, allowed after a pause that the prosecution had not searched the registers of the two neighboring jurisdictions where the factor had traded before. Amira held the appeal. She did not free the man and she did not confirm the term; she sent the matter back to the coastal court with an instruction that the neighboring registers be searched first and the term fixed to whatever the search returned, and she set a date by which the search must close so that the man would not rot uncharged while a clerk took his time. The advocate bowed. The court understood, as it was meant to, that the throne would neither be hurried into cruelty nor talked into laxness, and that it read the statute as carefully as it read the man.

Behind her, and a little to the right, set back of the formal dais where the carved screen threw a permanent band of shadow across the floor, Eleanor De Grosvenor sat in a plain chair that had been carried in for her and would be carried out when she left. From the body of the hall she was nearly invisible. A petitioner looking up at the throne saw the Empress and the canopy and the gold; he did not see the old woman in the shadow unless he already knew to look, and the ones who knew to look were careful never to be caught doing it. Amira did not turn to her. In the four years since she had taken the throne she had turned to Eleanor in open court exactly twice, both times in the first month, and she had decided after the second that she would not do it again. The compact between the De Grosvenor house and the Trinitarian Throne was older than either of them and would outlast both, and part of what it bought the throne was that Eleanor could sit in that chair and the court would feel her there without the Empress ever appearing to need her. Amira governed. Eleanor watched her govern. The arrangement suited them both and neither of them found it comfortable, which was, Amira had come to think, the proof that it was honest.

Near the western wall, where the colonnade ran back into the administrative passages, a woman in the dark working coat of the Lionesses stood with her hands behind her and her attention moving over the hall in a slow unhurried circuit. Kaviel. She was not on the rolls of the court and held no ceremonial place; she was there because Eleanor was there, and the room registered her the way it registered a load-bearing column, as a thing that was supposed to be present and so drew no eye. Twice Amira's gaze crossed the western wall in the course of the morning and twice it passed over Kaviel without stopping, which was how both of them preferred it.

The hall held perhaps two hundred that morning, a thin court for a working session with no ceremony attached. Along the standing rails the delegations waited in the dress of a dozen prefectures, the river guilds in their banded sashes, two magisters of the Bureau of Establishments in the gray of the administrative service, a delegation of upland herders whose business stood eighth on the register and who had come down out of the high country to wait however long the throne kept them. Faces dark and faces darker, the broad cheekbones and the long bones of the eastern steppe-blood mixed through the southern lines as they had been mixed for the four hundred years the floor had been broken and relaid, a court that looked like the empire because the empire had made it. Amira knew a third of them by name and another third by office and the rest she would know by the time they reached the foot of the dais, because Obasi would have told her, quietly, in the breath before each name was called, who they were and what they wanted and what they had wanted the last time. He stood now at her right hand with the register and waited for the under-clerk's pen to lift.

Obasi read the third matter.

"A petition of grievance," he said, "out of the Sennar Prefecture. The petitioner contests a ruling of the prefectural magistrate's court and seeks the throne's review of the judgment against him."

A man came forward into the cut light below the dais and went down to one knee on the warm band of marble. He was perhaps fifty, dressed in the good but unfashionable clothes of a provincial man of property, and his hands when he pressed them together were the hands of someone who had done physical work before he had done well enough to stop. He gave his name as Demba Tunde, a holder of orchard land in the Sennar uplands. He spoke plainly. The magistrate's court had found against him in a dispute over a water easement crossing his neighbor's ground, had awarded the easement to the neighbor, and had assessed Tunde the costs of the hearing. He did not contest the throne's right to let the ruling stand. He contested the court that had made it.

"On what ground," Amira said.

"On the ground that the magistrate who heard me had no business on the bench, Majesty." Tunde kept his eyes down, as the form required, but his voice did not waver. "Magister Iwan Kebe. He sat my case in the spring. I have since learned he was set in his office not three months before, over the head of the deputy magistrate who had served the district eleven years and whom every man in the prefecture took for the next to hold the seat. I do not say the ruling was bought. I say I do not know how the man came to be the one ruling, and when a stranger costs me my water and my coin both, I would know by what right he sat."

It was a common enough grievance and a weak one, in law. A litigant who loses will find fault with the judge; the throne heard a version of it most weeks. Amira had begun, almost, to frame the refusal. She turned to Obasi. "The magistrate's commission. Is it in the prefectural record?"

Obasi was ready for the question; he had read the file before he read the petition, as he read every file, and he had the relevant page marked. "It is, Majesty. Magister Iwan Kebe, confirmed to the Sennar prefectural bench by the Third Appointments Commission, seated under the Bureau of Establishments, by carry-forward reconciliation of a prior confirmation. The entry is regular. The seal is the Bureau's."

"Read me the dates."

"The carry-forward entry is dated the eleventh of Threshing. The session of the Third Appointments Commission under which it was entered convened on the second of the Reaping month." He paused only to be sure he had the months in the right relation, then read them again in order, as a clerk does. "Threshing precedes Reaping by some five weeks, Majesty."

It went past her. It went past the hall. A confirmation entered before the session that entered it was the kind of clerical disorder that filled the lower courts and the prefectural registries, a clerk transposing two dates out of a stack of three hundred, a copyist working from a draft instead of the engrossed minute. The Bureau of Establishments reconciled thousands of such entries a year and the carry-forward provision existed for exactly that reason, to let confirmations made good under one commission be carried into the record of the next without dragging every magistrate in the Outer Prefectures back through fresh review. Amira heard the disorder, weighed it, and set it where it belonged, among the small frictions of a machine too large to run without friction. The man's complaint was that he had lost. The commission was regular. The seal was the Bureau's.

"The commission's confirmation stands on its own authority, Magister Tunde, whatever the order of the clerk's pen," she said. "A magistrate's right to the bench does not turn on which day a copyist dated the carry-forward, and a ruling does not fall because the litigant who lost it dislikes the judge. The judgment of the Sennar court holds. I will not assess you the costs of this petition; you came in good faith and the throne does not punish that. But you will keep to the easement as the court has set it." She inclined her head a degree, the dismissal. "Go home and mind your orchard."

Tunde pressed his hands together once more, the disappointment closing over his face without surprise, as though he had not let himself hope for more than to be heard. He rose. He backed the three required steps out of the cut light, turned at the edge of it, and walked the long marble toward the western doors, and the cold came up around him as he left the warm band, and the sound of his good provincial boots came back off the high stone for the length of his going.

Behind the throne, in the band of shadow, Eleanor De Grosvenor moved.

It was a small thing. She had sat the whole morning with the stillness of the very old, the economy of someone who has learned that motion costs and pays for nothing, her hands folded over the head of a cane she did not strictly need, her eyes half-lidded in the smoke. When Obasi read the second date she opened them. When he set the two months in their right order her chin came up perhaps a finger's width, and her gaze, which had rested all morning on the middle distance, found the man Tunde and held to him, and tracked him, and did not let go of him as he crossed the floor. The half-lidded fatigue was gone out of her face. What replaced it was attention, the whole of it, gathered and pointed, and it cost her something to gather it; the lines around her mouth drew in and her knuckles whitened a shade against the dark wood of the cane. No one in the body of the hall could see it. The Empress, facing forward, could not see it. The petitioner, walking away, would never know that for the length of his walk out of the Trinitarian Throne Room the most formidable mind the empire had built in sixty years was bent on him entire, reading the back of his good coat, fixing the prefecture and the magistrate and the impossible pair of dates into a memory that had given up nothing important since before the Empress was born.

Across the hall, against the western wall, Kaviel had felt the change before she could have named its source. She had spent her life in rooms with her mother and had learned to read the temperature of Eleanor's attention the way a sailor reads a shift in a wind he cannot yet see on the water. She did not look at Eleanor. She looked where Eleanor was looking, at the orchard-holder crossing the floor, and found nothing in him, a provincial man who had lost his water and would lose his appeal and go home, and she filed the nothing away because her mother had filed something, and the difference between what each of them could see was the oldest fact of their lives.

She held the file in her head the way she held everything, by its parts. Sennar Prefecture. A magistrate named Kebe set over the head of a man who had earned the seat. A confirmation entered under a clause she knew from a hundred rosters, the carry-forward, the thing the Bureau used to move old work into new books without redoing it. A date in front of a date that was supposed to follow it. Taken one piece at a time it was the ordinary disorder of a registry, and Kaviel had read enough registries to know how ordinary that disorder was, how a single clerk on a long afternoon could put the months in the wrong relation and seal the error before anyone caught it. She turned the pieces over once more and they sat where they had sat, harmless, and she let them lie. What she did not let lie was her mother's chin coming up. Eleanor did not spend attention on a clerk's wrong afternoon. She had found no weight in the matter herself. She marked it anyway. The one person alive who could find weight where Kaviel found none had bent the whole of herself toward an orchard-holder walking out a door, and that was reason enough.

At the foot of the dais Obasi had caught it too, in his way. He had read the dates as a clerk reads, in the order the file gave them, and the order had snagged on something in him a half-beat before he smoothed it over and moved to the next matter; a man who handled the register every working day of his life felt a confirmation that preceded its commission the way a weaver feels a slubbed thread under the thumb. He had said nothing. It was not his office to say. He had marked the page with the small folded corner he used for entries the throne might want again, and he would carry the file back to the record room himself rather than send it down with the under-clerk, and that was the whole of what a careful man in his place could do without overstepping it. He waited at Amira's right hand and let the next name rise to his lips.

Obasi was already reading the next matter, a question of a guild charter and a disputed inheritance, and the court resettled toward it, and the smoke hung in the vaulting, and the angled light had moved a hand's breadth along the marble while the orchard-holder spoke so that the warm band fell now a little nearer the dais than it had. Amira heard the petition into the room. She did not look behind her. The morning went on the way mornings in that hall had gone on for four hundred years, the throne ruling and the court believing itself watched, and the work of it pressing down on her shoulders through the metal-threaded brocade with a weight she had stopped, long since, mistaking for the weight of the cloth.

At the western doors the petitioner Tunde reached the threshold, where two of the door-wardens stood the watch in their formal gray, and passed between them out of the cut light and the cold stone and the incense into the colonnade beyond, and the great doors took him, and he was gone.

In the shadow behind the throne, Eleanor De Grosvenor watched the doors swallow the man, and held her eyes a moment longer on the dark where he had been. Then she lowered her chin the finger's width it had risen, and folded one hand more closely over the other on the head of the cane, and looked again at nothing.

# Chapter 2 — The Lioness Quarter

The compound did not sleep, and it did not announce that it was awake. Asha had served in buildings that performed their readiness, that put armed men at every door and polished the brass twice a day so that a visitor would feel watched the moment he crossed the threshold. The Lioness Quarter was not such a building. From the imperial district a man walking past would see a long administrative block of gray stone with shuttered windows and a single unmarked door, the kind of structure that housed a records bureau or a tax registry, and he would walk on. Inside, the corridors ran on a rhythm that never stopped. Two floors below the assessment level the kitchens had been at work since the third hour, and the smell of them came up through the stairwells in slow drifts, bread and the burnt-sugar edge of something left too long over heat, so that the whole upper floor carried a faint kitchen warmth under the harder smell of metal polish and the oil they used on the door hinges to keep them from speaking. Asha had stopped noticing it years ago. She noticed it this morning only because the candidate, when she came in, lifted her chin a fraction toward the door as though to track where the smell had come from, and Asha marked that, and set it aside, and said nothing yet.

She had come down to the assessment level a half hour early, as she always did, and walked the floor before the candidate arrived. That was an old habit and she had stopped pretending it was anything else. The Quarter ran in shifts that overlapped at the edges so that no hour found the building unmanned, and the early walk let her take the temperature of it the way a watch officer takes a sounding. In the analysis hall the long tables had been at work since before her, a dozen of the junior researchers bent over rosters under the green-shaded lamps, the scratch of their pens and the dry whisper of turned pages making the only sound on that floor, and she had passed through without a word and been noted and not greeted, which was correct. Two of the wall maps had been re-pinned overnight against a revised jurisdictional chart, and someone had left the old sheet folded on the side table with a note clipped to it for the archivist, and Asha had read the note in passing and approved of the hand that wrote it. Below her the kitchens worked. Above her, in the records vault she did not go up to that morning, the cross-reference clerks held the files no single bureau was permitted to hold all together, and somewhere out past the noon transit Silva was already in the field, sending up by the standard channel the periphery summaries that would be on Asha's table by the time the assessment was done. The building did its work in all its parts at once and made no sound about any of it, and Asha had given the better share of her life to it and walked its floor in the early hour the way another woman might walk a garden.

The room was built to be plain. A table of dark wood worn pale at the edges where forearms had rested across forty years of these conversations. Two chairs. A third against the wall for Meret, who sat in it now with a folder closed on her knee and her hands folded over it, present and saying nothing, which was how Meret was present in most rooms. On the wall behind Asha hung the organizational maps, sheet after sheet of them pinned in overlapping ranks, the protective rosters and the jurisdictional charts and the personnel assessments reduced to columns of initials and date-codes that meant nothing to anyone who had not spent a decade learning to read them. The candidate's eyes had gone to the maps first, before the chairs, before Asha. That too Asha marked. A person could be told a great deal by what they looked at when they thought the looking did not count.

"Sit," Asha said.

The candidate sat. She was young, though not so young as the rawest of them, perhaps twenty-six, with the carriage of someone who had been a soldier and the steadiness of someone who had been good at it. Her file lay open in front of Asha, the standard-issue intake ledger with its ruled columns and its space at the foot of the page where the assessing officer entered the disposition in a hand the candidate would never see. Asha had read the file the night before and again that morning. Maryam Sow, born in the river prefectures, eight years in the Fourth Imperial Cohort, two commendations she had not asked anyone to mention and a third she had declined. A clean record. A recommendation from a cohort commander whose judgment Asha respected and whose handwriting cramped at the end of the page where he had run out of room for the things he wanted to say about her. Technically, on the page, the woman was past qualified.

"You know what we are," Asha said. It was not a question, and she did not pose it as one. She watched the candidate decide whether it was.

"I know what people say you are, Officer."

"Then tell me that. I will correct the parts that are wrong."

Maryam Sow took a breath that was a degree too careful, and gave it back. "That you guard the Empress. The senior house. That the Lionesses are the throne's own hand, and that when the regular guard cannot be trusted with a thing, it comes to you."

"That is the story they tell in the cohorts," Asha said. "It is flattering and it is most of it false." She turned a page of the file she did not need to turn, to give the woman a moment to settle into being wrong. "We do stand protective detail. It is perhaps a fifth of what the Quarter does, and the least of it. The rest you would find dull. We read appointment rosters out of prefectures you have never heard of, line by line, until the line that is wrong stands up off the page. We sit in the back of commission hearings and write down who arrived together and who left apart. We hold the records that the bureaus would rather no single office held all at once, and we cross them against one another, and when two of them disagree we find out why before anyone with a reason to lie has the chance to reconcile them. We are an instrument of oversight that answers above the military and below the throne, and most days the most dangerous thing in this building is a discrepancy of dates in a ledger." She let that sit. "If you came here to guard the Empress with your body, the cohorts have men enough for that, and they will let you keep your commendations. We are looking for something else."

The candidate held still under it. That was good; the ones who flinched at being told the work was paperwork were no use, and the ones who brightened at it were sometimes worse. Maryam Sow did neither. She absorbed the correction and adjusted, the way a competent soldier adjusts to a change in the order of march, and when she spoke again her voice had reset to the new fact.

"Then I came for the something else, Officer."

"We will find out what you came for," Asha said. "That is what the morning is."

She began the way she always began, with the parts that had answers. There was a structure to the morning that the candidate could not see and that Asha had built and rebuilt across four decades until it ran without her having to think where it went next. The first part was easy on its face and was meant to be, a set of problems with clean solutions, so that the candidate would settle and stop managing her face and start showing Asha how she thought when she was not watching herself think. The hard part came after, when the candidate believed the morning had relaxed. The cohorts ran their assessments the other way around, the difficulty front-loaded so that the recruit broke early or held, and that suited the cohorts, who were sorting for men who would hold. The Quarter was not sorting for that. The Quarter could find people who would hold in any barracks in the empire. What it could not easily find, and what no examination on a page would surface, was the thing Asha was listening for now, and so she put the easy problems first and waited.

She put situations to the candidate the way the cohort exams put them, a protected individual at a transit point, a contradiction in an advance schedule, a subordinate whose account of a movement did not match the manifest, and she listened less to the answers than to the shape of how the woman reached them. Maryam Sow was quick. She did not guess; she said what she could establish and marked the edge of it where she could not, which was rarer than the cohorts believed and harder to teach than anything else the Quarter taught. On the question of the mismatched manifest she found the discrepancy and then, unprompted, named the two innocent explanations before the guilty one, and Asha felt the small clean satisfaction of watching a mind work the way the work required. By the standard of the page the woman was already through. By the standard of the page she had been through before she walked in.

Asha did not assess by the page.

She had told the truth to a great many candidates over forty years, that she knew within the first ten minutes, and most of them had taken it for the kind of thing an old officer says to seem formidable. It was not. The first ten minutes were before the candidate had learned what the room rewarded; everything after was performance, and performance could be coached, and a coached answer told her about the coach. So she watched the early minutes, and in the early minutes Maryam Sow had done one thing.

She had wanted to be right.

That was the thing, and it had shown itself in the first ten minutes the way such things always showed themselves, before the candidate had learned what the room rewarded. Asha had watched a thousand of them sit in that chair, and she had built her whole reading of a person on the early minutes for a reason she could have explained to no cohort commander in the empire and did not try to. A person walked into the Quarter carrying the habits the cohorts had paid them to keep, and the cohorts paid for one set of habits and the Quarter needed another, and the place where the two parted was not in competence. Maryam Sow was competent past the line. It was in what the competence served.

Not to get it right. The two were close enough that the cohorts never separated them, and a cohort had no reason to; a soldier who needs to be right and a soldier who needs to get it right will both work hard and both do well, and the need is fuel either way. But the Quarter was not a cohort, and the difference, which cost nothing on a good day, cost everything on a bad one. When Asha had handed her the mismatched manifest the candidate's first motion had been a small straightening, a readying, the set of a person who has been given a chance to demonstrate. When she had named the two innocent explanations she had named them well, and she had named them fast, and under the speed had been the faint pressure of a person clearing the obvious so she could arrive at the answer that would land. She had not been turning the problem over to see what it was. She had been getting to the place where she could be seen to have solved it. It was a hairsbreadth thing. On the page it did not show at all.

Asha set down the file.

"I am going to put one more to you," she said, "and I want you to take your time. There is no clock on this one." She watched the candidate not relax, because the absence of a clock was itself a pressure to a certain kind of mind, and Maryam Sow's mind was that kind. "You are running an analysis on a cluster of appointments out of an Outer Prefecture. Forty confirmations. You have been at it three weeks. Your assessment, when you file it, will go up the chain and shape what the Quarter does next, and the officer above you has made it known, not in words, that she expects you to find the irregularity, because the whole reason the file came to you is that someone senior already smells one." She kept her voice level, an exercise and nothing more. "You finish your review. The forty confirmations are clean. There is no irregularity. What do you file?"

"I file that they are clean," the candidate said.

She said it well. She said it almost at once, and that was the trouble; Asha had built the question to have a right answer that any honest person would reach, and an honest person would reach it after sitting in the discomfort of disappointing the officer above her, and would file the clean finding with the small private cost of having found nothing. Maryam Sow had reached the right answer with no cost at all, because she had heard the question as a test of her integrity and had given the answer the test wanted. She had performed the incorruptible analyst the way she would have performed the loyal soldier, the way she had reached for the answer that would land. The content was right. The thing under it was the same thing it had been in the first ten minutes. She would file the clean finding when filing it cost nothing. Asha had spent forty years learning what such a person filed when the truth was clean and inconvenient and no one would ever check, when finding nothing meant three weeks wasted and a senior officer disappointed and the quiet suspicion that a sharper analyst would have found the thing that was not there. The candidate did not know what she would file on that day. That was the disqualification. Not a flaw of character. A flaw the work would find before she did.

Across the room Meret had not moved. But she had heard the question, and she had heard the answer, and when Asha glanced at her, once, in the pause after, Meret met her eyes and held them for the length of a slow breath and then looked down at the folder on her knee. They had sat enough of these together. Asha did not need her to say it and Meret would not have said it in front of the candidate in any case. The look was the whole of the exchange.

"That is a good answer," Asha said to Maryam Sow, and meant it, because it was. "Thank you."

She asked four more questions after that, none of them assessments, all of them courtesies, the kind a person deserved who had served eight years and earned commendations she did not boast of and come into a strange building and answered honestly everything she was asked. Asha asked after the river prefectures, which she knew, and after the cohort commander, whose hand she had recognized, and she let the candidate talk, and she watched the woman ease into the talking now that the testing seemed to be behind her, not knowing it had ended ten minutes into the morning and that everything since had been Asha making sure. She was sure. The certainty was the same weight it always was, and it did not get lighter with practice, and Asha had stopped expecting it to.

"You will hear from the Quarter within the month," she said, when the courtesies were done. "However it falls, you served well to be sitting in that chair, and the cohort is the better for you. That is not a thing I say to fill a silence."

The candidate rose. She had the grace not to ask, though Asha could see the asking move behind her face and then settle, a soldier accepting that the result was not hers to be told in the room. She inclined her head to Asha and then, after a beat, to Meret, who returned it, and she let herself out, and the oiled door gave back nothing as it closed. Her boots went down the administrative corridor, even and unhurried on the stone, and Asha listened to them go and could hear, under them and behind them, the other sound of the building, the harder report of boots on the training floor two doors down where a class was working through a protective drill, the heavier rhythm of it carrying through the wall, so that for a moment the corridor held both, the candidate leaving at a walk and the work going on without her.

Meret stood and brought the folder to the table and set it down by Asha's hand and did not open it.

"Silva sent the periphery summaries up," Meret said. "They are in there. There is one she flagged." She did not say what, because Asha would read it, and Meret did not waste a sentence telling a superior the contents of a folder she was about to hand her. "She is back out by the noon transit. She said to tell you she will have more by the week's end and that you would know what she meant."

"I will," Asha said.

Meret went. The door gave back nothing again. Asha sat alone in the plain room with the smell of the kitchens still threading up through the floor and the drill going on behind the wall, and she drew the intake ledger back in front of her and turned it to the foot of Maryam Sow's page, to the ruled box where the disposition went. She uncapped the pen. She did not write a paragraph; the box did not hold one, and the Quarter had learned long ago that a long disposition was an officer arguing with herself on the page. She held the pen a moment over the box, and then she set it down to the paper and made a single notation in the cramped exact hand the candidate would never read, and capped the pen, and closed the ledger over it.

# Chapter 3 — What Eleanor Keeps

The west window let the late light in low and long, the way it always did at this hour, laying a bar of pale gold across the floor of the office and up the leg of the writing table. Eleanor De Grosvenor did not look at the light. She had chosen this room sixty years ago for the way that window faced, then learned to work with her back half to it so the glare would not fall on the page, and the courtyard below it she had not walked through in a decade. The glass showed her a square of paving, a dead fountain, a bench nobody used. She kept the window for the light it gave the room and for nothing else.

The room held what work required and refused everything else. A long table scarred from generations of inkwells. Two chairs for visitors, one of which had not been sat in for years. Behind her, shelving that ran the wall to the ceiling, the upper registers reachable only by a ladder she no longer trusted her hip to climb, so the documents she needed daily had migrated downward over time, a slow tide of paper finding its level. On the remaining walls, maps. The Outer Prefectures in one frame, hand-corrected in three colors of ink as borders and jurisdictions shifted under successive bureaus. New Terra and its administrative wards in another. A third map showed nothing a stranger could read, only a grid of cells she had drawn herself, each marked with a code, each code a person or a channel or a debt.

In front of her sat the correspondence of the day, sorted into three stacks by a hand that was not hers and then resorted by a hand that was.

She worked through the first stack without hurry. A petition from the Bureau of Establishments requesting her endorsement of a procedural revision; she read it twice, found the sentence that mattered buried in the fourth clause, and set it aside to refuse. A note from a provincial administrator who wanted her to know he had named his third son for her late husband, which told her the man was either sincere or beginning to angle for something, and she would learn which by what arrived next from his office. A summary of grain reserves she had not asked for and did not need, sent by someone hoping she would remember he existed. She remembered. That had never been her difficulty.

The cold tea sat at her elbow where the morning had left it, a skin forming on its surface. She drank a mouthful out of habit and did not taste it past the bitterness, set the cup down a finger's width from where it had been, and reached for the second stack.

The second stack was the one that mattered, and she had taught herself young to leave it for the middle of the day, when the morning's edge had worn off her judgment and the evening's tiredness had not yet found it. The first stack was theater, the things people sent so that a record would show they had sent them. The second was the things people sent without meaning to send them, the letters where the writer believed he was reporting one matter and was in fact reporting himself. She read those slowly. A request for a transfer told her who a man had quarreled with. A report filed three days late told her the office it came from had something it preferred to file in a hurry behind it. The art she had spent her life on was the art of the margin, the part of a document the writer had not thought to guard because he did not know it was speaking.

The letter from Kaviel was near the bottom, which was where it would be. Her daughter wrote to her through the same dispatch chain any senior Lioness used, on the same gray paper, in the same fixed hand they were all trained toward so that no document could be read off its penmanship. The letter concerned a verification Kaviel had completed at a peripheral waystation, a protected individual confirmed safe and moved on to a secondary location. It reported the operation closed and clean. It described the difficulty Kaviel had encountered in plain operational language, the advance record wrong, the individual not where it said, a relocation managed quietly and without incident. It did not ask for anything. It did not say more than the work needed it to say.

Eleanor read it the way she read everything, once for the words and once for what the words were built around. There was nothing personal in it, which was correct, and she felt the correctness of that the way one feels a joint that has set well after a break, the ache that means the bone knit straight. She had taught the woman to write like this. She had taught her to close an operation in four sentences and waste no fifth on reassurance. Somewhere in the years between the child who could not sleep without a lamp and the officer who reported a relocation as if reporting weather, Eleanor had done that, deliberately, with her eyes open, and she did not let herself look away from it now any more than she had let herself look away from it then.

She wrote three words on the margin, an acknowledgment that the dispatch office would log and file, and that was the whole of her reply. She set the letter on the stack of completed business and reached past it to the third stack, where the report had sat for eleven days.

It was in its case, a flat hinged box of black document leather worn pale along the spine, and she had moved it from the table to the drawer and back to the table more times in eleven days than she had moved anything in months. She did not open it now. She knew what it said. She had read it on the first day until she could recite the figures, and the figures had not changed since, and would not.

She heard the footsteps in the corridor before the knock, knew them, and said come before the knuckles touched the wood.

Sefu Adebayo had served her for thirty-one years and had learned in the first of those that she did not want to be greeted. He came in, closed the door against the draft, and stood until she gestured him to the chair that still saw use. He was a narrow man gone gray at the temple, exact in his dress without vanity, carrying a slim folder he set on the table squared to its edge. He waited.

She had hired him out of the Bureau of Establishments when he was younger than Kaviel was now, hired him for a quality she had recognized at once and had never found a clean word for, a kind of refusal to be impressed. He could read a roster of a thousand names and tell her which three had been added in a different season of ink. He did not gossip, did not speculate past his evidence, did not bring her a conclusion when a fact would do. In thirty-one years he had been wrong in front of her four times, and each time he had come to her himself to say so before she found it, which was the reason she had stopped checking his work after the first decade and the reason she trusted nothing he could not show her. He was the nearest thing the apparatus had to a second mind, and he knew it, and it had never once made him careless.

"The reconciliation schedules," Eleanor said.

"All four prefectures, as you asked." He opened the folder and turned it so it faced her. "Sennar is the one that will hold your attention."

"It already has." She drew the folder closer and read down the column. Confirmations entered under carry-forward, each tagged to the authority of a commission session, each session dated, each appointment dated. The arithmetic was meant to be tedious. Tedium was the cover the whole arrangement wore. She had spent six decades learning to read the places where a document worked hardest to be boring, because that labor was a tell, the bureaucratic equivalent of a man holding very still. "These three." She set a fingernail beside three lines. "The confirmation predates the session that authorized it. Here, here. And this one by nine days."

Sefu leaned and looked. He did not say that he had found the same three. He had marked them in faint pencil at the foot of the page, and she saw the marks, and they were the same three, and neither of them remarked on it because the agreement that they would not waste words on what was obvious between them was older than most marriages.

"The clause permits it on its face," he said instead. "Carry-forward exists to reconcile a backlog. An appointment confirmed under a prior commission, entered late under the current one. On paper there is no irregularity. The dates only fail if you check them against the session record, and the session record sits in a different bureau, and no one checks one against the other because no one has ever needed to."

"Someone built it knowing that."

"Someone built it knowing that," he agreed.

She sat back. The light had moved while they spoke, the gold bar climbing the table leg and onto the wood, reaching now toward the black case where it lay closed. She watched it come without watching it, the way she watched most things.

"The field report says the same in fewer words," she said. "A cluster of confirmations in one peripheral jurisdiction, all of them tracing to carry-forward entries, all of them recent. The report does not name the clause. The man who wrote it did not have the schedules to see what the clause was for. He saw the pattern and flagged it, and he was right to, and he has no idea how right." She paused. "If I release the report now, the Lionesses have a thread inside a week. Kaviel will pull it. She works documents. She will have the shape of this faster than anyone I could put on it, and she will not need to be told where to look, which is its own protection, because nothing in the order will point at how I came to suspect it."

"And the report's source."

"Yes." She did not move her eyes from the case. "And the report's source."

He waited. This was the part he could not carry for her, and they both knew the shape of it, and he did not pretend to weigh it on her behalf.

The source had fed the apparatus for twenty-one years. Not directly, never directly; through three intermediaries and a channel she had built so that no single link in it knew more than one other, and the man himself believed, as far as she could measure belief at that distance, that his material went nowhere and meant nothing and was read by a clerk and filed. He was a senior administrative figure with access to the bureaus where the schedules lived. He was the reason she had the schedules now. He was, in the architecture of twenty-one years, irreplaceable, and the report in the black case had come up through his channel, and it was unusual enough in its content that a careful reader on the other side, asking who had access to flag exactly this, exactly now, would draw a short list, and his name would be on it.

Release the report and the thread opened and the threat could be met while it was still small. Hold the report and the source stayed dark and the thread stayed closed and the thing on the other end of it kept growing in the dark with him.

She had made this calculation before, in other rooms, with other names in the place where his now sat. The arithmetic never changed and never got easier, because the variables refused to hold still long enough to be solved. A source was worth what he could tell you tomorrow, and you could not know tomorrow's worth today, and so you guessed, and the guess was the work. She had guessed wrong twice in her life in ways that had cost her people, and she carried the names of those two the way she carried the key in the dish, in plain view of herself, where she could look at them whenever she was tempted to think she had grown infallible with age. She had not grown infallible. She had grown only patient, and patience was a poor substitute for being right, but it was the one she had.

"There is a thing you are not saying," she said.

"There is a thing I am letting you reach," Sefu answered, which was the same thing said honestly.

"Eleven days," Sefu said. It was not a reproach. He was the only person living who could have said it without it being one. He was telling her she had let the figure climb in that drawer for eleven days, and that he had watched her do it, and that he understood why, and that he would say nothing further about the why.

"The threat does not wait for me to feel ready," she said. "I know what it costs to move now. I have not yet decided it costs more than waiting." She closed the folder of schedules and squared it to the edge as he had left it. "What does the source's traffic look like this month."

"Ordinary. He sent the report through the usual interval, nothing irregular before or since. If we act on it slowly, with other matter around it, the path back to him stays long."

"With other matter around it." She turned the phrase over. "We would need the other matter to be real. A general review of reconciliation across all four prefectures, ordered for an ordinary reason, so the report becomes one input among a dozen and not the input. That takes weeks to stand up. Weeks are what I do not have to spend if the report is right."

"And if you stand up the review without the report, to have it ready."

She looked at him then. It was the first useful thing either of them had said in a quarter of an hour, and he had said it the way he said all his useful things, sideways, as a question he already half knew the answer to.

"Then the review exists before I move," she said slowly, "and when the report joins it the report is unremarkable, and the source is buried in the noise of a process I began for my own reasons, on paper, weeks earlier." She was quiet a moment. "It costs time. The threat grows during the time. But it grows slowly, and the source survives the moving, and a source who survives is worth more over a year than a week of advantage now."

"It is your judgment."

"It is my judgment." She did not thank him. She had stopped thanking him for the thing he did best, which was to lay the choice out clean and then stand clear of it, twenty years ago, when he asked her once to stop, and she had honored it since. "Order the review. Routine language, my authority, the ordinary reason. Sennar last in the list, not first; if it leads, someone reads the list as pointing at it. Bury it third."

He wrote nothing down. He would remember it precisely, and the absence of a written instruction was its own discretion. He rose, gathered the folder, and squared his chair to the table without being asked.

"There is one more thing in the day's correspondence you have not reached," he said. "It is from the office of a councillor. It is flattery, and it is well done, and that is what I wanted you to see in it. The flattery is too well made for the man it comes from. Someone is writing his letters for him now."

"Leave it on top," she said. "I will read who is writing it."

"Eleanor." He stood at the door with his hand on it. He used her name perhaps twice a year, and always to mark a thing he judged she might not be marking herself. "You held it eleven days because the man on the other end is worth holding for. That is not a fault in the work. It is the work."

She said nothing to that. He had not asked for an answer, and giving one would have made it larger than it was. He opened the door, and the draft came in cool and smelling faintly of the corridor's stone and old wax, and his footsteps went away down the passage at the even pace they had kept for thirty-one years, growing fainter, gone.

The light had reached the case. It lay across the worn black leather and warmed it to brown, and the small brass clasp threw a thread of brightness onto the table.

Eleanor sat with it a while. She did not open it again. She knew the figures; opening it would only let her read them once more and pretend the reading was deciding, when the deciding was done. The review would go up under her name in routine words. The report would wait a little longer in its case, joined later to a process built to hide where it came from, and the man twenty-one years in the dark would stay in the dark and stay alive in it, and the thing on the far end of the thread would grow some measured amount in the weeks that bought him, and she had weighed the one against the other with her eyes open, as she had weighed every loss she had ever chosen to take.

She drew the case toward her, set both hands flat on its lid for a moment, the leather cool under palms that had held thousands of such cases over six decades and had learned the exact weight at which a document stopped being information and became a debt. Then she lifted it, opened the drawer at her right knee, and laid the case inside on its bed of felt. She closed the drawer. She turned the small key in the lock and drew it out and set it in the dish where she always set it, in plain view, where anyone might take it and no one ever would.

The bar of gold had slid off the table and onto the floor again and was thinning toward the window as the sun dropped behind the wing. Eleanor reached past the locked drawer for the councillor's letter Sefu had left on top, turned it to the failing light, and began to read whose hand was hiding inside another man's name.

# Chapter 4 — Kaviel

Sabra Crossing had been built for movement, and it punished anyone who stood still. Kaviel had learned that on her first posting and never unlearned it, so she walked. She kept to the third concourse, where the light came down from panels set into the ceiling at intervals close enough that nothing ever cast a true shadow, and the air carried the burnt-oil weight of engine exhaust that the ventilation never cleared, only stirred. Around her the crowd moved with the rhythm of people who knew where they were going and how long they had to get there. That rhythm was the thing she read. A body that broke it, that slowed or doubled back or held a doorway too long, told her more than any face.

The advance record said the protected individual would arrive on the eastern transit at the seventeenth hour and present at the rebooking counter under a travel name, and that Kaviel would confirm the arrival, observe the onward boarding, and file the movement closed. Routine. She had run the same shape of operation forty times and could have written the report before leaving the compound. The report was already half-written in her head, in the flat administrative voice she used for these things, the voice that gave nothing away and recorded only what could be defended later.

The seventeenth hour came. The eastern transit unloaded its passengers in a long exhalation of bodies and luggage and the smell of food carried in cloth, and the protected individual was not among them.

Kaviel did not stop walking.

She let the disappointment register and pass, the way she let everything register and pass, and she turned the fact over. A missed transit was not yet a problem. People missed transits. The record could be wrong; advance records were compiled by people, and people were the weakest part of any system. She walked the length of the concourse to the rebooking counter and stood at the periphery, near a vendor stall where a woman was turning skewers over coals, the fat catching and snapping, and she watched the counter for the eleven minutes it took to be sure the travel name was not going to present itself.

Silva found her there. Silva had been working the upper gallery, where the rail looked down over all three concourses, and she came down the stair without hurry and stopped at Kaviel's shoulder, close enough to speak under the noise.

"Eastern transit ran on time," Silva said. "I had the platform the whole way. Nobody matching presented at the gate."

"The record says eastern."

"The record can say what it likes."

Kaviel let that sit. Silva did not offer theories, which was one of the reasons Kaviel had requested her. Silva observed and reported and left the meaning to whoever was paid to assign it. The meaning, in this case, fell to Kaviel.

"Pull the arrivals board," Kaviel said. "Everything inbound this jurisdiction since the fifteenth hour. I want origins, not destinations."

Silva went. Kaviel stayed with the vendor and the coals and let her eyes do the slow work along the counter line, the queue, the benches where people waited with the worn patience of those who had waited often. She bought a skewer she did not want, because a woman buying food was a woman with a reason to stand where she stood, and she held it and let it cool in her hand and thought about the advance record.

Advance records were assembled from booking data, and booking data could be entered wrong, or the protected individual could have been moved up a transit by a nervous handler, or the booking could have been made under a name nobody had passed to Kaviel because somebody upstream had decided she did not need it. The first was carelessness. The second was reasonable. The third was the one that made the back of her neck cool, and she set it aside because she had no evidence for it and inference that ran ahead of evidence was how operations went wrong.

She knew this work. That was the trouble Kaviel carried into every concourse like a stone in a coat pocket. She was good at it in a way that had nothing to do with wanting to be, good the way her mother was good, and the knowledge that the talent had come down the same channel as the name made it heavier to use. She read crowds because reading crowds was in her hands the way a craft sits in the hands of someone raised to it. She had not chosen the craft. She had chosen, at twenty-two, only to stop pretending she could do anything else.

There was a version of this work that other operatives performed, one she had watched and never managed to imitate. They ran a movement as a problem to be closed and went home empty, the file shut, the day done. For them the protected individual was a parcel with a heartbeat, a thing routed from one safe point to another, and there was no shame in that; the parcels arrived safe, the reports stood up, the system held. Kaviel closed her files as cleanly as any of them and could not make herself empty afterward. She carried the faces. She had a memory built for it, the same memory her mother had, the one that filed origins and arrival times and the angle of a stranger's attention without being asked, and the same memory would not let her set a face down once she had picked it up. She had moved a man through a crossing like this one four years ago and could still describe the way his hands shook on the rail and the routing she had written for him and the gate he had vanished through, and she had no use for any of it. The system did not require her to remember. She remembered anyway. It was the cost of being suited to the thing, and she had stopped expecting it to come due less often.

She thought, standing in the yellow light, of the last time she had stood in the Empress's presence on business of her own. It had been a briefing, brief and exact, Amira reading the movement summary Kaviel had filed and asking two questions, both of them the right questions, neither of them warm. Amira had thanked her by name, De Grosvenor, the name doing its work in the room the way it always did, and Kaviel had stood there carrying it and answered as the officer she was and not as the daughter the name announced. The Empress treated her as competent, which was the only currency Kaviel trusted, and the Empress was careful with her in the way one is careful with a person whose house one cannot afford to misjudge. That care was not friendship. Kaviel preferred it to friendship. Friendship asked things she did not know how to give. Competence she could deliver to the last decimal, and competence was what the room beyond the engine wash and the boarding gates was asking of her now.

Silva returned. "Three inbound transits since fifteenth hour. Eastern, which we had. A southern feeder out of the prefecture line, came in at the sixteenth hour, off the main board because it terminates here. And a re-route, northern stock, posted late, arrived sixteen-forty."

"The southern feeder. Where does it originate?"

"Sennar." Silva said it without weight, because to Silva it was a place name. To Kaviel it landed differently, though she could not yet have said why; only that the word had been crossing administrative desks lately in connection with appointments and magistrates, and her memory filed things whether she asked it to or not. She put it in the same drawer as the missing name and closed the drawer.

"A feeder that terminates here doesn't post to the onward board," Kaviel said. "Anyone arriving on it who needed to rebook would have to come to a counter on foot. They wouldn't appear in the transit arrivals at all. They'd appear in the rebooking queue."

"Which we're watching."

"Which we're watching for a name that may not be the name they used."

Silva absorbed this. "Then we're watching the wrong thing."

"We're watching one thing. We need the other." Kaviel set the cold skewer down on the edge of the vendor's stall. "Describe what we have. Not the name. The person."

It was a thin description, the kind that came down through three handlers and lost detail at every pass. A woman, middling height, traveling alone, would be carrying a document case of a kind issued by a peripheral records office, would not want to be looked at and would therefore have practiced not being looked at, which to a trained eye was its own signal. Kaviel had moved enough frightened people to know how the practiced ones held themselves: too still, or too busy, the stillness of someone counting exits.

The advance brief had given her a name and a transit and an hour, and two of the three were already wrong, and Kaviel turned that over again without letting it become a theory. A brief was a tool, and a tool that came back wrong told you something about the hand that had made it. Either the hand was careless or the hand was working from bad material or the hand had been given bad material on purpose, and she had no way yet to tell which, and so she did the thing the work required when the paper failed, which was to stop trusting the paper and start trusting the floor. The floor did not lie. People on a floor did what frightened people did, what bored people did, what waiting people did, and they did it in patterns older than any record, and if she read the patterns instead of the brief she would find the woman whether the brief had told the truth or not.

"What did the handler chain look like," she said. "On the advance."

"Three names before it reached us," Silva said. "I didn't pull the third. It was a peripheral office, the routing came through a relay I didn't recognize."

"Don't pull it now. Note that you didn't recognize it." Kaviel kept her eyes on the queue while she said it. A relay Silva did not recognize was a thread, and threads were for later, for a desk and a lamp and a file, not for a crowded concourse where pulling on one might tighten something around the woman they had come to move. "We close the movement first. The relay keeps."

"The southern feeder terminus is the second concourse," Silva said. "If she came in on it and nobody met her, she's had ninety minutes to learn she's alone."

"Then she's done one of two things." Kaviel was already moving, and Silva fell in a half step behind and to the left, the position that let her watch what Kaviel's back could not. "She's found a corner with sightlines and stayed in it. Or she's tried to solve the problem herself and is in the rebooking queue under whatever name she could buy."

They took the second concourse first, because the queue was the place a person went to act, and frightened people who had been trained at all preferred acting to waiting. The rebooking hall down here was older, the ceiling lower, the light yellower and humming faintly in a register Kaviel felt behind her eyes more than heard. The queue ran four deep along a rope line worn smooth by hands. She walked it slowly, a traveler reading the board, and she went through the faces the way she went through a roster, discarding most at a glance, holding a few for a second pass.

The woman was not in the queue.

Kaviel made the full length of it, turned, and made it again from the other side so that no one she had passed once saw her pass twice from the same angle. Nothing. She stepped out of the flow and stood by a pillar where the light pooled and let her gaze go soft and wide, taking the whole hall as a field rather than a set of individuals, which was how the breaks in rhythm showed themselves.

There. Not the woman. A man.

He was standing at the far end of the hall near the document desk, and he had been standing there too long, and he was doing it well, with a folded transit slip in his hand that he consulted now and then the way a man consults a slip while he waits for someone. But he was not waiting the way the others waited. The others waited toward the counters, toward the thing they wanted. He waited toward the hall, his attention turned outward across the floor, and twice in the time Kaviel watched him his eyes went to the same bench against the side wall and came back. He had a courier's satchel across his body, the flat hard-cornered kind that carried documents and nothing else, and a face that would not survive a description, even features over an unremarkable build, the sort of face a witness would later swear they could not recall and would be telling the truth.

Kaviel did not look at the bench. She looked at the board, and at the vendor, and at the floor, and she let the bench arrive at the edge of her vision on its own.

A woman sat at the end of it. Middling height, document case on her knees held with both hands, very still. Too still. Counting exits.

"Side wall, the long bench," Kaviel said, low. "Far end. Case on her lap."

"I have her," Silva said. "There's a man by the document desk who keeps finding her."

"I know."

"Is he ours?"

"No." Kaviel watched the man consult his slip again. He was not advancing on the woman. He was not signaling her. He had the loose attention of a person noting something for later, a person whose work was to see and to carry what he saw somewhere else, and that was all Kaviel could read in him, and she filed it without a frame because a frame was more than the evidence would hold. A man waited near a bench at a transit crossing. People did that. She let him be a face in the crowd, which was what he was, and she moved her attention back to the only thing that was hers to solve, which was getting the woman off that bench and onto a transit without either of them ever appearing to have met.

There was a decision in it that she made in the space of a breath and would have defended to any board that asked. She could move on the man. She could have Silva mark him, follow him, learn the satchel's contents and the relay he answered to, and there was a version of the night where that was the right call and the woman was bait set on a bench to draw exactly that response. But the woman was real and frightened and hers, and a protected individual exposed in a crowded hall did not get more protected while her escort chased a face she could not even describe. The brief was to move the woman. So she would move the woman, and the man would go on being a fact she had no frame for, and she would carry his face the way she carried the others, into a file she might never open. She had learned the hard part of the work years ago, that the thing in front of you you were sent to do came before the thing beside it you were curious about, and that curiosity indulged at the wrong moment was how protected people stopped being protected.

The cleanest method was the oldest. Kaviel did not approach the woman. She approached the rebooking counter, joined the short queue at the adjacent desk, and while she stood there she took from her coat a transit voucher of the kind the compound issued for exactly this, blank but for the imperial chop, and she filled in an onward routing in the small precise hand she had been taught. Then she did the thing that frightened people responded to better than rescue: she became boring. She finished at the counter, turned, and walked toward the bench at the unhurried pace of a traveler whose business was done, and as she came level with the far end she sat, leaving a respectful gap between herself and the woman, and set her own case down, and exhaled the way a tired person exhales.

The woman's hands tightened on her case.

"They overbooked the northern re-route," Kaviel said, to the air, to no one, the complaint of a stranger sharing a bench. "Three hours I've lost. If you're waiting on the same routing, save yourself the queue. They're sending people round to the seventh gate." She set the voucher down on the bench between them, face up, the chop visible, and stood, and gathered her case. "Seventh gate," she said again, mildly, and walked away without looking back.

It was a small thing and it depended on training. An untrained person would have left the voucher or called after her, confused, or worse, would have stood and followed, fixing the two of them together in the memory of everyone on the bench line. A trained one understood a handoff when it sat down beside her and complained about a transit. The woman picked it up. Kaviel did not see her do it; Silva, from the gallery she had climbed back to without being told, saw it and said so through the bead in Kaviel's ear, one word. "Taken."

The relief of that word was a thing Kaviel did not let herself feel until it was already over. The handoff was the part that could not be redone. If the woman had panicked, if she had read the voucher as a trap and frozen, Kaviel would have had to go back, and a second approach to the same bench was the kind of pattern that turned a face in a crowd into a witness with something to tell. The woman had not panicked. She had been trained well enough by whatever peripheral office had sent her up the line that she knew how to be handed a future and not look at the hand that gave it. Kaviel filed that too. A frightened woman who took a cold handoff cleanly was a woman someone had taken trouble over, and trouble taken was a thing worth knowing about a person you were moving, though it was not, again, hers to chase tonight.

Kaviel walked the long way to the seventh gate, through the first concourse and back down, so that anyone tracing her path would trace a traveler killing three hours and not an operator running a line. She bought tea she did not drink, the paper cup hot through her glove, the smell of it sharp and overbrewed. By the time she reached the seventh gate the woman was already there, standing at the margin of the boarding crowd with the voucher in her hand and the document case held a fraction less tightly, and Kaviel passed within two strides of her and did not slow and did not look, and confirmed in that passing only that the woman was on her feet, in the right place, holding the right paper.

The northern re-route boarded in a press of bodies and luggage, the gate attendant calling routings in a flat voice over the noise, the engine wash beyond the glass throwing heat and the reek of fuel back into the hall. Kaviel stood at the pillar nearest the gate and watched the woman move into the crowd. She watched the document case go past the attendant. She watched the practiced stillness of the woman's shoulders ease by a degree she doubted the woman knew she carried, the small unclenching of a person who has stopped counting exits because she has found one. The crowd took her. The boarding line compressed and shuffled and closed, and the gap where the woman had stood filled with other travelers, other shoulders, other bodies each bound for somewhere of its own, and the woman was gone into them as completely as a stone into water.

Kaviel filed the movement closed in her head, the flat voice, the defensible record. Protected individual arrived southern feeder, originating Sennar, sixteenth hour. Located, verified, onward routed northern re-route, seventh gate. Movement closed.

She did not move from the pillar. Silva came down and stood with her and did not speak, which Kaviel valued, because the thing in her now was not a thing she had words for and would not have spoken if she had. The advance record had said eastern transit, and the woman had come in on a feeder out of Sennar that did not post to any board, and no one upstream had passed Kaviel that origin, and a man with a face she would never be able to describe had stood by the document desk finding the same bench with his eyes. None of it was evidence. All of it was inference running ahead of what she could prove, and she knew the difference and held to it.

She watched the place in the crowd where the woman had been. The bodies kept moving through it, indifferent, each of them bound for somewhere of its own, and under the engine noise and the yellow hum of the light Kaviel stood very still and wondered, for the first time in the whole operation, whether the woman she had just moved was the one she had been sent to move.

The assessors confirmed that each of the six appointments in question bore a prior confirmation and an attestation from its originating office. The registrar directed that the six be entered under the carry-forward reconciliation, citing in each entry the prior Commission's authority and the date of original confirmation rather than the date of present entry, as the provision requires. The motion carried. The clerk read back the disposition of all one hundred and sixteen appointments, and the record was found correct as read.

# Chapter 5 — The Old Agreement

The room had been chosen long before either of them was born, and both of them knew it. It sat between the Empress's working quarters and the corridor where her household slept, small enough that no third chair would have fit without crowding the table, and arranged so that whatever was said in it left no trace in the registers. There were two chairs. There was a low table with a tray, and on the tray two cups. A window looked out onto an interior garden whose path nobody had walked in a season, and neither woman so much as turned her head toward it.

Amira poured. The Empress of the Trinitarian Throne lifted the pot herself, with no servant in the room to do it, because the absence of servants was the point of the room. Steam rose off the cups and broke against the cold of the stone walls. The garden window let in no warmth at this hour; the heat went straight up out of the liquid and disappeared, and the cups were already cooling before either of them touched them.

Eleanor sat with her hands folded over the head of a cane she did not lean on. She had walked into the room without it earlier in her life. She had walked in with someone's arm later in her life. Now she walked in with the cane and used it for neither, holding it the way she held everything else, as an instrument kept ready in case it became required.

"You held a commission review eleven days," Amira said. It was not a question, and she had not phrased it as one. She set Eleanor's cup nearer to her, an inch, no more.

"The Bureau of Establishments holds a great many reviews," Eleanor said. "I read more of them than I act on."

"I did not ask which you read."

Eleanor took the cup. She wrapped both hands around it and did not drink. The warmth would last perhaps a minute against her palms in a room this cold, and she let it last.

The first move had been made and answered, and both of them recognized it as a single exchange rather than two. Amira had told Eleanor that she knew Eleanor was sitting on something. Eleanor had told Amira that she would not be drawn by a date count. Neither sentence had carried its meaning on its surface. The surfaces were courtesy. Underneath the courtesy two people who had spent years measuring each other were taking the measure again, because the measure changed, and a year-old reading of Eleanor De Grosvenor was a dangerous thing to govern by.

"There is an arrangement between your house and this throne," Amira said. "Older than my grandmother. Older than your tenure."

"There is."

"I have read the terms of it perhaps four times. Each time I find them written so that they can be honored without ever being comfortable."

"They were drafted by people who did not trust each other," Eleanor said, "and who needed each other regardless. Comfort was not among their aims."

Amira drank. She had a way of drinking that gave nothing, the cup raised and lowered without expression, and Eleanor watched it the way she watched all such things, filing the absence of information as a kind of information.

The compact was not a document Amira could amend by signature, and Eleanor was not a subordinate Amira could compel by command. That was the whole shape of it. The De Grosvenor house served the throne, and the service was real, and it had teeth and reach and six decades of accumulated obligation behind it. But it served on terms the throne had agreed to before the throne understood what it was agreeing to, and one of those terms was that the house decided what the throne was told, and when, and whether the telling left the house exposed. An Empress could ask. The asking was honored. The answer was the house's to time.

"I want the report," Amira said.

"I know."

"Then we understand each other."

"We understand the half of it that you have stated," Eleanor said. "There is another half. I will state it when you are ready to hear it as a condition rather than a request."

Amira set the cup down. The faint sound of porcelain meeting wood was the loudest thing the room had produced. Outside, beyond the unwatched garden, a bell somewhere in the administrative wing marked an hour neither of them counted, and faded, and left the stillness heavier than before.

"You taught me," Amira said, "in the second year, that a ruler who demands the contents of every report ends with reports written to be demanded. I have not forgotten it. I am not asking you to empty your drawers."

"No. You are asking me to empty one drawer."

"I am asking you to tell me whether what is in that drawer touches the throne."

Eleanor turned the cup a quarter in her hands. The tea had gone tepid against her palms; she could feel the heat leave it, the way she could feel most things leave, by degrees and without announcement.

"Everything touches the throne," she said. "That is not evasion. It is the condition of the work. The question is not whether. The question is how far back the touch reaches, and that I do not yet know with the precision I require before I bring it to this room."

"Precision," Amira repeated.

"If I bring you a shape, you will rule on the shape. You cannot help it. It is what you are for. And a ruling on a shape commits the throne before the throne knows what it has committed to. I have watched that mistake unmake better houses than mine."

There was the second half, or the edge of it, brought out far enough that Amira could see it had a body without yet seeing the body. Eleanor wanted something. She had wanted it from before she sat down, and she had let Amira open the conversation so that the wanting would arrive as the answer to a question rather than as a demand into silence. Amira understood the maneuver because she used it herself, in the throne room, with petitioners who did not know they were being maneuvered. It was strange to sit on the receiving side of it and recognize the technique and choose, for the length of this conversation, not to resent it.

"Say the condition," Amira said.

"Not yet."

"Eleanor."

The name, used plainly, was itself a move. Amira did not use it often. In the throne room she said De Grosvenor, or she said nothing and let a glance carry it. Here, with no scribe to record the familiarity and read into it, the given name was a pressure applied at a precise point, and Eleanor felt the point.

"When I give you the report," Eleanor said, "I will have spent something to give it. There is a person whose continuance depends on this matter being handled in a way that does not announce how I learned of it. I have protected that person for longer than you have governed. I will not trade them for the satisfaction of resolving your curiosity eleven days early."

"It is not curiosity."

"I know it is not. I said it to hear you deny it, and you denied it the way you deny things that are true. You are worried."

Amira did not answer that. She poured more into her own cup, though the first had not been finished, and the act of pouring filled the silence the way a person fills a silence when the alternative is conceding a point. The fresh liquid steamed and the steam died against the cold, and the smell of it, faint and bitter and herbal, hung a moment in the still air between them and then was gone.

"There is a matter from the Sennar Prefecture," Amira said. "A petitioner in court. A magistrate's ruling contested on procedure. I sat through it three weeks ago and signed off on the procedural finding and thought no more of it. You were behind the dais. You did not move the entire session, except once."

Eleanor's face did not change, which was its own confirmation, and Amira read it.

"You will not tell me what you saw," Amira said.

"I will tell you that I saw something. I will tell you that I am not yet certain it is one thing rather than three coincidences that resemble one thing. And I will tell you that if it is one thing, it is not confined to Sennar, and the work of confirming that is the work I am doing in the eleven days you object to."

"I did not object. I noted them."

"You noted them in a room with no scribe," Eleanor said. "That is an objection with the record removed. I have done the same to people I worked under. It does not offend me. It tells me you have begun to suspect that the throne's information is being shaped before it reaches you, and that you have begun to suspect it might be shaped by the very house you rely on to keep it clean."

The accusation that had not been made hung now fully in the room, named by the only person who could have named it without it becoming an incident. Amira had not said it. Eleanor had said it for her, and in saying it had drawn its teeth, because a suspicion spoken aloud by its object is harder to hold as a suspicion. It was a generous move and a defensive one at once, and Amira marked it as both.

"I rely on your house," Amira said, "because the alternatives are worse, and because in forty years it has not failed the throne in a way I could prove. That is not affection. You have never asked me for affection."

"No."

"You have asked me for trust, which is a colder thing and a more expensive one, and I have extended it on the understanding that it is conditional and that you would tell me when the conditions changed. Have they changed?"

Eleanor was quiet for a long moment. The cold of the room had reached her hands fully now; the cup gave back nothing. Somewhere past the window a gardener's tool had been left against the wall a season ago and had begun to rust, and the iron smell of it would have reached the room if either of them had opened the window, and neither of them ever would.

"They are changing," she said. "I cannot tell you yet whether they have changed, which is a different statement, and the difference is the eleven days."

"And the condition you are not yet ready to state."

"Is that when I tell you, you let me tell you in full and at once, and you do not act on the first third of it because the first third is the part that exposes my source and resolves nothing. I am asking you to receive a wound and not flinch until I have shown you the whole of the body. Most rulers cannot do that. They flinch at the first cut and the surgeon loses the patient."

Amira considered the cup in her hands rather than the woman across from her. To look at Eleanor while deciding was to give Eleanor the decision in advance; the old woman read faces the way Amira read petitions.

"You are asking me to surrender the timing of my own response," Amira said.

"I am asking you to lend it to me, briefly, the way the throne lent my house the timing of its reports two hundred years ago, in this room, for reasons that have not improved with age and have not gone away either."

"And if I will not lend it."

"Then you will have the report tomorrow," Eleanor said, "incomplete, and you will act on it because you cannot help acting, and you will be right about the small thing and blind to the large one, and the person I have protected for twenty-one years will be the cost of your being right early. I will give you the report either way. The compact requires it. The compact does not require that I give it well."

That was the heart of it laid bare, as bare as Eleanor would lay anything. The compact bound her to deliver. It did not bind her to deliver in good order, with the source shielded and the shape complete. The difference between a report flung onto the table to satisfy the letter and a report carried in whole to serve the throne was a difference of perhaps two weeks and one human life, and Eleanor was telling Amira the price of each, and letting Amira choose, and not pretending the choice was anything other than hard.

Amira did not answer at once. She crossed to the window she had been ignoring all evening and stood before it without seeing the garden, her back to Eleanor, her hands joined behind her at the small of her back in the posture she took on the dais when a petition ran long and she wished to consider it without a hundred faces reading her. The glass had fogged faintly where the cold of the pane met the warmer air of the room, and her breath added to it, and through the blur the dead path and the rusting tool and the unpruned hedge had no edges at all.

"My grandmother distrusted your house," she said to the glass. "She told me so. She said the De Grosvenors had served four reigns and ended three of the four better off than they began, and that no servant grows richer than the master and stays a servant. She said it the year before she died. She also said that she would sooner be blinded by your house than given true sight by any other, because your house at least knew the value of the eyes it took. I did not understand her then. I have come to."

"Your grandmother and I disagreed about a great many things," Eleanor said. "We did not disagree about that."

Amira turned from the window. "You knew her."

"I served under the woman who served under her. I was young. She frightened me, which I did not let her see, and she knew it anyway, which I did not let her see that I knew. We managed each other for nineteen years and never once said a true thing in a room with a scribe in it. The arrangement we are sitting inside, you and I, is the arrangement she and I made habitable. It was drafted by the dead. We are the ones who have to live in the house they built, and the roof leaks where they wanted windows."

It was the most Eleanor had volunteered in the conversation, and Amira understood it was not warmth. It was provision of context, the laying of one more fact on the table so that the trade between them could be priced correctly. Eleanor gave nothing without a reason, and the reason here was to remind the Empress that the timing she was being asked to lend had been lent before, in this room, by a harder woman than Amira, to a younger and more dangerous Eleanor, and that the throne had survived the lending.

"You are telling me it has worked before," Amira said.

"I am telling you it has held before. Those are not the same. It held because both parties understood the cost of breaking it and chose the cost of keeping it, which is always higher in the moment and lower across the years. Your grandmother chose to keep it on a night she wanted to break it more than I have ever wanted anything since. I was in the room. I watched her choose. I have never told anyone what she gave up to make that choice, and I will not tell you, because the keeping of it is part of what makes my house worth the compact you dislike."

Amira returned to her chair and sat. She had not meant to sit again; standing had been the closing posture, the signal that the meeting was ending, and to sit again was to reopen what she had begun to close. Eleanor noted the reversal and gave no sign of noting it. The tea on the tray had passed beyond tepid into the flat cold of liquid that has taken the temperature of the room, and the steam was long gone, and the smell of it had thinned to almost nothing.

"There is a thing I have not said," Amira said.

"There is. You have been deciding whether to say it for the length of this conversation. Say it or do not. I will not pull it out of you. I am too old to enjoy the work and you are too able to make it easy."

Amira looked at her hands. "If your house is the source of the shaping, I cannot use your house to find the shaper. I would be asking the hand that moved the record to tell me which fingers were dirty. I have lain awake on that. It is the worst version of the problem and I have no instrument for it that does not run through you."

There. The thing beneath the thing, the fear under the suspicion, brought into the open by the Empress herself rather than left for Eleanor to name a second time. It cost Amira to say it, and the cost showed only in the slowness of the saying, the way a person sets down something heavy with care because dropping it would be worse than carrying it.

Eleanor let it sit a moment before she answered, which was respect, or the form of respect that she had instead of the feeling.

"If my house were the source," she said, "I would not be sitting in this room asking you for two weeks. I would have given you the incomplete report on the first day, let you act on the small true thing, watched you congratulate yourself, and steered you past the large thing while you were busy being right. The fact that I am asking you to wait, that I am spending my standing to buy time I could have used against you, is the only assurance I can offer that I am not the danger. It is not proof. There is no proof you could verify without an apparatus you do not have. It is the behavior of a house that is not betraying you, offered in place of a guarantee that does not exist. You will have to weigh it as that."

"I have weighed it as that," Amira said. "It is why you have your two weeks and not your dismissal."

"Then we are agreed, and we have spent an hour saying so."

"We have spent an hour making certain the agreement would hold under weight," Amira said. "That is not the same as saying so. You taught me that as well, though I do not think you meant to."

A sound passed between them then that was almost, in Eleanor, the ghost of amusement, gone before it could be confirmed, leaving only the slight settling of the lines at the corner of her mouth into a fraction less severity than before. She lifted the cold cup, looked at it, and set it down again without drinking, having confirmed for herself only that it was cold.

Amira stood. She did it without haste, smoothing the front of her robe with one hand, and the movement returned the room to its proper order, the Empress on her feet and the head of a serving house seated, the familiar names set down and the titles taken up again.

"You will have your two weeks," Amira said. "I will not flinch. When you bring it, you bring it whole, and you bring it to this room, and there will be no scribe. After that the timing is mine again."

"After that the timing is yours," Eleanor agreed.

Amira moved toward the door that led to her quarters. With her hand near it she paused, not turning fully, her profile lit cold from the garden window she still did not look at.

"The thing you saw in the throne room," she said. "When you bring me the whole of it. I will want to know whether I should have seen it too."

"You will not like the answer."

"I rarely do."

Eleanor rose then, slowly, the cane taking weight for the first time since she had entered, and she set the untouched cup back on the tray with both hands so that it would not rattle, and she inclined her head the precise depth that the head of the De Grosvenor house inclined it to the Trinitarian Throne, and no further.

# Chapter 6 — The Report Arrives

The report came up through the standard chain a little after the tenth hour of the night, which was the hour Kaviel preferred for anything that would ask something of her. The compound had gone quiet by then. The training floors were dark, the day's intake assessments filed, the mess two levels down scrubbed and shuttered, and the building had begun the slow settling it did once the people in it stopped moving through it, the small reports of cooling stone and contracting metal that an unfamiliar ear would have taken for footsteps and that Kaviel had long since learned to hear as the place breathing. The night runner who brought the dispatch knew better than to knock hard. He set the gray case in the slot built into her door for exactly that purpose, and she heard the soft double tap of the flap, and the runner was gone before she rose to take it.

She had been at the desk since the eighth hour with the lamp turned low and the window cracked the width of two fingers. She thought better with something moving, had since she was a girl, and the office held its air too well; with the window shut the room grew close by midnight and her attention thickened with it. So she kept the gap, and the cold came in off the inner court in a thin steady draw, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone from the afternoon's rain and, under it, the lamp oil, and under that the cold remains of the meal she had not finished, a heel of bread and a smear of bean paste going hard on its dish at the corner of the blotter. She had meant to eat it hours ago. She had meant to do a number of things hours ago.

She brought the case to the desk and opened it.

She knew the hand on the cover sheet before she read a word of it. It was the routing hand of the central dispatch office, fixed and characterless, the same hand every senior file wore so that nothing could be read off its penmanship, and it told her only that the document had passed through Eleanor's authority and been released downward to the Lionesses for action. It did not say released by Eleanor. The chain did not work that way and was not meant to. A file came down with an authority stamped on it and a routing assigned, and the officer who received it worked the file and not the question of who had decided she should have it. Kaviel had been trained to that discipline by the woman whose authority sat on the cover sheet, and she did not waste the night's first clear hour wondering why the report had come now and not a month ago. The report had come now. That was the fact the work began from.

She read it once through without stopping, the way she read everything the first time, for the words and nothing else.

It was a field report out of a peripheral records jurisdiction, filed by an administrator whose name had been redacted to an office code before it reached her, which was ordinary for material of this grade. The body of it was short. The writer had noticed, in the course of routine work, a cluster of judicial and administrative appointment confirmations in his jurisdiction that shared a single trait: every one of them had been entered into the standing record under carry-forward reconciliation rather than fresh review, and every one was recent, and the density of them in a short span sat oddly against the writer's experience of how the clause was normally used. He did not name the clause as a fault. He did not name it at all in the language of suspicion. He set the pattern down, flagged it as worth a second eye, and left the meaning to whoever read above him. Kaviel recognized the restraint. It was the restraint of a man reporting one thing and aware he might be reporting another, and unwilling to put a frame around a fact he could not yet stand behind. She had written reports in that voice. She had stood at a transit counter not three weeks past and built a movement summary in that voice, the flat one that recorded only what could be defended later.

She read it a second time, slower, for what the words were built around.

The thing the report did not have was scope. The writer had his own jurisdiction and the confirmations that had crossed his own desk, and that was the whole of his window. He could see a cluster. He could not see whether the cluster was a local accident, a clerk in one office overusing a convenience, or the visible corner of a pattern that ran wider than any single desk could hold. He had no way to test it, because the test would have asked for records that lived in a bureau he had no business inside, and a careful administrator did not go pulling at records outside his remit on the strength of an unease. So he had done the one thing his position allowed. He had written it down and sent it up, and the sending up was the limit of what the report could do on its own.

The limit was the part that held Kaviel at the desk.

Eleanor would have read this differently. Kaviel knew that with the certainty of a person who had spent her childhood watching the reading done. Her mother worked from the living shape of things, from who fed whom and who owed whom and which channel had gone quiet in a month it should have spoken; she would have held this report against twenty years of names and traffic and known within an evening whose silence it confirmed and whose it broke. Kaviel did not have the network. She had never wanted it, had built herself sideways out of the house precisely so that she would not be the keeper of the cells and the codes and the debts. What she had instead was paper. She read documents the way her mother read people, and where her mother would have asked who, Kaviel asked which records, and both of them would arrive, and the difference between the routes was the whole of the difference between them.

She rose and went to the wall of reference shelving and took down the compound's standing index of active matters, and she carried it back to the lamp and ran her finger down the entries with the cold air moving at her wrist. She was not looking for the report. She was looking for the things the report touched without naming, the way a stone dropped in water touches the bank a stride off from where it fell.

Three matters lay in the index where her finger stopped.

She did not pause to ask herself whether she was entitled to pull them. She was a senior Lioness and the report had come to her for action and the action began with the records nearest it, and the entitlement was not a thing she spent thought on. She went to the door and put her head into the dark of the corridor, where the duty lamp burned at the far end above the night clerk's station, and she saw that the figure at the station was Meret, which was good, because Meret would bring her what she asked and would not ask her why.

"Meret."

Meret looked up from the ledger she kept the night log in. She was a square-built woman with a still face and a way of holding a pen that suggested she had never in her life made an unconsidered mark with one. "Senior."

"Three files from the central record." Kaviel gave her the index references in order, reading them off the open book against her forearm. "The Sennar Prefecture magistrate matter, the grievance heard in open court this season; the petitioner was an orchard-holder, the magistrate a man named Kebe. A transit manifest, Sabra Crossing, the southern feeder line, the movement closed three weeks back under my own filing. And the roster of a prior appointments commission, the session that ran before the one now sitting. Whatever the record room has on all three."

Meret wrote the three references in her own hand, read them back once, exactly, and closed the log. "It will take the better part of an hour. The night archivist will have to wake for the commission roster; it is held below the active line."

"The hour is fine. Wake him gently."

Meret rose. She did not ask what the three had to do with one another. She did not ask why a movement Kaviel had filed closed with her own hand was being pulled back up at the tenth hour of the night, or what a transit manifest had to say to a magistrate's commission, or whether Kaviel wanted anyone told. She gathered the lamp from the station and went, and her boots made the even unhurried sound down the administrative hallway that the building's boots made, nothing like the harder report they made on the training floors two levels off, and the sound thinned and turned the corner and was gone. Kaviel went back to her desk. The not-asking was not incuriosity. Meret was as sharp as anyone on the rolls and would have her own shape forming of why a senior wanted these three files at this hour, and she would keep it to herself and bring the files, because the discipline of the place was that you did not pull on a thread a superior was holding until the superior handed you the end of it. Kaviel had given that order to others. She knew exactly what it cost to obey it well, and Meret obeyed it better than most.

She used the hour. She read the field report a third time and made no notes, because notes this early would only fix a shape before the shape had earned its lines. She turned the lamp up a half-turn against the strain on her eyes and turned it back down when the brightness made the room feel smaller. She ate the cold bread without tasting it, because a body that had not eaten read worse than a body that had, and the work needed her reading at its best. The bean paste had gone to a paste of a different kind and she ate it anyway. Outside the cracked window the rain had stopped and the court below held its puddles up to whatever light reached down into it, and once a bird she could not name said something twice into the dark and fell silent.

Meret brought the files a little before the eleventh hour and set them on the desk squared to its edge, three folders of varying thickness, the commission roster the heaviest and bound in the dun board the deep archive used. "The archivist asks that the roster go back by the sixth hour; it is wanted for a reconciliation review that stands open under a senior authority." She did not say whose. "He flagged it. The review is recent."

"It can go back by the sixth." Kaviel heard the rest of it land and set it where she set things she could not yet use. A reconciliation review, recently stood up, holding the prior commission's roster as one of its inputs. She did not know whose authority kept it open and she did not let herself reach for the name, because the reach would be inference and the inference would run ahead of the page in front of her, and she had learned the hard way that an early name was a thing the mind then bent every later fact to fit. "Thank you, Meret."

Meret inclined her head a degree and went back to her log, and Kaviel was alone again with the lamp and the cold draw of the window and four documents.

She built it the way she built everything, by its parts, laid flat where she could see them at once.

She opened the Sennar matter first because it was the one with a face on it. Demba Tunde, orchard-holder, grievance against the ruling of the prefectural court, the easement lost to a neighbor, the costs assessed and then waived by the throne. The petition contested the judge and not the judgment, which was a weak ground in law and had been refused. She read down to the part she wanted, the commission entry that gave the magistrate his bench. Magister Iwan Kebe, confirmed to the Sennar prefectural bench by the Third Appointments Commission, seated under the Bureau of Establishments, entered under carry-forward reconciliation of a prior confirmation. She read the two dates the file gave, the carry-forward entry dated the eleventh of Threshing and the commission session it was entered under convened on the second of Reaping, and she set the months in their order in her head, Threshing then Reaping, five weeks between, and saw that the entry stood five weeks ahead of the session that was supposed to have authorized it. A confirmation entered before the thing that confirmed it.

She had seen this date in open court. She had stood against the western wall and heard Obasi read it and watched it go past the whole hall, and she had let it lie because taken on its own it was the ordinary disorder of a registry, a clerk transposing two months out of three hundred, and because her mother had not let it lie, and the gap between those two facts was the thing she carried out of the throne room and had not set down since. She knew now why it would not set down. One wrong date was a clerk's long afternoon. A clerk's long afternoon did not call to a field report out of a different jurisdiction flagging a cluster of carry-forward confirmations that should not cluster. The report gave the throne room date a second instance to stand beside, and two instances were not a clerk; two instances were a method.

She opened the prior commission roster next, the heavy dun folder, and worked it against the report and the Sennar entry both. This was the test. The carry-forward clause did one honest thing and could be made to do one dishonest one, and the difference lived in the roster of the prior session, because the whole legitimacy of a carry-forward turned on a prior commission having confirmed the appointment first. The clause permitted a confirmation made good under one commission to be carried into the record of the next without fresh review. It did not permit a confirmation that no prior commission had ever made. So she went through the prior roster name by name, the way Sefu Adebayo would have gone through it though she had never watched him do it, looking for Iwan Kebe among the confirmations the prior session claimed to have made.

He was not there.

She went through it again, slower, allowing for variant spellings and the chance of a clerk's hand turning a letter, and he was not there the second time either. The carry-forward entry on the Sennar magistrate cited the authority of a prior commission and a date of original confirmation, as the clause required, and the prior commission's own roster held no confirmation under that name on that date or any other. The thing the carry-forward was reconciling did not exist. The clause had been fed an appointment it could dress in the authority of a session that had never seen the man, and the dressing was the date discrepancy the throne room had passed over, because an honest carry-forward and a fabricated one looked identical on the face of the current record and parted only when you laid the current entry against a prior roster that lived in a different bureau and that no one ever thought to lay it against.

Someone had built it knowing that. The phrase arrived in her mother's cadence and Kaviel did not chase where she had heard it; it was the shape of the thing speaking, and the shape was a designed one.

She sat back and let the cold move across the back of her neck and turned to the third file, which was the one she had pulled on an instinct she could not yet justify to a board, and which she had half expected to come up empty. The transit manifest from Sabra Crossing. The southern feeder line, the movement she had run and filed closed with her own hand, the frightened woman with the document case who had come in off a board that did not post and gone out the seventh gate on a voucher Kaviel had filled in herself. She had filed the woman's origin in that report because the work required an origin and the feeder had given her one. Sennar. The woman had come up out of the Sennar Prefecture, on a line that terminated at the crossing and posted to no onward board, carrying a records-office document case, practiced at not being looked at.

Kaviel laid the manifest beside the Sennar magistrate file and the field report and the prior roster, and she made herself go slowly, because this was the place the night could go wrong, the place where a tired mind reached across a gap because the reaching felt like seeing. The manifest did not name the woman; the woman had traveled under a name that was not hers, and Kaviel had moved her under a third. There was no line on the manifest that touched a line in the magistrate file. What touched was the prefecture. A protected witness moved quietly out of Sennar, in the same season that a fabricated magistrate's confirmation out of Sennar surfaced in open court, in the same season a field report flagged a cluster of fabricated confirmations and a senior authority stood up a reconciliation review and buried it deep enough that a night archivist had to be woken to surrender a roster. And a man at the document desk in the second concourse with a courier's flat satchel and a face built to be forgotten, who had found the woman's bench with his eyes twice and never moved on her, who had the loose outward attention of a person whose work was to see a thing and carry what he saw somewhere else.

She had filed his face without a frame three weeks ago because the evidence would not hold a frame, and she had been right to, and she was careful now not to give him one he had not earned. A face in a crowd at a transit hub was a face in a crowd. But a witness does not get moved out of a prefecture for nothing, and the someone who took the trouble to move her had taken it because of something she could tell, and a witness with something to tell out of Sennar, in this season, was a fact that lay against the others with a weight Kaviel could feel and could not yet prove. The relay Silva had not recognized in the handler chain sat in the same drawer. Three names before the advance reached them, the third unpulled, the routing through a channel Silva did not know. Kaviel had told Silva to note it and leave it. She wished now she had a name for the relay and was glad she did not, because a name she wanted this badly was a name to distrust.

She had the shape. She turned it over twice more and it held its lines and added none it had not earned. A clause built to be boring, fed appointments no prior commission had made, dressed in dates that failed only against a roster no one cross-checked, surfacing in Sennar and in at least one jurisdiction more, with someone senior enough to stand up a review that hid where the first suspicion came from, and a witness lifted quietly out of the middle of it, and a courier with a forgettable face standing where she had been lifted. It was a method and it had a hand behind it and the hand had taken pains to be a process and not a person, so that anyone who pulled on it would find clauses and clerks and the ordinary friction of a machine too large to run without friction, and no one to charge.

She did not have the hand. She had the shape of the glove it left in the record, the size of it and the reach of it and the patience of it, and where the shape narrowed she could feel the absence at its center the way you feel a missing stair in the dark. She knew better than to fill it. Inference that ran ahead of evidence was how operations went wrong, and she had told a younger officer that in a stairwell once and meant it, and she was not going to spend the credit of a clean night's reading on a name she wanted because the shape ached for one.

The roster had to go back by the sixth hour. She squared the four files, the manifest atop the magistrate matter, the report atop those, the heavy roster beneath, and she turned the lamp down until the flame stood low and blue at its base and the corners of the room went to dark. She did not turn it out. She sat in the near-dark with the cold still drawing past her wrist and the wet-stone smell thinning as the court below dried, and she let the shape sit with her without working it further, the way she had learned to let a thing settle before she trusted what she had built.

Then she took a clean half-sheet from the drawer and uncapped the pen and held it over the paper a moment. She wrote one word. She read it once. She capped the pen, folded the sheet twice into a small square, and put it in the inner pocket of the coat that hung on the back of her chair, against the lining, where her own hand would find it and no one else's would. She drew the coat on. She gathered the three files for return and left her own where it lay, and she went out into the corridor toward the duty lamp where Meret kept the log, the folded paper riding cold against her ribs through the lining.

# Chapter 7 — The Court Moves

The state hall had been built to hold a thousand and tonight it held perhaps four hundred, which made it worse rather than better, because four hundred people in a room built for a thousand each carried their own pocket of air with them and moved it through the others, and the heat of them rose into a ceiling too high to give it back. They had come for the announcement of the autumn commissions, the routine confirmations of the bodies that would sit through the cold half of the year, and the throne had chosen to mark it with a dinner because a dinner cost less than a ceremony and bought as much, and because Amira had learned that the court behaved best when it was fed and watched at once. The long tables ran the length of the hall under the high windows, dark now and giving back the lamplight in the glass, and the smell of the food came up the room in layers, the roasted meat first and under it the sweet of the spiced wine and under that the wax of four hundred lamps burning down toward the second course.

Amira entered after the hall was seated, which was the form, and the hall rose, which was also the form, and she crossed to the raised table at the head of the room with the slow exact step that the overrobe required of her and that she had stopped resenting somewhere in her second year. She did not look at the room as she crossed it. She looked at the chair she was walking toward, because a ruler who scanned the faces while she walked told the room she was counting it, and the room would count itself back, and the dinner would become a census instead of a meal. She let them watch her arrive. She let the watching do its work. When she reached the head table she turned and stood a moment and let the hall hold its rising a breath longer than it expected, and then she sat, and the hall sat, and the long exhale of four hundred people lowering themselves onto benches ran the length of the room and was lost in the music from the gallery.

The musicians played from above and behind, set in the gallery over the doors so that the sound came down into the hall without a source the eye could fix, and the effect of it was that the talk had to climb over the music to be heard, and so every conversation in the room was pitched a half-step louder than its speakers wished, and leaned closer than they wished, and gave more away. Amira had ordered the musicians placed there in her first year and had never moved them. She ate little. She watched the room without appearing to watch it, in the glass of the dark windows where the hall hung reversed and dim, and in that reflection she could follow a man crossing the floor without turning her head to do it.

---

Councillor Hadi Marun came to the head table between the first course and the second, which was the right time, early enough to show eagerness and late enough to show he understood he was not the only man who wished to be seen. He was a broad, easy man somewhere past fifty, with the kind of face that gathered other faces toward it, and he made his bow to the throne with a fraction more depth than the floor required and held it a fraction longer, and Amira marked the fraction and did not hold it against him. Some men flattered because they wanted and some because they could not help it, and Marun, as far as the throne had ever been able to determine, was the second kind, which made him useful and slightly dangerous in ways he did not himself suspect.

"Majesty," he said, straightening. "The hall is in good spirits."

"The hall has been fed," Amira said. "Spirits follow the kitchen more reliably than the throne."

He laughed, the real laugh of a man who enjoyed being answered, and the laugh drew two heads at the nearest table and he was aware of them drawing and let himself be aware of them, comfortable in the small gravity of his own presence. "I had hoped to put a word in for the river prefectures before the commissions sit," he said. "The waterworks matter from the spring. There is feeling in the guilds that the cistern ruling left things unsettled."

"The cistern ruling settled the law," Amira said. "It left the feeling unsettled because the feeling was never about the law. Bring me the instrument they want drawn and I will read it. Do not bring me the feeling. I have feeling enough of my own about cisterns."

Marun inclined his head, taking the correction the way a man takes a coin he half expected, and he asked after the autumn commissions then, lightly, as a man asks after the weather of a country he means to travel through, and Amira told him what would be announced because it would be announced within the hour regardless. The Bureau of Establishments would seat its review bodies. The Third Appointments Commission would carry forward into the cold session. He nodded along to the names of the bodies with the attention of a man who found them as dull as he was meant to find them, and there was nothing in his face when the Third Appointments Commission was named, no flicker, no settling, and Amira marked the nothing the way Eleanor had taught her to mark such things, and filed it where she filed all the nothings, which was in a place she did not yet have a use for. He bowed again and withdrew, and the second course came up the room behind him on the shoulders of the servers, and the smell of it changed from meat to the sharp green of the dressed bitter leaves that the southern tables loved and the northern delegations left untouched on their plates.

---

In the colonnade along the eastern wall, where the lamplight thinned and the music came through as a pressure more than a melody, Lady Provost Imara Shandt stood with a cup she was not drinking and conducted three conversations at once without appearing to conduct any. She held the provostship of the upland schools, which gave her the placing of every magister who came out of them, and so she was never without men around her, and tonight there were four, and she moved among the four of them with the economy of a woman who had long since stopped finding any of it interesting and had not let the boredom reach her face.

A magister of the Bureau of Establishments stood at her left, a younger man in the administrative gray, and he was telling her about the autumn rosters in the low fast voice of a man who believes he is being indiscreet and is enjoying it. "The carry-forward list is longer this session than last," he was saying. "Half again as long. The reconciliation backlog out of the Outer Prefectures has grown faster than the commissions can clear it, and so it carries, and carries, and the books grow fat with it."

"The books have always grown fat," Lady Provost Shandt said. "That is what books are for." She turned her cup a quarter and did not drink from it. "Which prefectures."

"Sennar chiefly. The eastern uplands. The whole of the Outer establishment, but Sennar leads it." The young magister lowered his voice further, pleased with his own caution. "Between us, Provost, I have stopped reading the carry-forward entries with any care. There are too many. You confirm what the prior commission confirmed and you move to the next. To read each would take a year and confirm nothing the prior body did not already confirm."

"Then you confirm what the prior commission confirmed," she said, "and you trust that the prior commission read what the one before it confirmed, and so on back to a commission that read nothing because it was the first and had nothing behind it but a clean book." She set the cup down on the ledge of the colonnade window and left it there. "You have described a chain in which every link trusts the link before it and no link bears weight. I taught logic before I placed magisters. Go and enjoy the wine. You have given me something to be tired about."

The young magister did not understand that he had been dismissed, or understood and did not mind it, and he drifted toward the wine, and Lady Provost Shandt stood a moment longer at the window with her undrunk cup beside her and looked out at the dark grounds where nothing moved, and then she turned back into the room and was claimed at once by the next of her men, and the thing the young magister had said went into her the way a splinter goes in, unfelt at first under the press of the evening, set to ache later.

---

Amira left the head table during the third course, which a ruler was permitted to do once in an evening, to move down the room and let the lower tables feel the throne pass near them. She went with two of the Lionesses at a distance that read as escort to those who needed it to read that way and as nothing to those who did not, and she stopped at the table of the upland herders' delegation, who had waited a season for their petition and had been seated, by her order, no lower than the river guilds, and she asked the eldest of them about the autumn drive and the state of the high pasture, and she listened to the answer, the whole of it, while the man's hands described the grass with the unconscious eloquence of a man who had spent his life reading it. The southern oils and the foreign scents of the court tables gave way here to wool and woodsmoke carried in the herders' clothes, the smell of the high country that did not wash out in a single season at court, and Amira breathed it and was, for the length of the answer, somewhere other than the hall.

It was while she stood at the herders' table that she saw Eleanor.

The old woman had been brought a chair, as she was always brought a chair, set near the eastern colonnade where the light failed, and she sat in it with her hands folded over the head of the cane and her eyes half-lidded against the smoke of the lamps, and from the body of the hall she was furniture, an old administrator placed where the room could feel her without being asked to attend to her. Amira did not cross to her. In the four years of her reign she had crossed to Eleanor in a public room exactly never, and she would not begin tonight. She let her gaze pass over the colonnade the way it passed over the colonnade's lamps, and in the passing she saw what Eleanor was watching, because Eleanor's stillness had a direction to it the way still water has a current under the surface, and the direction was the long service door at the foot of the eastern wall through which the wine came up from the cellars. Amira looked at the door and saw a server pass through it with a tray and saw nothing else, and she returned her attention to the herder's hands and the high grass, and filed the door, and the angle of the old woman's attention, and the nothing she had seen in the doorway, into the place where the nothings went.

---

Behind the eastern wall the service passage ran the length of the hall, low-ceilinged and lit by working lamps set in iron brackets, and the music did not reach it; it stopped at the wall as though the wall had been built to stop it, and what crossed into the passage instead was the duller sound of the kitchens below and the feet of the servers on the stone and the occasional swing of the door letting in a wash of the hall's roar before it closed again and cut it off. The air here was different, cooler and wetter, the cellar air rising through the floor, and over it the smell of the kitchens, the fat and the smoke and the scorched edge of a sauce held too long over the fire.

Two men spoke in the passage near the wine store, a steward of the household and a man in the plain dress of a contracted carrier, the sort of man the household used to bring goods up from the lower city and who came and went through the service entrances by the hundred across a season. They spoke of the wine. The steward was counting it against a list and the carrier was confirming the count, and it was the dullest conversation in the building, and a third man stood a little apart from them and waited, and he was the kind of man the eye declined to hold. He was of middle height and middle years, with a face the demographic of the empire had stamped out a hundred million times, neither dark enough nor pale enough nor marked enough to fix in the memory, and he wore the same plain carrier's dress as the man at the list, and he carried a flat leather case of the sort the bureaus used to move documents, and he waited with the patience of a man who had waited in a great many passages. When the steward finished his count and turned back toward the hall, the forgettable man stepped to the carrier and the two of them exchanged a word too quiet for the passage to carry, and the carrier took something from the flat case and put it inside his coat, and the forgettable man took the case back and turned and walked the length of the passage away from the hall, toward the lower doors and the city, and no one marked his going because there was nothing in his going to mark. A man with a case had come and gone through a service passage. The passages were full of such men. That was what the passages were for.

---

Magister Faraj Oduya found Councillor Otho Bey at the wine table during the lull before the final course, and the two of them stood with their backs to the hall in the way men stand when they wish the hall to see only their backs. Oduya held a magistracy of the appellate courts and Bey held a seat on the council that voted the bureau budgets, and between them they could move a great deal of the empire's middle machinery, and they knew it, and they were careful with each other the way two men are careful when each holds a thing the other wants.

"The autumn budget," Bey said, low, into his cup. "The Bureau of Establishments has asked for more clerks. Reconciliation clerks. To clear the carry-forward backlog before it carries another year."

"And you will give them the clerks," Oduya said.

"I will give them the clerks. It is the dullest request in the budget and it will pass without a word said. More clerks to clear more backlog. Who votes against clerks." Bey drank. "I tell you because you sit appeals out of the Outer Prefectures, and the magistrates you hear appeals on are the magistrates these clerks will be confirming, and I would have you know that the books behind your bench are about to grow faster than any one man could read them, so that when a thing comes up out of Sennar that does not sit right, you will not waste a year of your court looking for a clerk's error in a stack no court could clear."

Oduya was quiet a moment, turning his cup. He was a careful man, and the care had kept him on his bench through three reigns of the appellate courts, and the care heard something in what Bey had said that Bey had not meant to put there, or had meant to put there and would never admit to. "You are telling me," he said slowly, "to expect things out of Sennar that do not sit right."

"I am telling you," Bey said, "to expect a great deal of backlog and a great many clerks, and to keep your bench clean while both grow, because a clean bench is the only thing a man in your seat or mine will have left to stand on if the books behind us are ever read by someone with a year to spend and a reason to spend it." He set down his cup. "I am telling you nothing. I am a councillor talking about a budget at a dinner. Go and find better company. You have made me say more than the wine should let a man say."

He moved off into the room, and Oduya stood at the wine table with his cup and looked at the hall for a while, and the music came down from the gallery over the dark windows, and somewhere the last course was rising from the kitchens on the shoulders of the servers, and the magister of appeals filed away what he had heard in the place a careful man keeps the things he cannot yet act on and cannot afford to forget.

---

Amira gave the announcement from the head table before the last course was cleared, standing, with the hall stilled under her and the music dropped to nothing at her lifted hand. She named the autumn commissions in the flat exact voice she used for instruments of state, the review bodies of the Bureau of Establishments, the seating of the appellate rosters, the carry-forward of the Third Appointments Commission into the cold session, and the hall received the names with the polite half-attention the names deserved, because they were the dullest possible news and the dinner had been good, and the only sign that anything in the recitation carried weight was that nobody anywhere in the room reacted to it at all, which was itself a kind of stillness, and which Amira felt from the dais the way a swimmer feels a cold layer of water rise from below. She finished the announcement. She wished the hall a good cold season. She sat, and the music came back up out of the gallery, and the last course went down the room, and the dinner began the long settling toward its end.

She did not look for Eleanor again. When she let her gaze cross the eastern colonnade a final time, near the end of the evening, the chair that had been brought for the old woman stood empty, carried out or soon to be, and Eleanor was gone, and the place where she had sat was only a place again, a chair in the failing light at the edge of a room. Amira marked the absence the way she had marked the presence, and put it where the rest of the evening had gone.

---

The hall emptied the way such halls empty, slowly and then all at once, the delegations leaving in the order of their precedence and the precedence dissolving near the doors into the ordinary press of people who had eaten and drunk and wished now to be elsewhere. The servers came in behind the leaving to strip the long tables, and the music stopped in the gallery without ceremony, the players packing their instruments into the cases that fit them, and the great room that had held four hundred pockets of moving air gave the air back to the high cold ceiling and grew still, and the smell of the food went stale and flat as the plates were carried out, the rich layers of it collapsing into the single tired smell of a room where a great deal had been eaten and the windows had not been opened.

The Clerk of Petitions, Obasi, came through near the end with the household stewards, walking the length of the hall to confirm that the throne's tables had been struck in the right order, and at the foot of the eastern wall, near the service door through which the wine had come up all evening, he found a small side table that had not been cleared, set apart from the long tables in the lee of the colonnade, laid for one. A single place. A cup, a plate, the small folded card the household used to mark a seating, the name on it in the steward's hand, and a chair drawn out and never pushed in. The food on the plate had not been touched. The wine in the cup had not been poured. The steward beside Obasi looked at his list and looked at the table and could not find, in the list, the reason the table had been set, and Obasi told him to leave it, and the steward left it, and they went on down the room.

The household put out the lamps as it went, the long rows of them quenched in order from the head of the hall toward the doors, and the great dark came down the room behind the stewards in a slow tide, and the last of the servers passed out through the eastern door with the last of the plates, and the hall stood empty under its high invisible ceiling. At the foot of the eastern wall, in the lee of the colonnade, beside the chair that had been drawn out and never pushed in, one lamp had been left burning over the small table laid for one, where the card stood in the steward's hand and the cup held no wine and the plate held a cold untouched meal set for someone who had not come.

# Chapter 8 — What Meret Knows

Meret had asked for the room at the second hour, before the compound came to itself, and Asha had granted it the way she granted most things Meret asked for, without question and with the understanding that Meret did not ask for much. The room sat at the end of the assessment corridor, the small one, built so that a voice raised to its ordinary pitch did not reach the wall and a voice dropped below that reached nothing at all. The walls were doubled and the door hung on oiled hinges that gave back no sound, and there was a table and two chairs and nothing on the walls, none of the columns of initials that hung in the larger rooms, because the room had been built for the kind of conversation that left no record until an officer decided to make one. Asha came down to it cold from her own quarters, the corridor still dark, the duty lamps burning low at their stations, and she let herself in and sat in the chair that faced the door, as she always did, and waited.

The compound at this hour had a smell it lost by the fourth, before the kitchens woke and the day's oil went onto the hinges. It was the smell of stone that had cooled all night and the faint mineral edge of the wash-water the night crew ran through the lower corridors, and over it, very thin, the cold ash of yesterday's lamps not yet relit. Asha had spent more mornings than she could count in this stillness before the building filled, and she knew that a person who asked for a room at the second hour had been awake in it a long time before the asking.

Meret came in at the second hour exactly. She closed the door with the flat of her hand, felt it seat, and sat in the chair across the table without being told to, square to it, her forearms coming to rest on the worn wood. She had a folder with her. She set it down and kept her hands on it, folded, the way she kept her hands on things, and did not open it.

"You have had this a while," Asha said. It was not a question. Meret did not ask for the small room to bring something a day old.

"Six weeks," Meret said.

Asha let that sit between them. Six weeks was a long time for Meret to carry a thing without sending it up the chain, and the length of it was the first piece of information in the room, before the folder, before a word of what was in it. Asha did not ask why Meret had waited. She would hear the why in the shape of what came next, or she would not, and either way the asking would have been an officer wanting to be told she was owed an explanation. Meret had her reasons and they had a discipline to them, and that was enough to begin from.

"Then tell me what it is," Asha said, "and tell me how it came to you, in that order."

Meret nodded, once, and began the way she did everything, from the bottom, with the parts that had dates on them.

"I keep the periphery summaries that Silva sends up," she said. "Not her field notes; the clean summaries, after she has cut them down for the file. I log them in and cross them against the transfer registers from the outer waystations, the arrivals and departures the stations post up the standard channel. That is the routine work, the reconciling. I have done it three years and I know its grain." She turned the folder a quarter on the table, not opening it, only squaring it nearer her own hands. "Six weeks ago I logged a summary out of a waystation on the Sennar feeder, and there was a face in it Silva had noted in passing, a man at a document counter, no flag on him, just a description she put down because she puts everything down. A courier's satchel. The loose look of a man waiting for nothing in a place where everyone waits for something. She moved on. It was not her matter; she was working a different line that day."

"Go on."

"I had seen the description before." Meret said it flat, without weight, the way she would have read a register entry aloud. "Not the man. The description. Three weeks earlier, in a summary out of a different waystation, a different jurisdiction, on a feeder that does not connect to the Sennar line and has no traffic that crosses it. Same satchel. Same loose attention. A man at a transfer point with no onward booking the station could post, present long enough to be noted and gone before the next register cycle closed." She paused, and Asha understood the pause was Meret deciding how much of her method to put into the room and choosing to put all of it. "I do not trust a description twice. A satchel is a satchel and a man waiting is half the people in a waystation. So I did not flag it. I laid the two summaries beside each other and looked for the thing that would tell me it was the same man or the thing that would tell me it was two, and there was not enough on the page to do either. So I left it open and kept logging, and watched the registers as they came up, and waited to see whether the description came a third time."

"And it came a third time," Asha said.

"It came four more times in six weeks." Meret opened the folder now. She lifted the cover and turned it back and laid her hand flat on the top sheet, one she had ruled herself, columns down it, dates and station codes and a short line of description against each, the same phrases recurring down the column because she had used the same words each time on purpose, so that the eye could catch the repetition. "Six instances, six weeks. Five waystations. Every one a transfer point, where a thing moves from one line to another and the register has to be re-cut by hand. Every one on a feeder that touches a jurisdiction where the field work has flagged something in the appointment records this season. I did not put that last part together myself. I keep the summaries, not the appointment files. But I keep the jurisdiction codes, and the codes on my six are the ones I have heard senior officers ask the analysis hall about in the last two months, and I am not deaf in a corridor."

Asha drew the folder a hand's width across the table and read the ruled sheet without touching it, top to bottom, the way she read everything the first time, for the shape and not the argument. Six rows. Six dates, the earliest seven weeks back, the most recent eleven days. Five station codes, one of them appearing twice. A column of descriptions in Meret's square hand, the same four or five phrases worked across all six rows so that they stacked when you ran your eye down them. It was the cleanest kind of work the Quarter did, the kind no examination on a page would teach a person to do, because the doing of it was patience and the patience could not be coached.

"You are reconciling registers," Asha said, reading still, "and a face you have no name for keeps standing up off the page in the places the appointment work is hot. And you have laid it out so that if you are wrong, the wrongness shows as plainly as the rightness."

"If I am wrong it is because six men in six waystations carried the same satchel and waited the same way and booked nothing the station could post," Meret said. "I do not think that is what happened. But I have built it so that it could be, because the day I build it so that it cannot be is the day I have stopped reconciling and started to decide."

Asha looked up at her then. The room was small enough that there was nowhere to look but at the other or at the table, the closeness the point of it, two people set near enough that nothing passed between them the room would let escape. Meret met the look and held it, plain and unhurried, and Asha saw in it none of the thing she watched for in the assessment chair, none of the wanting to be right, only a woman who had carried a finding carefully for six weeks and decided this morning she had carried it as far as one person should.

"This is why you waited," Asha said.

"I waited because three instances is a coincidence I have seen before and been wrong about," Meret said. "Four would have been a pattern, but two of the four I could not stand behind; the descriptions were thin and I would not pad them. I waited until I had six I could stand behind. I have six." She closed the folder over her own sheet, gently, her hand flat on the cover. "I did not wait because I was afraid to be wrong in front of you, Officer. I waited because if I bring you a thing before it will hold, and you act on it, and it does not hold, I have spent the Quarter's reach on my unease, and there is an operation in the field this season the unease could foul. I do not know what it is. I know there is one, because the analysis hall has not slept and a senior was woken for a roster eleven days ago and Kaviel has been in her office past the tenth hour more nights than not. I keep the night log. I see who burns the lamp. I did not need to be told there was something running to know that a wrong move from me could put a foot through it."

Asha sat with that. It was the longest she had heard Meret speak in a year, and Meret had spent it telling her, in operational language and no other, a thing a lesser officer would have dressed as conscience and a worse one as apology. There was no apology in it, only a woman accounting for a decision the way she would account for a register, and Asha respected the accounting more than she would have respected any speech about duty. She did not say so. Meret had not brought the finding to be told she had done well with it.

"You were right to wait," Asha said instead, the same thing said as a finding rather than a comfort. "And you are right to bring it now. Six holds. Three would not have. You know the difference and you held the line at it. That is the work."

Meret inclined her head a degree and said nothing, which was the correct amount to say.

Asha opened the folder again and read it the second way, slower, against what she carried that Meret did not. Meret kept the summaries and the registers and not the appointment files, and so had brought a pattern in transport and could not see the pattern in the records it lay against. Asha sat high enough in the analysis chain to have heard the jurisdiction codes spoken in the hall and to know which had gone hot and why, and she laid Meret's six station codes against that knowing now, row by row, and watched them fall into the hot jurisdictions one after another with the regularity of a thing that was not chance.

"Tell me about the man," Asha said. "Not the satchel. You have given me the satchel. What does this face do, in your reading, that puts him in five waystations in six weeks at the transfer points and nowhere the register can post."

"He carries," Meret said. "He does not travel. A traveler books an onward leg and the station posts it; this man has no onward leg the station can show, which means he is handing off. A transfer point is where a thing leaves one line and joins another and the register is re-cut by hand, and a thing handed across at the moment the register is open can be moved without a clean line from where it started to where it went. He is at the seam every time, and the seam is where the record can be made to lose the thread." She said it without heat, a register entry still. "I think he moves paper, because a courier's satchel carries paper and the jurisdictions are the appointment jurisdictions, and a record-trouble that runs across jurisdictions needs a hand to carry the false thing from where it is made to where it is filed, by a route that does not post. I cannot prove the satchel holds appointment papers; I have never seen inside it. I am telling you the shape my reading takes and where it stops being something I can stand behind."

It was the same discipline Kaviel kept, the line drawn hard between what the page held and what the mind reached past it for, the thing the Quarter selected for above all the rest, the thing she had watched a competent soldier fail to have in the assessment chair weeks back. She set the thought aside.

"You have a face and a method and six instances and no name," Asha said.

"I have no name," Meret agreed. "Silva did not have one. The registers do not carry one for him, because a man who hands off at the seam does not give the counter a name it logs. I have a description and a route and a rhythm. I do not have a man I could put a hand on."

Asha read the rightmost column again, the one Meret had not spoken to, a narrow column at the edge where she had set down, against four of the six rows, a cluster of marks that were not description and not date. Asha had taken them for register codes on the first reading. They were not. They were initials, partial ones, a single letter standing alone in three of the rows and a letter and a half-formed second in the fourth, set down small and tight against the rule.

"What are these," Asha said, and put her finger to the column without touching the page.

"That is the thing I am least sure of," Meret said, "and the reason I almost brought you only the pattern." She did not reach for it; she let Asha hold it. "Three of the waystations keep a hand-signed transfer chit when a thing is moved across by a person rather than down a posted line. It is an old practice, kept in the outer stations and let go in the central ones, a man putting his mark to say the thing passed through his hand. It carries no legal weight. No one reconciles it; it sits in a box at the station until the box is full and then it is burned. Silva does not collect it because it tells you nothing on its own. But three of my six stations keep it, and on the days my face was in those three, a chit was signed across the seam, and the mark, where I could read it, began with the same letter."

Asha was very still.

"Which letter," she said.

"B," Meret said.

The room held it. Asha did not move and did not let her face move, the not-moving a discipline she had kept for forty years and kept now, because a single letter set down in a small room at the second hour was either the first true edge of a thing the Quarter had been reading the shape of for two months or a coincidence of a courtesy practice in three outer stations, and her face would not decide which before her reading had.

"Where you could read it," she said. "Not all three."

"Two I read plainly. The third the chit was smudged and I would not swear past the letter, which is why the half-mark is in the fourth row and not a full one. The fourth station did not keep chits; that row has no mark and I left it empty rather than carry the letter across to it, because the letter belongs to the chits and not to the man, until I have a reason to join them." Meret's hands had come back to folded on the closed folder. "It is the first letter of a name signed on a courtesy chit by a man who handed a thing across a seam, on the days my face was in the station, at three stations of the five. It is not the man's name. It could be the man. It could be a station hand who signed for a transfer that had nothing to do with him. The chit says nothing past the one mark. I laid it at the edge of the sheet on purpose, so that it sits beside the pattern and not inside it. If you take the letter into the pattern you are deciding the courier's name begins with it, and I have not earned that, and I will not put my hand to it until it is earned."

Asha read the column one more time, the lone B set tight against the rule in three rows, the empty fourth where a lesser hand would have filled the letter in to make the page look finished. She thought of a roster pulled from below the active line eleven days back and a reconciliation review stood up under an authority no clerk would name, and of the appointment work that ran across exactly the jurisdictions whose codes were stacked in front of her in Meret's square hand. She held enough of their shape to know that Meret had brought her, without being able to see the far side of it herself, the first thread in two months that ran from the records into a hand, a hand that moved at the seams where the record lost its thread, in the jurisdictions where the false appointments surfaced, leaving a single letter on a courtesy chit no one was meant to read and that Meret had read because Meret read everything.

It was the most incomplete thing she had been handed in weeks, a letter and a satchel and a rhythm, and the incompleteness was the part she would have to carry now, because she sat where the deciding was. Act on a letter as though it were a name and the operation already running in the field, the one whose lamp Meret saw burning past the tenth hour, could turn on the wrong man, or on the right man too soon, before the thing behind him had shown its reach. Wait for the name and the man kept handing off at the seams while they waited, and the longer he handed off the more the record lost. Asha had sat with that choice a hundred times and it did not get lighter with the hundredth.

This was not a day to move on the man. A courier handing off at the seams was a node and not a head, the kind of figure a process used and replaced, and to reach for him now was to spend the surprise on the part of the machine that mattered least and warn the part that mattered most. But the letter was a true edge, and a true edge logged did not have to be found again, and the finding of it had cost Meret six weeks she had spent well.

"You will keep doing exactly what you have been doing," Asha said. "Log the summaries, reconcile the registers, change nothing in how you do it, because the value of what you brought me is that it came up out of the routine and not out of a man looking for it. If you start looking for him, the looking will change the shape of what you see, and I would lose the one reading in this building that is not bent toward an answer. You watch. You log. The next time the face comes up, you note the station and the date and whether a chit was kept, and you bring it to me here, to no one else, and you put it in no standing file. A thread this thin breaks if it is handled by more hands than need to hold it, and right now the hands that need to hold it are yours and mine."

"Understood," Meret said.

"The sheet stays with me."

"I made it to give to you."

Asha drew it the last of the way across the table and squared it to the folder's edge. "Kaviel is working a shape of this from the records side. She does not have what you have; you do not have what she has; neither of you is to be told what the other holds until I decide the two halves are worth joining, and that decision is mine and it is not made this morning." She said the name once and let it lie, because Meret kept the night log and knew whose lamp burned and would have her own shape forming of why Asha named her, and she would keep that shape to herself the way she kept everything. "You have not spoken Kaviel's name in connection with any of this. You will not."

"I have not," Meret said. "I will not."

Asha rose and went to the low cabinet against the doubled wall, unmarked, locked with a key she carried and not one the building held. She took out a ledger that was hers and not the Quarter's, narrow, bound in plain dark board, the log a senior officer kept of threads too thin to file and too true to lose, and carried it back and uncapped her pen. Meret had risen when Asha rose, and stood at the far side of the small table with her closed folder under her hand, waiting to be released, watching without watching the way she did.

The room had warmed a half-degree with the two of them in it, the ash smell of the dead lamps thinning, and somewhere far down through the doubled walls the first sound of the kitchens reached them as a vibration more than a noise, the day beginning to assemble itself two floors below while the small room held its silence a moment longer. Asha turned to a clean page near the back of the ledger, past the older threads in her own cramped hand, and set the date at the head of it, and the station codes from Meret's sheet beneath, the rhythm, the absence of an onward booking, the courtesy chit and what it did and did not prove. She wrote it short, because a long entry was an officer arguing with herself on the page and she was not arguing; she had decided. At the foot of the entry, in the narrow space she left for the thing each thread came down to, she wrote a single letter, and looked at it a moment in her own hand, the lone B sitting at the bottom of the page the way it had sat at the edge of Meret's sheet, an edge and not a name, true and not yet enough.

She capped the pen. She closed the ledger over the letter and set her hand flat on the cover for the length of a breath, and then she stood.

"We work from what we have," she said.

# Interlude 2 — Personal Correspondence, Eleanor De Grosvenor to Kaviel De Grosvenor

The following was recovered from a locked compartment in Eleanor De Grosvenor's private effects, sealed but bearing no dispatch mark, written in her own hand. It was never sent, and there is no record that Kaviel ever read it.

---

Kaviel,

I have started this letter four times across as many years and burned three of the attempts. I am keeping this one because I am old enough now to know I will not write a fifth, and because the things I have left unsaid have begun to outweigh the things I have managed to say to you, which were never many.

I want to tell you first what I saw when they brought you to me. You will not remember it, and I have never described it, because to describe it would have meant telling you what I decided in the same room, within the same hour. I held you for the length of time it takes to read a short report. Then I gave you to the house, to be raised by people I trusted to do it better than I would, and I went back to work that did not wait. I have told myself for thirty years that this was the correct allocation. It was correct. It was also a thing I did, with my hands, and the correctness of it has never once made it lighter to carry.

You should understand that I did not give you to the work because I did not want you. I gave you to the work because I knew what I was, and I knew what I would make of a child kept close. I had already watched the house take in promising people and grind the soft parts off them. I did not want to be the hand that did that to you across a breakfast table for fifteen years. So I arranged for it to be done by strangers who were kinder than I am, and by a discipline that does not pretend to love you while it shapes you. I believed you would be more whole that way. I still believe it. I am only no longer certain the belief was about you.

I have read your field assessments. All of them, going back to your probationary year, including the ones Asha marked low and the ones you appealed. You were right to appeal two of them and wrong to appeal the third, and the fact that you knew which was which before the review board did is the thing that told me what you would become. You do not work the way I work. I have spent six decades learning who owes whom, who can be moved by debt and who by vanity, where a man keeps the favor he is ashamed of. You learned instead to read a page until it confesses. I used to think the document was the smaller instrument. I was wrong about that, and the wrongness has cost the house time I cannot recover. The networks I built are aging out with me. The records will outlast every name I ever cultivated. You saw that before I admitted it.

I want to say something now that I have never said inside the walls, where it would be heard as weakness and filed as such. I am afraid. The apparatus I assembled was built around the assumption that I would always be in the room. I am not always in the room any longer. There are afternoons I lose, hours I cannot account for, decisions I have to make twice because I do not trust the first version. When the test comes, and it is coming, I will not be at full strength to meet it. You will. And I put you where you stand, knowing this hour would arrive and that you would have to hold the thing I built without me holding it with you. That was not an accident of your talent. It was a choice I made when you were small, and I dressed it in words like obligation so that I would not have to call it what it was.

The compact between this house and the throne is older than Amira and older than me, and you have inherited a place in it that you did not ask for and could not refuse, because I arranged your life so that refusing was never available to you. I am not going to pretend that was fair. It was not fair. It was necessary, by my reckoning, and I have learned that necessity and unfairness sit at the same table more often than anyone in my position will admit aloud. You have every right to resent the position. I would think less of you if some part of you did not.

Here is what I cannot say to your face, and so I am saying it to a page I may never give you. You did not become what you are because of the name. The name opened doors. It did not write the assessments. It did not catch the things you have caught. Whatever I arranged, the work itself is yours, and no part of it was inherited. I have watched a great many people fail to be equal to what was demanded of them. You have not been one of them. That you are not warm toward me, that you keep the careful distance the work requires between us, I understand and do not hold against you. I built that distance. I would only ask, if you ever read this, that you not mistake my distance for indifference. It was the opposite, kept under discipline so long that it learned to look like nothing at all.

I am going to seal this and put it where the rest of my private papers go, and I expect it will be read, if it is read, by someone going through my effects with a ledger after I am gone. If that someone is you, then know that I went over every line and meant each one, and that I corrected the punctuation twice, because even here I could not let it go untidy.

Be careful with the documents. They are telling you the truth before the people do. Trust them, and trust yourself reading them, ahead of anything I taught you about loyalty.

E. De Grosvenor
