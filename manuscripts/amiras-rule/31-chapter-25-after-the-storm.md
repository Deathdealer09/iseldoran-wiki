# Chapter 25 — After the Storm

The incense had been lit at the fourth hour, as it was lit on every working morning, and by the time the first matter came forward the smoke had settled into the high vaulting the way it always settled, and given up trying to rise. Amira sat in it. The overrobe held its own shape across her shoulders, the metal thread stiff along the seams, and she let it hold her, as she had learned to let it. The marble ran in long bands of polish before the dais. Through the polish, where the light from the eastern clerestory struck it, the old repair lines showed, the seams where the floor had been broken and laid again across four hundred years of audiences. Nothing in the room had changed. That was the first thing a careful eye would have read, and the wrong thing, because the room had been through something, and the rooms that have been through something wear it in what they have decided not to show.

Three weeks had passed since the chamber on the third floor of the Hall of Establishments had heard the floor drop out from under its assumptions. In those three weeks the Throne Court of Inquiry had taken the file and begun its slow exact work; Lord Marshal Tewodros Marek had been formally charged before it, his authority over the Bureau of Establishments suspended pending the inquiry's finding; and the financial mechanism that had run for seven years beneath the appointments fraud was being taken apart channel by channel by people who understood that a wrong move would send the resources fleeing to jurisdictions the prerogative could not reach. None of that was in the throne room this morning. The throne room held a court that had to keep governing while the inquiry governed the men who had governed it wrongly, and the keeping was the work, and Amira had come to do the work.

She heard the first matter through. A boundary dispute out of the river prefectures, two estates and a shifted channel and a question of which bank now owned the silt the river had moved. She asked the Clerk for the date of the original survey, and when Obasi found it she asked who had certified it, and when the certifier proved to be an office rather than a man she ordered a fresh survey under the prefect's seal and held the matter open against its return. The estates withdrew, neither satisfied, neither cheated. That was the work, and it was the same work it had been before the crisis, and she did it the same way, because a court watching for a tremor in the throne would find none.

But the court was watching for the tremor. She could feel it the way she could feel the cold come up through the marble a stride to either side of the warm band of light. The hall this morning held perhaps two hundred and fifty, a fuller working session than usual, and the fullness itself was a message; men who had let a clerk attend their business for a season had decided to attend it themselves, to be seen attending it, to be in the room while the room settled into whatever it was going to be. Faces dark and faces darker along the standing rails, the broad cheekbones of the steppe-blood and the long southern lines mixed through as they had always been mixed, the court that looked like the empire because the empire had made it. And under the polite attention to the boundary survey ran the other attention, the recalculation, the quiet arithmetic of people working out where the weight had moved and whether any of it had come loose near them.

Obasi read the second matter into the room, a question of a guild charter, and Amira heard it, and in the interval before she ruled she let her gaze cross the western wall, where the colonnade ran back into the administrative passages, and found Kaviel there in the dark working coat, hands behind her, attention moving over the hall in its slow circuit. The room registered her the way it registered a load-bearing column. Amira's gaze passed over her without stopping, which was how both of them preferred it. Then her gaze crossed the band of shadow behind the screen, set back of the dais, where the plain chair had been carried in.

Eleanor sat in it.

She sat as she always sat, with the stillness of the very old, her hands folded over the head of the cane she did not strictly need, her eyes half-lidded in the smoke. From the body of the hall she was furniture, an old administrator placed where the room could feel her without being asked to attend to her. And she was furniture in a way she had not been before, and Amira saw it, because Amira had spent four years learning to read the one chair in the room she never turned to look at. The stillness was the same stillness. What had gone out of it was the current under the surface, the direction, the thing that made Eleanor's silence a held attention pointed at one corner of the room while the rest of her rested. This morning the silence had no current. Eleanor watched the hall, and the watching cost her, and she had less to spend, and she was spending it the way a person spends from a purse she now has to count. The chin did not come up at the second matter, or the third. The attention did not gather and point. It rested, because resting was what was left.

Amira ruled on the guild charter and did not turn toward the shadow and did not, by any motion the hall could read, mark what she had seen. She had crossed to Eleanor in open court exactly twice in five years and had decided after the second time that she would not do it again, and that decision held this morning the way it had held through everything, because the compact bought the throne the right to feel the old woman in the room without the Empress ever appearing to need her, and the crisis had not bought it back. She set what she had seen where the throne set such things, in the place she kept the matters she could not act on in session and could not afford to forget.

Obasi read the third matter.

"A petition of the Sennar Prefecture," he said, "brought by the prefectural assembly, touching the confirmation of magistrates seated under carry-forward reconciliation during the past four sessions."

It was the crisis come up out of the floor and into the open court, dressed in the clothes of ordinary business, and the hall knew it, and the polite half-attention along the standing rails sharpened into the held quiet of a room that has decided to listen and not be seen listening. The Sennar assembly had sent a delegation, three of them, a senior holder of orchard land at their head, and they came forward into the cut light below the dais and went down, and the eldest spoke. He did not contest the throne's authority. He asked, with the care of a man who knew he was asking it in front of the whole court, what was to become of the rulings of magistrates whose confirmations the inquiry was now examining, and whether the men and women of Sennar who had won or lost before those benches stood now on judgments that would hold.

It was the right question, and a hard one, and Amira had known it would come, because she had spent part of three weeks deciding how she would answer it when it did. She let the silence sit a moment. The censer-bearer crossed below the dais on soft feet to feed the nearest brazier, and the incense thickened, sweet and resinous over the flat mineral cold of the stone, and caught at the back of her throat the way it caught every morning, and she made no sign of it.

"The inquiry examines the confirmations," she said. "It does not examine the rulings. A magistrate wrongly seated may yet have ruled rightly on the water that crosses one man's ground to another's, and a magistrate rightly seated may have ruled badly, and the throne does not pretend that the soundness of a judgment follows from the cleanliness of the appointment that produced the bench. Every ruling of a questioned confirmation stands until a party to it brings the throne cause to review the ruling itself, on its own ground, and not on the ground of the magistrate's seating. The seating is the throne's affair and the inquiry's. The ruling is the litigant's, and the litigant who was wronged in law has the door he always had. I will not throw out four sessions of Sennar's judgments to punish four sessions of Sennar's appointments. The prefecture will not be made to relitigate its whole life because the Bureau was made to relitigate its honesty."

She paused, and let that reach the standing rails, and then she gave them the rest of it, because the rest of it was the consolidation and the court needed to hear the throne speak it plainly.

"The carry-forward reconciliation is suspended across the Outer Prefectures," she said. "It will not be used again as it was used. Confirmation by carry-forward required no fresh review, and that absence is what the fraud lived in, and the absence is closed. The Bureau of Establishments will seat appointments under a new oversight, answerable past the Bureau, the form of it to be entered into the record within the month under the Throne's hand. Until it is seated, no appointment moves in the Outer Prefectures except under direct review. The backlog will grow. The throne would rather carry a backlog than carry a fraud, and the prefectures will be told as much in plain words, so that no holder of orchard land waits a year for a confirmation and reads the waiting as the throne's neglect when it is the throne's care."

The Sennar delegation pressed their hands together and made their obeisance and withdrew, and the eldest of them backed the three required steps out of the cut light with the look of a man who had come for less and been given more than he had dared to frame, and the cold came up around the three of them as they crossed out of the warm band, and the sound of their going came back off the high stone for the length of it. Along the standing rails the held quiet let itself out into the small shiftings and settlings of a court that has heard the thing it came to hear and now knows the shape of the season. Talk would have been a discourtesy the throne could mark, and the rails kept to the shiftings.

That was the consolidation, and it was done, and Amira felt it land the way she had felt the nothing land in this room before, as a layer of changed water rising from below.

Obasi read the fourth matter, and the fifth, the ordinary business of a working session, a customs review and a charter and a question of a bridge toll, and the court resettled toward the ordinary the way a body settles toward sleep after a fright, slowly, with a part of it still listening. Amira ruled each in its turn and gave none of them less than its due, because a court that saw the throne hurry the small business after the large would learn that the throne had favorites among its matters, and the lesson she meant the court to take from this morning was the opposite one, that the throne ruled the bridge toll with the same attention it ruled the Bureau, and that the attention had not been spent down by the crisis any more than the marble had been worn through by the feet that crossed it.

It was during the fifth matter that Councillor Imani Tau came forward.

She held no business on the register; she came in the interval, in the way the court permitted a councillor of standing to approach between matters, and she made her bow to the throne with exactly the depth the floor required and not a fraction more, which was its own kind of statement in a room where the fractions were being read. She was a spare upright woman past fifty, dark, close in her bearing, and she had sat the council through the whole of the crisis without ever having been any part of the thing the crisis was about, and she carried that now, the carrying of a person who had been in the building while it was being robbed and had not seen the robbery, and who had decided that the honest answer to not having seen was to come and stand where the throne could see her and say so without saying it.

"Majesty," she said. "The river charter the throne held this morning. The river prefectures will want to know whether the new survey runs under the old seal or a fresh one."

"A fresh one," Amira said. "The prefect's, and certified by name and not by office. The throne has had enough of certifications that no man's hand stands behind." She let that sit between them, and Imani Tau heard the second thing in it, the thing under the river survey, and did not flinch from it.

"There will be more of that asked, Majesty," she said. "Names where offices used to do. The council will want to know how far the throne means to take it."

"As far as it goes," Amira said. "The council will find that the throne means to be answered by people and not by stamps, and the council will find it tiresome, and the council will do it, because the alternative was tried for seven years and the throne has read the bill." She inclined her head a degree. "You did not see it, Councillor. Nor did most of this hall. The throne does not hold that against the people who were not looking where the fraud was hidden, because the fraud was built to be where no honest person was looking. It holds it against the people who built the hiding. Go back to your seat. When the new oversight comes before the council you will read it closely, because you are one of the readers the throne trusts to be tired in the right places."

Imani Tau understood that she had been given something, the throne's word that the honest majority would not be made to pay for the dishonest few, and she understood that she had been given a charge with it, and she made her bow and went back along the rail to her place, and the going of her was watched by a good part of the hall, because the hall had been waiting to learn how the throne would hold the people who had merely failed to see, and now it knew, and the knowing ran along the rails the way the earlier quiet had run.

The morning went on. The light had moved a hand's breadth along the marble while the Sennar delegation spoke, so that the warm band fell now a little nearer the dais than it had, and the smoke hung in the vaulting, and the throne ruled and the court believed itself watched, and the work pressed down through the metal-threaded brocade with the weight Amira had long since stopped mistaking for the weight of the cloth. She did not look to the band of shadow again. Once, in the interval before the last matter, she let her gaze cross the western wall, and Kaviel was where she had been, and her circuit had slowed, and twice in the morning it had passed the band of shadow behind the screen and held there a half-beat longer than a column's eye would hold, and Amira marked that Kaviel had marked her mother, and put it where the rest went.

Obasi read the last matter and Amira ruled it, and then the Clerk of Petitions closed the register, and the under-clerk dipped his pen and wrote the closing minute, and the soft scratch of it carried up into the smoke and was lost, and the court rose with the long exhale of two hundred and fifty people lifting themselves off their feet at once, and the rising ran the length of the hall and broke against the high cold ceiling. Amira held them a breath longer than they expected, the way she held them, and then she gave the dismissal, and the court began to empty.

She did not rise with it. A ruler who left while the court was leaving gave the court a back to talk about; she had learned in her first year to let the room go before her, to sit the throne while the hall drained, so that the last thing the leaving court carried out the doors was the throne still in its place, attending, unmoved. So she sat, and watched the room empty.

It emptied the way the hall always emptied, slowly and then all at once, the delegations going in the order of their precedence and the precedence dissolving near the western doors into the ordinary press of people who had business elsewhere. The Sennar delegation went, and the river estates, and Imani Tau among the council, and the standing rails thinned, and the door-wardens in their formal gray stood the watch and passed them through. Eleanor's chair, when Amira let her gaze cross the band of shadow a final time, stood empty; the old woman had been helped up and out during the last matter, earlier than she would have left before the crisis, and the plain chair waited to be carried out, and the place where she had sat was a place again. Kaviel was gone from the western wall, gone out behind her mother in the working coat, and the column the room had registered was registered now only by its absence.

The servers came in behind the leaving to ready the hall for the afternoon, and the household began to put out the lamps it did not need through the daylight hours, quenching the long rows in order from the head of the hall toward the doors. The smell of the morning's incense went stale and flat as the braziers were let to die, the sweet resin collapsing into the single tired smell of a room where a great deal had been decided and the high windows did not open. The great room that had held two hundred and fifty pockets of attention gave the air back to the ceiling and grew still.

Amira rose then, when the hall was nearly empty, and came down the two steps of the dais onto the marble, onto the warm band where the eastern light still fell, and stood a moment in it with the cold waiting a stride to either side. The overrobe held her up. She let it.

At the foot of the eastern wall, in the lee of the colonnade, where the household had set a small side table apart from the working benches, a single lamp had been left burning. She had ordered it set there that morning, before the court, in the way she ordered the small things that the court would never see and that were not for the court. A table laid for one. A cup, a plate, the small folded card the household used to mark a seating. Once, in the autumn of her first hard year, such a table had been laid in such a place at a dinner for the court, set for someone the throne had reason to want present and could not name, and the someone had not come, and the household had found the table at the end of the night with the food untouched and the cup unpoured and one lamp left burning over it, and the place had stayed in Amira's keeping the way the nothings stayed.

This table was not empty. The cup had been poured. The plate had been eaten from, the meal taken plainly and without ceremony by a person who had sat at the small table in the lee of the colonnade through part of the morning and risen and gone on to the work the afternoon held, the way that person took every meal, between one obligation and the next, neither resting nor performing the lack of rest. Sefu Adebayo's hand was in the folded card, the steward's seating done in the small exact script of Eleanor's longest-serving man, who had set his principal's chair where she could sit out of the hall's sight and be near it, and had seen her fed, because she would not see to it herself and someone in sixty years had always had to.

Amira stood over the small table in the failing rows of the lamps, and looked at the poured cup and the cleared plate and the one lamp burning, and the cold came up around her ankles where she had stepped out of the warm band without marking that she had stepped, and from the high windows the angled light moved another finger's width along the marble toward the dais she had left.
