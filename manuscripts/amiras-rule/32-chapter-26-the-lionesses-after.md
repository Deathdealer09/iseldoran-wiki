# Chapter 26 — The Lionesses After

The compound did not stop for the case and it did not stop after it, and that was the first thing Asha set down when she came back to her own desk and found the intake stacked where the runners had left it through the days she had been in the investigation room.

She had been away from intake for eleven working days, which was longer than she had been away from it in nineteen years, and intake did not forgive an absence. It only accrued. The tray on the corner of her desk held the assessment files of the four candidates the Quarter was carrying through the spring cohort, two of them due their second board within the week, and beside the tray sat the standing reports she signed without reading because she had read their like a thousand times and beneath them the few she read twice because they were the ones a careless hand would have signed. She drew the chair in and sat and pulled the first file and opened it, and the room around her was the room it had always been, the narrow assessment office under the east wall with the one shelf of bound standards and the smell of stylus ink and the floor wax the keepers laid down each night, and the light through the slot window came in thin and climbed the wall as the morning ran, and she worked.

She worked the first candidate clean. The young man had come up through the surveyor's track and the second-stage work showed the thing the first board could not have shown, which was what he did when the watch went long and nothing happened. He held. The file said he held. Asha read the field exercise and the keeper's hand at the foot of it and set her own mark below the keeper's, and she meant to move to the next file, and she did not, because she had reached the line where the board recorded the candidate's grasp of the limits of the mandate, and she sat with the line a moment, and then she set the stylus down.

The line said what it had said for nineteen years. It asked whether the candidate understood the bounds of the charter and could state them and could hold them under pressure, and the young man could, and the keeper had marked it so, and the mark was true and the mark was not enough, and Asha had known it was not enough since the third day in the investigation room and had not had the hours to say so until now. The bounds of the charter were the bounds Kaviel had drawn for Zola in the cold store the autumn before, the line between reading the record and reaching past it, and the case had stood in the registry because the line had held, because Asha had hit the category she could not investigate and logged the wall and not crossed it, and a board that asked only whether a candidate knew the bounds was a board that had not asked the harder thing, which was whether the candidate could build a case that did not need the crossing. Knowing the wall was there was the easy half. Working so that the work never had to touch it was the half the registry morning had turned on, and the standard sheet did not measure it, because the standard sheet had been written before anyone in the Quarter had needed to prove a finding to a court that wanted it struck.

She pushed the candidate files aside, squared, unfinished, and took a clean sheet, and at the head of it she wrote the date and below the date the words *Revision, assessment standard, second-stage board*, and she began.

She did not write fast. She wrote the way she wrote everything, short, each clause weighed against the use it would be put to, because a standard was a thing other hands would hold long after hers and a loose clause in a standard was a candidate passed wrong for a generation. She wrote the new line first. The old line had asked whether a candidate knew the bounds. The new line asked whether the candidate could carry a finding past a wall by inference and leave the wall unbreached and make the inference stand on the ground below it, and she wrote beneath it the means a board would use to test the thing, a constructed record with a category sealed inside it and a finding that lay above the seal, and the candidate set to reach the finding without the means to open the seal, and the board reading the candidate's work for the place the reach had been made. The work she had done on her knees in the spring, blind, by instinct and forty years of habit, she was setting down now as a thing that could be taught and graded and required, so that the next researcher who hit a wall under a priority docket with a clock running would have been made to practice the discipline before the day came that needed it.

The slot window climbed past noon and the light came off the wall and the keeper outside drew the mop along the corridor and the wet trail of it carried the wax smell in under the door, and Asha wrote, and she did not break for the meal, and by the middle of the afternoon she had the spine of it. It was not finished. A standard was never finished in a day and this one would go to the bound shelf only after Kaviel had read it cold and after a board had run it against a live cohort and after the places it failed had been found and closed. But the spine held. She read it back against itself, hunting for the clause a careless hand would misread, and she found two and rewrote them, and she set the sheet at the head of the candidate files she had pushed aside, because the four candidates of the spring cohort would be the first the new line tested, and they would not thank her for it, and that was not the work's concern.

---

Meret wrote the incident review in the small administrative office at the end of the records corridor, where the light was bad and the work was the kind of work that did not need light, and she wrote it slowly because she would not put a thing in it she was not sure of.

The review was hers because the review was always hers, the discipline of setting down what had happened in the order it had happened and against the record that proved it, so that the Quarter could read its own conduct after the fact and find where the conduct had been sound and where it had been lucky and tell the two apart. She had the channel logs of the registry morning, every line that had crossed the trays, the time of each and the hand that sent it and the hand that took it, and she had Silva's field set transmission and Asha's reply and the cover line Kaviel had written to the magister Oduya, and she had the registrar's ruling itself, copied to the Quarter under the standing arrangement, and she laid them in the order the morning had run and read the morning forward through them as a stranger would read it.

She did not write what she did not have. That was the discipline and it was the whole of her. There was a thing in the morning's record that drew the eye, the speed with which the courtier had been walked to the authenticating desk, the two hours from the gap opening to the seal set, and a faster hand would have written it down as proof of how close the faction sat to the court. Meret did not write it down. Silva's transmission gave her the time the man climbed the steps and the time he came down, and the times were facts and she set them in, but the meaning a watcher might hang on the times was not a fact, and Meret had held information until she was sure of it for so long that the holding was no longer patience in her, it was the shape of her mind. She wrote that the courtier had authenticated the petition at the eighth hour and a quarter and that the petition had been filed at the seventh, and she let the gap between the two hours stand on the page as a gap, unfilled, for a reader with standing to draw the conclusion she would not draw without the proof of it.

The thing she would not leave out was the gap in the Quarter's own conduct, because a review that recorded only the things done well was a review that taught nothing. She wrote the place plainly. The dangerous passage between the gathering's end and the authority's entry had been known to Kaviel a week before the morning and could not be closed, and the Quarter had built replies against a challenge it could not yet see, and the replies had held, and the holding had rested on Mokoena failing to swear an oath the Quarter had no way to prevent him swearing. She wrote that the morning had turned, in part, on the conscience of a man four hundred miles south whom the Quarter did not control and could not have controlled, and that a review which credited the Quarter with the whole of the outcome would be a review that had not read the morning honestly. The reply had been built well. The reply had also been built in the hope that a thing outside the Quarter's reach would break the right way, and it had, and the next time it might not, and the review existed to say so before the next time came.

She wrote into the dim, the bad light no trouble to her, the smell of the bound logs close around the small room and the dry rasp of the old channel paper under her hand as she turned it, and outside the door the corridor carried the sounds of the compound running, a board sitting somewhere down the east wall, the keepers moving the day's carts, the low continuous murmur of an institution at its ordinary work. She finished the body of it by the end of the second day and set it aside to cool, because a review read back warm was a review the writer agreed with too easily, and she would read it cold in the morning and find the place she had been generous to the Quarter and harden it.

---

Silva came back from the field on the fourth day, off the night transit, and she came into the Quarter the way she came into everywhere, without anyone marking the hour of it until she was already inside.

She had been three weeks at the spinward edge on the work that followed the striking, watching the thin accounts where the resource would have run if the order had failed, confirming with her own eye that the resource had not run, because a thing proven on paper in the investigation room was a thing the field still had to walk to be sure of. The order had held. The road out had been closed before the cover came off and the short had shown into a closed room and the resource sat where it had sat for seven years, frozen now under the second instrument and waiting for the court, and Silva had stood in the cold of three river towns and watched the accounts not move and sent up the line each night that they had not moved, and now she was back, and the field was off her the way the field came off her, slowly, over a day, as the watchfulness she wore in the open settled back down into the watchfulness she wore at home.

She went first to the assessment office, because Asha kept the field rotations and a field officer reported her return to Asha before she reported it to anyone, and she stood in the doorway with the satchel still on her shoulder and the road still on her, the smell of engine exhaust and other people's tobacco worked into the coat, and Asha looked up from the standard sheet and read her the way Asha read a returning officer, for the things the officer would not say.

"They held," Silva said.

"I know they held. You sent it up." Asha set the stylus down. "Sit. You came off the night transit and you are standing in my doorway as though you mean to turn around and walk back to it."

Silva sat. She put the satchel down between her feet and she did not take the coat off, which Asha marked and did not name, because an officer three weeks in the open did not shed the open in a single doorway and pushing her to it would only teach her to perform the shedding. They went through the field rotation the way they always did, the accounts and the watch positions and the faces Silva had logged and the one face she had logged twice because it had been at two towns it had no reason to be at, and Asha took the twice-logged face and set it where it belonged, which was into the running record of who the faction had left in the field after the reach was cut, the cleanup the other side was running now, the same as the Quarter ran cleanup, and Silva's twice-logged face was a thread for another week.

"There is a board sitting," Asha said, when the rotation was done. "Down the east wall. The spring cohort, second stage. You have earned past sitting it. You will read the new line before the next field rotation, because the next researcher I send to a river town is going to be held to it." She slid the standard sheet across the desk, the revision, the spine of it. "Read it now. Tell me where it fails."

Silva read it. She read it the way a field officer read a thing written by a desk officer, looking for the place the standard asked of a candidate a thing that was clean on paper and impossible in the open, and she found one, the means of the test, the constructed record with the sealed category, and she said that a candidate who learned the discipline on a constructed record would learn to find the seal the board had built and not the seal the world hid, because the world did not band its walls in red for the convenience of the reader. Asha took it. It was the thing she had half-seen and not had the field in her to name, and she wrote it into the margin of the sheet in the cramped hand, that the constructed record must hide its seal the way a true record hid it, without a marker, so that the candidate found the wall by reading and not by looking for the place the board had put it, and she set the sheet back at the head of the cohort files with the margin note standing.

"Go and sleep," she said. "You are no use to me with the transit still on you."

Silva picked up the satchel and rose, and at the door she stopped, because a thing had occurred to her on the steps coming up that she had meant to say and the rotation had pushed out of her head. "The girl," she said. "Adimu. She is still in the room?"

"She is still in the room."

"She did the climb." Silva had seen it the once, the five-column wall, the day she came down to the investigation room before the spinward rotation, and she had stood at the foot of it the way a person stood at the foot of a thing built well by someone younger than the building should have allowed. "The whole body of it, up the wall."

"She did the climb," Asha said.

Silva took that, and nodded, and went to sleep, and Asha went back to the standard sheet, and the matter of the girl who had done the climb sat now in two heads where it had sat in one.

---

The commendation came down from the compound's own board, not from the court, because the court did not commend a junior researcher it had never been told the name of, and the Quarter did not let the court hold the only record of what its people had done.

Asha drafted it and Kaviel signed it, and that was the order it was done in, because the assessment was Asha's and the standing was Kaviel's to confirm, and between them the thing was made the way the Quarter made things, on paper, exact, with the citation set down in language a board would read in twenty years and understand. Asha wrote the citation against the record and not against the feeling, the dates and the method and the finding, the reconciliation-trace method learned in the autumn and carried through the winter and run blind across seven years of accounts to lay bare the body of the second mechanism, and she wrote that the finding had made the case prosecutable and that the case had stood, and she did not write that the girl had done it on the third day with the brightness gone glassy in her eyes and had held the size of it in her head and not on the front of her face, because that was not a thing a citation recorded, and it was the thing Asha would carry about her longest.

They did it in the investigation room, which surprised the girl, because the room was a working room and not a room where things were marked, and that was the point of doing it there. The room had been struck down to half its size in the days since the case closed, two of the three long tables carried back to storage, the five-column climb taken off the wall and bound and sent to the court under seal, the disbursement registers returned to the offices they had been pulled from, so that what remained was the crosswise table at the head and the bare north wall and the cold that banked in the corners and the engine clicking above, the room going back to what it had been before the case had made it more. Zola was there because Asha had sent her down to begin the close-out of the materials, the dull careful work of accounting for every sheet that had passed through the room, and she was at the table with the inventory when Kaviel came in, and Asha behind her, and the girl stood, because she stood when a senior came in and had not stopped.

"Sit, or stand, as you like," Kaviel said. "This will not take long, and it is not a board."

Zola stood.

Kaviel had the citation in her hand, the single folded sheet, and she did not read it out, because the Quarter did not perform its commendations and Kaviel performed nothing. She set it on the table under the bureau clock where the case had sat the day it was complete, and she said the thing the sheet said in fewer words.

"The board has confirmed you to full standing. As of the date on that sheet you are a Lioness of the Quarter, full charter, not a researcher carried under a senior's mandate. Your name goes on the roll in your own right." She paused, and what she did next she did because the girl had earned the saying of it and not because Kaviel was a woman who said such things easily. "Zola Adimu. The roll will carry the whole of it, both names, because the work you did is going to be cited in matters that outlast everyone in this room, and a citation that a court reads in twenty years does not say *the junior researcher*. It says the name. You will be in the record, by name, as the hand that found the body of the thing. I wanted you to hear it from me before you read it off a sheet."

The girl took it the way she had been taught to take a finding, the work behind the eyes and the front of the face held still, and the holding broke a little at the edges and she let it, because she was among the people she had earned the right to let it break in front of, and she did not say anything for a moment, and then she said the thing a Lioness said, which was not thanks.

"Where do you want the close-out," she said. "By jurisdiction, or by the order the sheets came in."

"By jurisdiction," Asha said, from the door. "And then index it both ways, because the court will want it one way and we will want it the other." She came in and looked at the half-struck room and the bare wall where the climb had been, and she put her hand for a moment flat on the crosswise table, the way she put her hand on a thing she was setting in its place, and she took it off. "You will run the next intake board's research module. You are full charter now and full charter teaches. I have a revision to the standard that needs a hand who has worked a live case to run it the first time. That is yours."

Zola looked at her.

"You found the wall and you did not cross it," Asha said. "You did it because Kaviel told you the bounds and you held them, which is the easy half. The hard half is that you built the climb so it never needed the crossing. You did that without being taught it, because the patience was already in you. I am going to teach the next one to do it on purpose, and you are going to help me, because you are the proof that it can be done." She picked up the inventory sheet and read the head of it and set it down. "By jurisdiction. Index both ways. I want it by the end of the week."

"By the end of the week," Zola said.

Kaviel left the citation on the table under the clock and did not watch the girl pick it up, because watching her pick it up would have made the picking-up a thing, and it was not a thing, it was a sheet of paper that said what the work had been. She went out, and Asha stayed to set the close-out running, and Zola Adimu, full charter, by name on the roll, drew the inventory to the table edge and began to account for every sheet that had passed through a room that was going back to what it had been.

---

Kaviel came through the compound a last time at the end of the week, between one thing and another, the way she came through any place she was responsible for and not occupying, and she went where she went when she came through, which was the rooms where the work lived and not the offices where it was administered.

She read the new standard off the bound shelf where Asha had set it after the cohort board had run it once and found two places it failed, both closed now, the sheet gone from a revision to a standard in five days the way a thing went when the people working it did not stop. She read the incident review in the small office with the bad light, Meret at the desk turning the cold copy for the third time, and she read the place Meret had written that the morning had turned in part on the conscience of a man the Quarter did not control, and she set the review down and said only that it was honest, which from Kaviel was the whole of an approval, and Meret took it and went back to the page. She put her head into the assessment office where Silva, slept-out now and the field off her, sat across the desk from Asha working the next rotation, and she did not interrupt it, because a rotation being worked was a thing that did not want interrupting, and she went on.

She ended where the case had ended, in the half-struck investigation room, where Zola had the close-out laid out by jurisdiction along the crosswise table and indexed both ways and very nearly done, and she stood in the doorway a moment and read the room the way she read any room she meant to leave, the bare north wall, the two tables gone to storage, the cold in the corners, the engine clicking above, the girl at the table accounting for the last of the sheets, the room a store again in everything but the smell, which would take another season to leave it. The case was bound and under seal at the court. The mechanism was frozen and waiting. The people who had run it were charged or fled or, in one office four hundred miles south, sitting at a pale-edged desk waiting for an instruction that was never going to come. The Quarter had read its own conduct and found the place it had been lucky and written the luck down so it would not be mistaken for skill. The standard sat on the bound shelf where Asha had set it. The girl was on the roll under her own name. The work had come back to the rhythm it ran at between the things that asked everything of it, the rhythm it had kept before the case opened, the keepers moving the day's carts down the corridor and a board sitting somewhere down the east wall.

Asha had come to the door of the assessment office down the corridor, the rotation set, and she stood in it with a sheet in her hand and watched Kaviel reach the outer door of the compound where the corridor gave onto the yard and the gray light came in off the stone.

Kaviel did not turn at the door. She had her hand on the frame of it and the yard light on her and she stood half a breath, and then she said it, low, into the corridor behind her, to the woman she knew was standing there because she always knew.

"Good work."

Then she went out, into the yard, and the door drew shut on its own weight behind her. Asha stood a moment longer and watched the door where it had closed. Then she looked down at the sheet in her hand, the next candidate, the second-stage board due within the week, and she went back into the assessment office and sat at the desk and pulled the chair in and opened the file and read the first line.
