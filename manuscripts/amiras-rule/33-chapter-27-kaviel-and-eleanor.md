# Chapter 27 — Kaviel and Eleanor

The corridor outside the office had not changed in any way Kaviel could name, and she had walked it perhaps a dozen times across her life, always on business, always with a folder in her hand or a report shaped in her head, and she walked it now with neither, which was the thing that had changed. She had sent no notice ahead. Sefu had let her into the administrative wing at the side door without asking why she had come, which told her he had been told to expect her, or had decided on his own that she would, and with Sefu the two were hard to tell apart. He had walked her as far as the turn and then stopped, the way he stopped at the edge of what was his to do, and said the door was open, and gone back the way they had come at the even pace he kept.

The west light was in the room when she came in. It lay low and long across the floor and up the leg of the writing table the way it had lain there for sixty years, because the window had been chosen for it, and Kaviel had read the room enough times in reports to know the window was chosen and had never once stood in the room while the light was doing what the reports described. It smelled of aged paper and cold tea and the dryness of a space that one body had occupied for longer than Kaviel had been alive, a smell that was almost nothing, that a stranger would have called the smell of no smell at all, and that Kaviel found she knew the way she knew her own hands.

Eleanor was sitting down.

That was the second thing, and it landed under the first before Kaviel had crossed the threshold. She had never seen her mother sit at the writing table. In every account of this room that had ever reached her, in the few times she had stood in its doorway, Eleanor stood at the table, or stood half-turned from the window with her back to the glare, or stood while a subordinate sat. The chair behind the table was a chair she kept for the look of the thing, a chair the room expected. Eleanor was in it now, settled into it, her hands resting on the arms, and the settling was not the settling of a woman taking a moment's ease between two efforts. It was the settling of a body that had decided where it would be for the afternoon because rising and crossing the room and standing had become a sum it preferred not to pay more than it had to.

"You found the side door," Eleanor said.

"Sefu found it for me."

"He would." Eleanor moved one hand off the arm of the chair, a small motion toward the visitor's chair that still saw use, the one that was not the one nobody had sat in for years, and Kaviel understood that her mother had kept the count of which chair was which even in this, even now, and would have been put out to have a guest take the wrong one. Kaviel took the chair that saw use. She sat the way she sat in any room she was reading, back straight, hands resting where they could be seen, and then she heard herself doing it and let her shoulders down a degree, because there was no one in this room to read her for it except the one person who had taught her the posture in the first place.

Eleanor watched her do it. The half-lidded eyes did not miss it. But she said nothing, and the not-saying was not the held attention Kaviel had spent her life reading off her, the silence pointed at a corner of a problem while the rest of the face rested. It was a quieter thing. It had no edge to it.

The cold tea sat at Eleanor's elbow where some earlier hour had left it, a skin on its surface. Kaviel had read about the cold tea too. She had thought, reading it, that it was a habit, a thing her mother did because she forgot the cup in the work. Looking at it now she saw it was older than habit. It was a cup poured by someone, by Sefu most likely, set down at the right hand because a right hand expected it there, and let go cold because the work that was supposed to interrupt itself to drink it had thinned out, and the cup had outlasted the reason for it.

"You ran the morning court from the colonnade," Eleanor said. "The Sennar petition."

"You were behind the screen. You'll have heard it better than I read it."

"I heard the throne hold the rulings and suspend the clause." Eleanor's voice was even, and lower than Kaviel remembered it, and she paced it differently now, leaving small rests inside the sentences where before there had been none. "She gave them the orchards and took the procedure. It was the right division. Most rulers would have thrown out the judgments to be seen punishing the appointments, and made an enemy of every litigant who had won fairly before a bench that was seated foully." A rest. "She did not. She is better at the work than the men who taught me used to say a throne could be at the work."

"She spent three weeks on the answer to that petition," Kaviel said. "She told me she knew it would come and she made herself ready for it. She does that with everything now. She prepares for the question before anyone has thought to ask it."

"I know she does." Eleanor's hand moved on the chair arm, the fingers settling. "I have watched her learn to do it. I will not watch much more of it."

She said it the way she said anything, without weight laid on it, a fact set on the record in the frame of facts, and Kaviel did not answer it, because there was no answer to it that would not have made it larger than her mother had made it, and because the term that governed the morning at the oversight chamber had taught her something about being in a room and saying nothing, and she carried what it had taught her into this room and let the sentence sit where Eleanor had put it.

The light moved on the floor. A building this old ran without ceasing, sixty years of it under this one roof, and the running came up faintly through the stone, the dim travel of a cart somewhere below in a service court, the closing of a far door, the wind finding the courtyard Eleanor never used and turning over in it and going. Kaviel had learned in the compound to hear a room. This room she could have heard with her eyes shut and named the hour by the angle of the cart-sound and the warmth of the air.

"The financial channels," Eleanor said. "Zola's skeleton held them."

"It held them. We took apart all three. The resources are pinned where the prerogative can reach them. Nothing fled." Kaviel said it plainly, the close of an operation in the fewest words it would take, the way she had been taught to close one, and she watched her mother receive it the way her mother received any report, once for the words and once for what they were built around.

"You taught the girl the trace yourself."

"I taught her the method. She found the rest. She found more of it than I would have, faster, because she did not yet know which parts were supposed to be hard." Kaviel paused. "Asha is confirming her to full standing. There's a commendation she doesn't expect."

"Good," Eleanor said. And then, after a rest, in the same even voice, "You teach the way I never managed to. You give them the method and then you let the work be theirs. I gave people debts and called it teaching. The debts aged out. The methods will not." Her eyes had moved to the window, to the square of glass and the dead light beyond it, and Kaviel could not have said whether her mother was looking at the courtyard or at nothing, and decided it did not matter which.

Kaviel had come without knowing what she had come to do. She knew it now, sitting in the chair that saw use, with the tea going colder at her mother's elbow and the light leaving the floor. She had not come to deliver a report, though she had delivered one, because reports were the channel they had, the gray dispatch paper and the fixed hand and the four sentences, and to come into the room and say nothing of the work would have been a stranger's silence between them, not theirs. She had come because there was an afternoon, and there had never before been an afternoon, and there would not be many more, and the arithmetic of that was the only arithmetic in the room that her mother had not laid out clean and stepped back from.

On the table, near Eleanor's right hand, past the cold cup, lay a flat hinged box of black document leather worn pale along the spine. Kaviel knew that case too. It was in the record. It had held a field report for eleven days once, years ago, in a calculation about a source whose name Kaviel had learned only this spring. It was empty now, or held something else now, and it lay closed under the moving light, and Eleanor's hand was near it without touching it, the way a hand rests near a thing it has stopped needing to open.

"You read my assessments," Kaviel said. She had not planned to say it. It came up out of the room.

Eleanor's eyes came back from the window. "I read everything."

"The probationary ones. The ones Asha marked low."

"I read those." A rest. "You appealed three. You were right on two." The faintest movement at the corner of her mother's mouth, not a smile, the place a smile would have been on a face that had spent sixty years declining to spend itself on smiling. "You knew which two before the board did. That was the thing I read in them. Not the appeals. The knowing."

Kaviel sat with that. She had not known her mother had read them. She had assumed, the way she assumed everything she could not document, that the assessments had gone into the apparatus and been filed and that Eleanor saw the summaries the head of a house saw, the standings and the readiness counts, and not the probationary file of one officer who happened to carry the name. She had assumed it because the alternative was a thing she had no place to put. She had a place for it now, and it was not a comfortable place, and she did not reach for any of the things a person reaches for to make such a place comfortable, because her mother had not built her to reach for them and the building had held.

"You never said," Kaviel said.

"No." Eleanor's hand found the arm of the chair again. "It is not the kind of thing I said. You will have noticed."

"I noticed."

"I am not going to take it back now and call it a fault and ask you to think better of me for naming it late." The voice stayed even. "I did the work the way I did it. I am too old to revise the method to look kinder in the last light of it. You would see through the revision faster than anyone I ever trained, and you would be right to, and I did not raise you on the kind of paper that confesses one thing in order to hide another." A rest, longer than the others. "I read the assessments. I have read them all, for years. That is a fact. I am setting it down. What you do with a fact has never been mine to direct, which is the one part of your training I can take no credit for, because it was already in you when the house took you in, and I only declined to grind it off."

Kaviel did not answer. The not-answering was its own answer, and Eleanor would read it as one, and the reading would be correct.

The afternoon thinned toward its end. The bar of light had climbed off the table while they sat, the way it climbed every day, and slid down the table leg and onto the floor again, and was thinning now toward the window as the sun went behind the wing of the building. The room cooled by a degree that Kaviel felt on the backs of her hands. Somewhere below, the cart that had gone over an hour ago came back the other way, and the wind in the courtyard had dropped, and the dryness of the air carried the cold tea now, the thin bitter edge of it, and under that the leather of the empty case and the aged paper on the shelves that ran the wall to a ceiling Eleanor could no longer reach.

"Sit a while longer," Eleanor said. It was not a request, and it was not an order, and it was the nearest thing to either that she would put into the air. "You have somewhere to be. You always have somewhere to be. I built it into you." A rest. "It will keep an hour."

"It'll keep," Kaviel said.

So she stayed. They did not fill the staying with talk. Eleanor said, once, that the third map on the wall, the one a stranger could not read, the grid of cells each marked with a code, was a map of people and channels and debts, and that most of the cells were dead now, the people gone or aged out or no longer worth the keeping, and that she had not redrawn it because there was no longer a reason to and because she liked to look at the shape of a thing she had spent a life building even after the thing had stopped working, the way a person keeps a house she no longer fills. Kaviel looked at the map. She could not read it. She had spent her life learning to read pages until they confessed, and this page would not confess to her, because it was written in the one hand she had never been given the key to, and she found she did not mind that it would not. There were keys it was right not to be given.

Eleanor's eyes closed for a stretch in the middle of the afternoon, not asleep, or not in a way that asked to be treated as sleep, and Kaviel sat through it without moving, the way she had sat through the oversight chamber, doing the only thing the hour left her, which was to be in the room. When the eyes opened again Eleanor did not remark on having closed them, and Kaviel did not remark on it either, and the small mercy of that, the thing left unsaid because saying it would have made it a thing, passed between them and was the closest the afternoon came to tenderness, which was close enough, because neither of them had ever needed it nearer.

The light reached the case. It lay across the worn black leather and warmed it to brown for a moment, and the small brass clasp threw a thread of brightness onto the table, and then the sun dropped another degree behind the wing and the thread went out and the leather went back to black, and the room began to take the blue that came into it before the lamps.

Kaviel rose. She did it without announcing it, gathering herself out of the chair that saw use, and Eleanor watched her do it and did not move to rise with her, because rising was a sum she would not pay to see a daughter to a door her daughter knew the way out of.

"I'll send the morning report through the usual channel," Kaviel said.

"The gray paper," Eleanor said. "The fixed hand."

"The four sentences."

"I taught you the four sentences." Eleanor's hands rested on the arms of the chair. "Waste no fifth on reassurance. I told you that when you were nineteen. You have never once wasted the fifth." A rest. "Do not start now. I would not know what to do with it, and it would only mean you had begun reading me as someone to be managed, and I have had enough of being managed by people who love me to do it, of whom there have been fewer than you would think, and you are not going to become one of them in the last of it."

It was the only time in the afternoon either of them had set the word near the surface, and Eleanor had set it there inside a refusal, wrapped it in the order not to soften, so that it could not be answered as the thing it was, and Kaviel understood the wrapping for what it was, which was the only way her mother had ever had of carrying it, and she did not unwrap it. She let it lie in the blue light the way she had let everything lie.

"All right," Kaviel said.

She crossed to the door. She put her hand on it. The leather of the old hinge was cold under her palm and the corridor beyond breathed up faintly with the smell of stone and the wax the household rubbed into the wood, and she stopped there, her back to the room, her hand on the door, and did not turn. Behind her she could hear the small sounds of the room and her mother in it, the creak of the chair as a weight shifted in it, the cup not lifted, the building running on under the stone the way it had run for sixty years and would run after. She stood a moment longer than the leaving needed. Then she opened the door and went out into the corridor and let it close behind her, and her footsteps went away down the passage at an even pace.

In the office the blue light deepened toward dusk. Eleanor De Grosvenor sat in the chair she had never used until this season, her hands on its arms, the cold tea at her elbow and the empty case near her right hand, and she did not call for the lamps. She turned her head to the window. The square of glass held the last of the day going gray over the courtyard she had not walked through in a decade, the dead fountain, the bench nobody used, and she looked at it, and the light went out of it by degrees, and she watched it go.
