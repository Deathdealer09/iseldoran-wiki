# Chapter 24 — Amira Acts

The oversight chamber had been built to hear the institution judge itself, and everything in it served that single purpose. It sat on the third floor of the Hall of Establishments, reached by a stair of worn imperial stone, and its proportions were the proportions of a room that wanted no one in it to feel larger than the procedure. The ceiling was low for so formal a space, a flat coffered field of dark wood that pressed the sound downward and held it. The arbiter's bench ran across the head of the room, plain heavy timber gone the color of strong tea with a century of oil and handling, and before it stood the two tables for the parties, set at a width measured so that neither could be said to face the bench from the place of advantage. The benches for the few who were permitted to attend ran along the side walls, narrow, hard, made for sitting upright. High along the courtyard side a row of clerestory windows let in the angled light of late afternoon, and the light fell in bars across the floor and across the empty center of the room and warmed nothing, because the chamber was stone under its wood and held the cold of the building the way the whole Hall held it.

They had come back into the chamber to hear the arbiter set the date of the formal review, and that was all the morning had been meant to be. The proceeding against Eleanor De Grosvenor had run its first session the day before and adjourned without resolution, the complaint laid, the answer given, the matter taken under the arbiter's hand to be weighed in the slow time such weighing took. Eleanor sat at the table on the left, where the answering party sat, with the cane standing against the table's edge and her hands folded over the head of it, and she had not poured the water set out at her elbow. Kaviel stood behind her at the wall, in the place the proceeding allowed her, present and not permitted to speak, with the stillness on her that she had worn through the whole of the first session, a stillness that gave a watcher nothing and that Eleanor did not turn to read because she did not need to read it.

Arbiter Sabel Doumbia sat at the bench with the file of the proceeding squared before her. She was a spare woman of late middle years, dark and close-cropped and unhurried, who had conducted oversight reviews for two decades and conducted them the same way each time, as a body of work the parties passed through rather than a contest she presided over. She had heard the complaint without leaning toward it and heard the answer without leaning toward it, and she had adjourned the first session on a clean notation and meant to open this one the same way, with the setting of a date and the assignment of the file to formal review. She had drawn the breath to speak it.

The chamber doors opened at the foot of the room.

They opened without the announcement that preceded any ordinary arrival, because the household that would have made the announcement had been left below, and so the first thing the room knew of it was the sound, the two leaves of the door drawn back on their hinges and the fall of footsteps on the stone that did not belong to any clerk of the establishment. Doumbia did not finish the breath. The few on the side benches turned. And the Empress of the Trinitarian Throne came up the center of the chamber, alone at the front of it, in the formal robes of the dais and not the working robes she wore to the rooms without windows, and behind her at the precise distance the throne kept walked two she had brought and no more.

The room changed as she crossed it. It was a thing that happened in the sound of the place before it happened in anything a person could point to, the low held hush of the coffered ceiling shifting under the weight of who had entered it, the way a struck bell changes the air of a room it is rung in. The bars of clerestory light lay across the floor and the Empress walked through them and the dust turned in the disturbed air behind her. She did not look to the side benches. She did not look to the table on the left where Eleanor sat. She came to the open center of the chamber, before the bench and between the two tables, and she stopped there, and the footsteps behind her stopped, and the chamber that had been built to hold the institution judging itself held instead the arrival of the authority the institution answered to, and the difference in it was the difference between a room and the same room with the floor dropped out from under its assumptions.

Doumbia rose. She rose without haste, because haste was not in her, and she inclined her head the depth the bench gave the Throne, and she remained standing, because no one sat at a bench while the Empress stood before it.

"Majesty," Doumbia said. "This is a proceeding under oversight review. It is adjourned to a date not yet set." She said it as a fact the Empress would wish established, the standing of the room, the authority under which it sat, the place in its own process it had reached. She did not say more. She had conducted reviews for twenty years and she had never had a sovereign come up the center of her chamber, and she set the fact of the proceeding down between them the way a careful officer sets down the ground before anything is built on it.

"I know what it is," Amira said. "I have read the complaint. I have read the answer. I have read the notation under which you adjourned it yesterday, and the authority under which this chamber sits, and the schedule of the review you were about to set." Her voice carried in the low room without lifting, pitched for the coffered ceiling that pressed sound down, and it reached the side benches and the back of the chamber and the table on the left without seeming to reach for them. "I have come to remove the matter from this chamber's hands. I will tell you the ground of it, and the instrument, and the body it goes to, and you will enter what I give you on the record of your proceeding, because the record is yours to keep and what I am doing belongs in it."

Behind her, the two she had brought stood at the distance. One was a clerk of the Throne who carried the second sealed copy of the instrument and would enter the hour of it on the Throne's own record. The other was a senior Lioness operative in the plain dark of the Quarter, a tall woman with a soldier's stillness who had come to witness and to be seen to witness, Meret, who carried the incident as the house would record it and whose presence in the chamber meant that what passed here passed under the eye of the apparatus and would be held in its account. She did not move and did not speak. She stood where the Empress had placed her and watched the room the way Silva watched a periphery, and the watching was the function.

Amira drew from within the formal robe a single folded sheet. The room knew the stock of it. It was the stock the Throne used for instruments that bore no scribe's countersignature, heavy and unmarked, and she had carried it folded along its single square crease since she left the quarters two levels below the records floor, and she unfolded it now in the bars of light and did not read from it, because she had written it in her own hand and did not need to read it, and she held it open so the bench could see that it was written and sealed.

"The matter this chamber was convened to hear," she said, "is a complaint against the head of the De Grosvenor house. Abuse of intelligence authority. Surveillance of a senior official without sanction. Procedural violation in the conduct of an inquiry. The complaint is partially true, and the arbiter has weighed it as such, and the arbiter would have weighed it honestly, because the arbiter is honest, and that is not the difficulty." She let the sentence set. "The difficulty is the senior official. The complaint asks this chamber to judge the conduct of an inquiry into the Marshal of the Bureau of Establishments. This chamber sits under the Hall of Establishments. The review it would assign runs through offices the marshal's authority reaches. The instrument that brought the complaint was drawn in the establishment and authenticated in the establishment, and the establishment is the thing the inquiry was inquiring into. A proceeding cannot judge the inquiry into the body the proceeding belongs to. It can only protect that body, whether it means to or not, and this one, run to its end, would protect it."

Doumbia stood very still at the bench. She had conducted reviews for twenty years and she had read the shape of this one and logged the shape of it privately and gone on conducting it because conducting it was her office, and she heard now the thing she had not let herself name laid open in the center of her own chamber by the only authority that could lay it open and survive the laying. She did not protest it. She was not built to protest a true thing. She inclined her head a fraction, the smallest motion, granting the ground.

"I invoke the emergency prerogative of the Throne," Amira said.

The words went into the low room and the low room held them, and on the side benches a man who knew the establishment well enough to know what the words meant drew a breath he did not finish, and the small involuntary sound of it was loud in the pressed quiet. The prerogative had not been spoken aloud in a chamber of the establishment in the memory of anyone sitting in it. It was the instrument that ran above the establishment because it answered to no desk within it, the authority of the Empress alone to suspend the ordinary process of the imperial body, and it had lain unused for thirty-one years, long enough that the men who governed under it had come to govern as though it were not there, as though the ordinary process were the whole of the reach of the Throne, and the speaking of it now told every person in the chamber that the ordinary process had been judged unequal to the thing it was meant to correct and had been set aside by the one hand that could set it aside.

"Under the prerogative," Amira said, "I suspend this proceeding. Not its record, which stands, and not its arbiter, whose conduct I have no fault to find in. The proceeding. The complaint against the head of the De Grosvenor house, and the matter it arises from, and the inquiry it sought to judge, are removed from the oversight authority of the establishment as of the entering of this notation." She turned the open sheet a degree toward the bench. "I have constituted a Throne Court of Inquiry. It sits under the prerogative, outside the Hall of Establishments, outside the reach of any office implicated in the matter it will hear. The complete evidentiary record of the inquiry is lodged with it, the appointments and the financial mechanism beneath them and the reach of the office that protected both, documented and sealed three days ago. The Marshal of the Bureau of Establishments is subject to its jurisdiction from this hour. So is every officer named in the record. So is the conduct of the inquiry itself, which the court will examine on the same footing as the conduct it was inquiring into, because a court that will not examine its own instrument is the thing I am dismantling and I will not build another."

She came forward to the bench and laid the sheet on the timber before Doumbia, squared to the edge the way the house squared everything, and she did not let go of it at once. She held it a moment under her flat hand on the worn dark wood, the way a person rests a hand on a thing before she commits it, and the bars of light from the clerestory lay across the back of her hand and across the heavy unmarked stock with its seal pressed into the foot of it that no other hand in the imperium could press. Then she lifted her hand, and the instrument lay on the bench, entered, and the lifting of the hand was the act that could not be unwrought, and she had laid each stroke of it knowing that, two levels below the records floor, in the even shadowless light, and she did not flinch over the laying of it here any more than she had flinched over the writing of it there.

"It is irreversible," she said, to Doumbia, and to the chamber through Doumbia, because the chamber needed to hear the cost said plainly by the one who carried it. "An act under the prerogative cannot be appealed within the establishment, because it does not arise within the establishment. It can be judged only by the reign that follows mine, reading the record I have made, deciding whether the Empress who set aside the ordinary process had cause. I have made the record so that the cause is in it. I have not made it so that the judgment is easy. If there is support behind the marshal that the inquiry did not map, support in this Hall or above it, this is the hour it shows itself, because this is the hour the protection runs out and the men who relied on it must decide whether to be seen relying on it still. I have come into the open to find out. I would rather find out here, in a chamber with a record, than in the dark where the establishment prefers its difficulties settled."

The chamber held. No one rose from the side benches. The man who had drawn the unfinished breath sat with his hands flat on his knees and looked at the floor where the light lay, and the others sat as he sat, and the stillness that came over them was the stillness of people doing the arithmetic the Empress had set them, the count of which way the weight had moved and whether any of them had a reason to be seen moving against it, and none of them did, or none would own to it in a room where the Throne stood in the open and the house watched from the wall. The thing the faction had built had depended on the ordinary process running quietly through offices it reached. The Empress had taken the matter out of the ordinary process and into the light of a court that reached nothing the faction held, and there was no procedural seam left to work, no half-hour to be raced, no challenge to be authenticated in a registry, because the prerogative answered to no registry. The elegant fast moves were spent. What remained was the record, lodged with a court the marshal could not touch, and the marshal subject to it, and the chamber full of men who understood that the ground had shifted under the establishment and that the shift had been made by the one authority entitled to make it and entered on a record their successors would read.

Doumbia looked down at the sheet on her bench. She read it once, the whole of it, in the unhurried way she read everything, and what she read was sound. The invocation was within the prerogative. The constitution of the court was within the prerogative. The transfer was clean and the record was lodged and the cost was the Empress's and stated in the Empress's own hand. There was nothing in it for an arbiter to weigh, because the arbiter's authority ended where the prerogative began, and the prerogative had begun. She squared the sheet to the file of her own proceeding, joining the two, the record of the chamber and the instrument that closed it, and she set her hand flat on the joined papers as the Empress had set hers, marking the entering of it on the proceeding she kept.

"It is entered," Doumbia said. "The proceeding is suspended on the authority of the Throne. The matter passes to the court named in the instrument. The record of this chamber notes the hour and the authority and the transfer, and the record will say that the proceeding was conducted to its adjournment without fault found in any party before it, and that the transfer arises from the authority of the prerogative and not from any failing of this chamber, because that is the truth of it and the record I keep keeps the truth." She inclined her head to the bench's full depth. "Majesty. The chamber is yours to close."

Amira looked at her a moment, and there was in the looking the nearest the room would come to acknowledgment, one honest officer marking another across a thing that had cost them both something to do correctly. Then it passed, and the Empress was only the Empress in a suspended chamber with the light going long across the floor.

She had not looked at the table on the left through any of it. She looked at it now. Eleanor De Grosvenor sat where the answering party sat, her hands folded over the head of the cane, and she had not moved through the whole of it, had not poured the water, had not turned to read Kaviel at the wall behind her, and she met the Empress's look across the open center of the chamber and held it. Nothing passed between them that a watcher could have named. There was no word, no nod, no softening on either face. There was the old measuring, the two of them taking each other's measure across a room the way they had taken it across the low table in the small reception room with the unwatched garden window, the house and the throne reading each other once more because the reading changed and a year-old reading was a dangerous thing to govern by. Eleanor had walked into the neutral room alone and spent the trust forward and written the cost into her own column, and the Empress had been bound by it after the fact the way the compact was built to bind her, and had carried the matter into the open and set aside the ordinary process and made herself answerable for it to the reign that would follow. The house had laid the table. The Throne had decided what was eaten from it. Neither of them said so, because neither of them needed to, and the not-saying was the compact operating as it had operated for three generations, trust spent and obligation taken up, the whole of it present in a look across a chamber and none of it on the record but the instrument on the bench.

Amira turned from the table. She turned without hurry and without a backward glance at the woman she had just bound her reign to defend, because the look had been the whole of it and a second look would have been less. She crossed the open center of the chamber toward the doors at the foot of it, through the bars of clerestory light, and Meret fell in behind her at the distance the house kept, and the chamber that had held the institution judging itself held now the Empress leaving it under her own authority with the matter taken up into her own hand.

At the foot of the chamber Eleanor rose. She rose on the cane, slower than she had risen a year before, using it now where she had held it ready, and Kaviel came off the wall and stood beside her, and the two of them turned to follow the Empress out, because the proceeding was closed and there was no reason left to stay in a chamber that no longer held them. Doumbia stood at her bench above the joined papers. The few on the side benches sat where they had sat.

Amira went out through the doors and into the corridor where the light came in from the windows over the freight terraces, and Eleanor and Kaviel came behind her, the Empress ahead and the head of the house and her daughter following, the distance between the three of them held exact, the length of the corridor running out before them in the long afternoon light, the loading engines below sending up through the stone the low vibration that never stopped.
