# Chapter 16 — Message Trace

The room ran in daylight now, and Kaviel had not yet decided whether she liked what the daylight did to it.

For the weeks since she had opened it the north room had been a night room, a single lamp and two people and the cold of the deep masonry, and the work in it had been quiet enough that she could hear a stack squared at the far end of the table. That room was gone. The store-smell had gone with it, beaten out at last by the bodies and the warm air and the lamp oil burned every night, and what filled the room at the second hour of the morning was the sound of a thing at full use. Five people where there had been two. The long table pushed against the north wall to clear the floor, and the floor given over to the racked relay logs Ferran had asked for, the banded volumes set up on their ends in the wooden cradles he had built so that a reader could walk the line of them and pull any one without disturbing its neighbors. The high window gave its flat gray light across the near half of the room and left the racks at the dim end in the shadow they kept all day, and Kaviel had set a second lamp down there, against her own habit, because the daylight that had once been enough for two was not enough for five.

She stood in the doorway a moment before she went in, the way she did with any space she meant to work from, and read it. Asha at the table's near end with the financial side drawn up beside her, the disbursement registers she would not let out of her own keeping. Zola at the racks, slower than anyone and right where the others were fast, working a relay log against a list in her own ruled hand. A second researcher Asha had given up to the room, a careful man named Otieno who did not yet know what he was working and had not asked. And Ferran, at the table's far end, where Kaviel had put him on the first day so that the dim end and the racks were his and the near end with the maps and the names was hers.

Tobias Ferran was the thing in the room she had not built and could not unmake, and she had spent the first day of having him deciding how much of the room he would be allowed to see and the rest of the days holding to it. He was a records-systems specialist out of the transit administration, contracted to the Quarter through a billing line that read as building maintenance, and he had come because the trace needed a thing no Lioness in the compound knew how to do, which was to read the routing record of a private courier message back through the relay points it had passed. He was good. She had known he was good within the first hour, the way she had known Zola was patient, and she had not let the knowing soften the other fact about him, which was that he sat outside the chain. Everyone else in the room had been read and held and made by the Quarter over years. Ferran had been hired. He did not know the names. He did not know the shape. He knew the routing problem she had brought him, and he believed it was a billing fraud in the courier service, a thing of stolen franking and misposted relay fees, because that was what she had told him it was and she had built the cover well enough that the work he did on it was true work and not a lie he could trip over.

"Where are we," she said, coming to the near end of the table.

"Two of three." Ferran did not look up from the log under his hand. He spoke the way he worked, flatly, with the figures first. "The message that went to your registrar in Adwa came in to the office courier service off the private line, not the registry line. Private matter, hand-addressed, no office mark, the franking paid against a standing account. That account is the first point. I have it."

"You have the account."

"I have the account number and the relay it franks through. The account is held in a service name, a forwarding office in Adwa that takes private correspondence for clients who would rather the registry not see it. Lawful. Common. The forwarding office is a wall, not a window, it does not keep who its clients are, it keeps the account and the account pays. But the account is a thing, and a thing routes." He turned the log a quarter toward her, not so she could read it, he had learned in the first day that she did not read his logs, but so the gesture stood for the showing. "The forwarding office did not originate the message. It received it from somewhere and franked it on. Every message it franks carries the relay stamp of where it came in from, because the relay charges the forwarding office and the charge has to post against something. I read the relay stamps back. The forwarding office took your message in from a second relay, a transit hub two jurisdictions north, on the spinward line. That is the second point. I have that one too."

Kaviel set two fingers on the map at the near end of the table, on the spinward jurisdictions where Zola's dots ran across the border, and found the transit hub without being told where it was, because she had read the spinward line for other reasons in other weeks. The second relay sat where the spinward courier lines came together before they ran down toward the Outer Prefectures. North of it the lines thinned and went up toward the bureau seats. She did not say any of that.

"And the third," she said.

"The third is the one that matters and the third is slow." For the first time Ferran looked up, and his face had the small fixed attention of a man who has come to the part of a problem that is worth his time. "The transit hub does not keep client accounts the way the forwarding office does. It is a relay, it moves volume, it posts its charges in bulk against the lines that feed it and reconciles them at the fiscal turn. So I cannot read your message back through it by the account, because at the hub your message is not a message, it is one franking among forty thousand in a posting batch. I have to find the batch your message rode in, and then find what fed the batch, the line that brought that volume into the hub on that day. That is in the hub's own relay reconciliation, and that is what I am pulling now, and it is in those." He nodded at the racks at the dim end, where Zola and Otieno worked. "Three days of the hub's relay reconciliation either side of your message's date. The line that fed the batch is in there. When I have the line, I have the third point, and the third point is where your message entered the courier system to begin with. Whoever wrote it handed it to that line. That is your origin."

"How long."

"Today, if the racks hold what I asked for. Tomorrow if they pulled it short." He went back to the log. "The hub keeps it. The hub keeps everything, it has to, the whole system bills off it. The only question is whether your clerks pulled three days or pulled one and called it three."

"They pulled three," Asha said from the near end, without looking up from her registers, and Ferran took that the way he took most things she said, which was as a fact he did not have standing to argue, and said nothing.

Kaviel left them to it and went down the room to the racks.

This was the part of the trace she had thought about most, the reading of it once he handed her the line. The routing of it was Ferran's and beyond her. She had run the shape of it in her head on the walk down from her own office that morning. The first point was a service name behind a forwarding office, a wall. The second point was a relay batch in a transit hub, a number. Neither of them was a hand. But the third point, the line that fed the message into the system, was the closest thing the message would ever bring her to the hand that wrote it, because a man who sent a private warning did not walk it to a public forwarding office himself, he handed it to a line he trusted, and the line he trusted ran from somewhere, and the somewhere was an office. Ferran would give her a line. She would have to give the line a place.

Zola straightened from the rack when Kaviel came down, and did not speak, because the room in daylight was a room where things were said in front of Ferran and Otieno and the rule was that nothing was said in front of them that the cover did not cover. Kaviel had taught her that the first morning of the larger room, in the corridor, in four sentences, and Zola had set it where it belonged and kept it. She tilted the relay log she was reading so Kaviel could see the column she had reached, the franking volumes posted against the feeder lines, day by day, line by line, and she touched one figure with the back of her stylus and did not say what it was. Kaviel read it. The volume on one feeder line had spiked on the day before the message reached Adwa, a small spike, the kind a fiscal reconciliation would pass over as noise, and it had fallen back the day after. Zola had marked it because Zola marked the thing that did not fit the texture around it, which was the method, run now on relay volumes instead of carry-forward entries, the same eye for the place where order was too clean or too sudden.

"Good," Kaviel said, low, the one word, and Zola went back to the rack.

She stood a moment at the dim end with the second lamp throwing the racks' shadows up onto the cold stone, and the daylight from the high window not reaching this far, and she let herself feel the difference in the room. In the night room she had known where every page was. Here the work was distributed across five hands and two of the hands were not hers, and she could not hold the whole of it in her head at once the way she had held it when it was her and the girl and the cracked window. That was the cost of the room growing. She had weighed it before she let it grow. A trace through a transit hub's bulk reconciliation was not a thing two people did in a season, and she had needed Ferran and she had needed the racks and the racks had needed bodies to walk them, and so she had grown the room and accepted that the price of the speed was that the room could now be felt from outside, that five people working a thing left a wake where two had left none.

She was thinking exactly that, standing at the dim end with the lamp on the racks, when Ferran said her name.

He did not raise his voice. He had not raised his voice once in the days she had worked him. He said her name the way a man says a thing he has just read and is not yet sure of, and the not-sure in it was new, because Ferran was a man who said the figure first and the doubt never, and she came back up the room before he had said the second word.

"The third point is gone," he said.

She stood at the table's far end and did not touch the log. "Say it plainly."

"I found the line." He had the hub's reconciliation open under both hands now, two volumes of it, the spike day Zola had marked and the day before it. "The batch your message rode in fed off a single line, I have the line, it is a dedicated relay, not a public feeder, a closed line that runs up the spinward route toward the bureau seats and carries office matter for one subscriber. A closed line has a subscriber record. The hub has to keep it, the closed line is billed direct to the subscriber, the subscriber's office routes its private correspondence down it and the hub posts the charges back up it at the turn. The subscriber record is the third point. It is the office your message came from." He turned the second volume. "And the subscriber record is gone."

"Gone how."

"Suspended." He set his finger on a posting, and for the first time he looked at her instead of at the page, and his flat administrative face had the thing in it that she had brought him to read it without giving him: the recognition of a man who knows the machine and has watched it do a thing it is not supposed to be able to do quietly. "Three days ago the closed line was suspended at the subscriber's request and the subscriber record sealed pending audit. That is a lawful thing. A subscriber can suspend a closed line and seal its record if it suspects the line has been compromised, billing fraud, stolen franking, the kind of thing I came here to find. When a record is sealed pending audit, the hub closes it to all reading but the auditor's, and the auditor is named by the bureau the line answers to, and until the audit runs the record shows nothing but the seal. I can see that the line existed. I can see that it fed the batch. I cannot see the subscriber, because three days ago someone asked the hub to stop anyone seeing the subscriber, and the hub did what it is built to do, and sealed it."

The room had gone quiet behind her, the working quiet thinned rather than the full quiet of the night room, Zola's stylus stopped at the rack and Otieno's hand still on a volume he had been about to lift and Asha at the near end with her registers untouched under her hand, all of them not looking and all of them listening, because a thing had happened in the room and the discipline that held them from showing it was the discipline that left the room this quiet.

Kaviel read the posting. She did not need Ferran to read it for her now that he had told her where to look, and she read the date of the seal and the form of the request and the bureau named to run the audit, and she read it twice, the way Asha had taught her, looking past whether it was what Ferran said it was, hunting the place where it might be something else, and finding none. It was what he said it was. Three days ago, while her room ran the trace toward the third point in good order and believed itself unseen, an office on the far end of a closed line had reached down through a transit hub it subscribed to and sealed its own record against the very reading her room was four hours from making.

"Three days ago," she said.

"Three days ago." Ferran did not ask the question that sat under it, because he did not know there was a question, he thought the seal was a courier-fraud subscriber covering a billing exposure, and he said the next thing the way he would have said it to anyone. "It happens. A subscriber gets a fraud notice, panics, seals the line. The audit will open it in a quarter or two and then I can read it. I can put the request in to the bureau, but it will be slow and it will have my name on it and the bureau will want to know why a courier billing matter wants a sealed subscriber record. I would not put that in without your word."

"You will not put it in." She said it level, and she said it as the routing it was, an instruction to the contractor and not a thing he could read past. "Not under your name, not under the building line, not at all. The audit will run when it runs. We do not chase a sealed record through the bureau, because the bureau is the line we are reading and a request through the bureau is a thing the bureau reads. You have done the work I brought you for. You found the line. The line is the finding. Write me the line, the closed-line reference and the feed and the date of the seal, and write me nothing about the subscriber, because there is nothing to write, and then I want the racks logged back to where Otieno can return them by the channel without a question, and I want you off this by the end of the day."

Ferran looked at her a moment longer than he had looked at her in three days of work, and she watched him decide not to ask the thing he had begun to feel, the thing that did not fit a billing fraud, which was that a subscriber covering a stolen-franking exposure did not seal a closed line three days after a quiet reconciliation reader began walking the hub's batch posting toward it, that the timing of the seal was the timing of a thing that had been watched. He was good. He had felt it. He set it down, the way a contracted man who wanted to be contracted again set down the thing the client did not want him to have felt, and he drew a clean sheet and began to write the line.

Kaviel went back down the room to the dim end and stood at the racks where the daylight did not reach, and she put her hand flat on the rail of the nearest cradle, the worn wood Ferran had cut and joined in the first days, and she stood there in the second lamp's reach while the room behind her came back by degrees to its working sound, the stylus moving again at the next rack, Otieno lifting his volume, Asha's page turning at the near end, the building running on through the second hour of the morning the way it always ran while in this room five people sat at the edge of a thing that had just reached back and shut a door in their faces. She had built the room to move faster and the speed had cost her the wake she knew it would cost, and the wake had been read, and the reading had been answered before her own hand had finished reaching, and there was no part of that she had not weighed, except the part that no weighing prepared a person for, which was the flatness of it, the way it had come as a posting and not a blow, a lawful seal in a clean hand on a page, a door closed by a subscriber whose name she could not read and did not, this morning, need to read, because she knew whose office sat at the top of a closed line that ran up the spinward route toward the bureau seats, and the source had told it to her mother in a private residence outside the complex eleven days ago, and her mother had told her, and she carried it now where she carried everything she could not write.

She came back up the room.

The relay display Ferran had built ran on a frame at the near end of the table, a clean board where the trace stood mapped in three points and the lines between them, the forwarding office, the transit hub, and the closed line running up toward the third point that should by the day's end have carried a name and now carried a seal instead. Ferran had set the seal onto it before she reached the table, a small struck mark over the third point in the convention he used, the convention that meant a record reading no further, and the three points sat on the board with the road between them drawn and the end of the road closed, the trace gone dead on the display a hand's reach short of the office it had been climbing toward all week.

Kaviel watched it a moment.

"He knows we're looking."

Asha, beside her: "Then we move faster."
