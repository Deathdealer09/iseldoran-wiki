# Chapter 10 — The Administrator's Breakfast

The Bureau of Establishments' Outer Prefectures office had been awake since before the sun reached the city of Adwa, because the work it did had never depended on light. Sael Mokoena arrived at it the way he arrived every morning, on foot from the third terrace, through the lower gate the clerks used, into the long ground-floor corridor where the night registry was already handing off to the day. He liked the hour. He liked the building before it filled, when the great wheel of it turned slowly enough that a man could hear its parts. Somewhere above him a transcription engine clicked through a backlog, patient as rain on a roof. A floor-keeper was drawing a wide mop across the stone of the entrance hall, and the wet trail of it shone and faded behind her as the dry air took it back. The smell that met him at the door was the smell that had met him for nineteen years, lamp oil and old paper and the faint mineral cool of stone that the sun did not reach until midday.

He went first to the registry window, where Adisa had the night's intake stacked and banded.

"Morning, Deputy Registrar," she said, not looking up from the band she was tying. She was new, six weeks in the office, and she still used the full title where the others had let it wear down to nothing.

"Anything that won't keep until the session?"

"Two contests out of Lomé. A query from the Sennar registry about a folio reference, I put it on your desk. And the carry-forward schedule came up from reconciliation overnight, they want your initial before it goes to the assessors."

"Leave the schedule with me. The Lomé contests can go to the morning list." He took the banded query without breaking his stride toward the stair, and she went back to her tying, and that was the whole of it, a deputy registrar collecting his correspondence from a registry clerk who thought no more about him after he had passed than she thought about the door.

His office was on the second floor, at the end where the corridor narrowed and the windows looked down into the inner courtyard the building had been built around. He had earned the room by length of service and held it the same way. It was not large. A desk worn pale at the front edge where two generations of registrars had rested their forearms, a wall of pigeonholes he had organized himself and trusted no one to touch, a side table for the kettle and the small enamel pot he kept there. He set the night's correspondence down, lit the lamp though the window gave him enough to see by, because he liked the lamp, and put the water on.

Breakfast came up from the kitchen at the half-hour, as it had every working day since the kitchen had learned his habits. A boiled egg, two flat rounds of the dark prefectural bread, a wedge of the hard yellow cheese the south sent up by the crate, and the bitter red tea he had learned to drink in his first posting and had never afterward been able to do without. He ate the way he did most things, without haste and without pleasure shown, the egg first, the shell going into the small dish for the purpose, the salt from the twist of paper he kept in the drawer. The bread he tore rather than cut. He read while he ate, because the morning was the only time the office gave him to read without interruption, and what he read was the carry-forward schedule reconciliation had sent up.

It was a good schedule. He could tell that at a glance, the way a man who has built a thing many times can tell sound work from poor. Forty-one entries, each one citing a prior commission's authority and attested by an originating office, the dates set down in the order the provision required, the date of original confirmation cited and the date of present entry left to the clerk. Thirty-nine of the forty-one were exactly what they appeared to be, lawful appointments long delayed in the record, reconciled now because the backlog had at last reached them. He had reviewed thousands like them. The work of the office was real work, and most of it was honest, and the honesty of the most was the ground the rest stood on.

Two of the forty-one were his.

He did not look at them differently from the others. That was the discipline, and the discipline was the whole of the craft. He had learned long ago that a man who lingered over his own entries taught the page to betray him, that the eye of a later reader was drawn to wherever the writer's eye had lingered, and so he read his two the way he read the thirty-nine, no slower, no faster, his pen moving down the margin in the small even ticks that meant a deputy registrar had seen each line and found it in order. The two appointments were a magistrate's seat in a jurisdiction three prefectures east and a deputy assessor's post in a registry office no contest had ever troubled. Both cited a prior commission that had sat, that had kept minutes, that had confirmed appointments under exactly the authority now cited. The prior commission was real. Its authority was real. Only the two appointments had never been before it. He had given them the commission's authority the way a man borrows a true name to carry a false errand, and the errand passed because the name was good.

He initialed the schedule, all forty-one, and set it in the tray for the assessors, and finished his egg.

There was no part of this that felt to him like the thing the word for it would have made it. He had turned the word over once, years ago, alone, the way a man turns over a stone to see what it is, and had put it down again because it did not fit what he did at the desk in the early light. What he did at the desk was careful. It was patient. It harmed no one he could see, took nothing from anyone he could name, moved a few seats into hands he judged steadier than the empire's own indifferent procedures would have found for them, and did it inside the very provision the founding instrument of the commission had written for the purpose of moving things into the record without fresh review. The provision was the door. He had not built the door. He had only learned, better than the men who built it, where it led.

He poured the second cup of tea and let it cool while he opened the rest of the night's correspondence.

The Sennar query was nothing, a folio reference entered transposed, a clerk asking which of two numbers was right; he wrote the right one in the margin and set it for return. A circular from the Bureau's New Terra office on the standardization of attestation forms, which he read twice because anything that changed the forms could change what an attestation had to show, and put aside satisfied that it touched nothing of his. A note from the assessor Okonjo about the order of the morning session.

The last item in the band had no office mark.

It had come in as private correspondence, addressed to him by hand in a script he knew, routed through the courier service the office used for matter that did not go through the registry. He knew the hand because he had received perhaps a dozen such notes in four years, never more than a few words each, never signed. He broke the wax with his thumbnail and read it standing at the desk with the cold tea in his other hand.

*A reconciliation auditor has been asking after Sennar entries. Three days, perhaps four. Not from the prefecture. From the center.*

He read it once. He set it down on the desk, face up, and drank some of the tea, which had gone past warm into the flat lukewarm he disliked, and he read it again.

Then he finished the tea, because the tea would be no better for waiting and the note would be no worse for his having finished it, and he sat down.

A reconciliation auditor asking after Sennar entries was, in itself, nothing. Sennar was contested ground. A petitioner had brought a magistrate's ruling under question some quarters back, and a contested magistracy drew auditors the way standing water drew insects; it was the office working as the office was built to work. He had watched three such audits cross the Sennar entries in four years and pass through them without a snag, because the entries were sound on their face and an auditor read the face. An auditor from the prefecture was routine. From the center was less routine. The center did not send a man down over a single contest unless the single contest had grown a tail.

He did not let the thought run ahead of what the note gave him. The note gave him an auditor, a region, and a span of days. It did not give him a name, an office, or a reason, and a man who acted on the part he had imagined rather than the part he had been told was a man who left marks where there had been none. He had survived four years by acting on what was in front of him and no more. He folded the note along its first crease and held it.

What was in front of him was this. Of his entries across four years, the Sennar cluster was the oldest and the densest, made in the period before he had learned to thin his work across jurisdictions, and four of the carry-forward entries in the original commission record traced to it. The four were sound. But sound on the face was a defense against an auditor who read the face, and an auditor sent from the center over a contest that had grown a tail might read deeper, might pull the prior commission's own minutes and lay them beside the carry-forward citations and count. If he counted, the count would hold for thirty-nine entries and would not hold for two, and the two were not in Sennar. The two he had initialed this morning were in jurisdictions east. But the method that found two would find them wherever they sat, and the method began in Sennar because Sennar was where a man would begin.

He rose and went to the wall of pigeonholes.

He made two adjustments before the morning session, and both were small, and both were the kind of thing a deputy registrar did a dozen times a week in the ordinary course of the office, which was why he had chosen them. The first was to the routing of the carry-forward schedule he had just initialed. He drew it back from the assessors' tray, where it would have gone up the line that day, and he reentered it on the supplementary list, the list of matter held for the close of the session pending a point of order, citing the standardization circular from New Terra as his ground; until the attestation forms were confirmed, he wrote, prudence held that fresh carry-forward entries wait. It was a defensible note, the note of a careful man, and it meant his two newest entries would not enter the standing record this quarter, would not exist for any auditor to find this quarter, would sit on a held list among a dozen others held for the same plausible reason, and could be quietly withdrawn entire if the days the note had warned him of turned into something worse.

The second adjustment took longer and showed less. He drew from the pigeonholes the working folder on the Sennar reconciliation, the one that held his own marginal notes from four years of entries, the small private record by which he kept his work straight in his own mind. None of it was evidence of anything; it was a registrar's working notes, and any registrar kept them. But his notes on Sennar were denser than his notes elsewhere, because Sennar had been first, and density was a thing a careful reader could see. He took the folder to the side table, and over the cooling kettle he separated the Sennar sheets from the rest, and he did not burn them, because a burned thing was a fact and ash in a registrar's office was a question, and he had no answer prepared for the question. He folded the Sennar sheets into the standardization circular instead, the genuine New Terra circular he had every reason to keep and to study, and he filed the whole back into the pigeonhole under the circular's reference, where the sheets were no longer Sennar notes at all but study material on attestation forms, and where they would draw no eye because the eye that found them would find them filed exactly where a diligent man would file them.

He washed his hands at the side table because the old paper had left its dust on them, dried them on the cloth he kept there, and put the kettle on again out of habit before he remembered he had drunk his two cups.

A knock. Okonjo, the assessor, with the morning list and a question about the order of the Lomé contests, and Sael answered it, and they spoke for a few minutes about nothing that mattered, the assessor leaning in the doorway with his thumbs in his belt, complaining about the standardization circular as every assessor in the building was complaining about it that week, and Sael agreed that it was a nuisance and that the forms had been fine as they were, and meant the agreement, because the circular had this morning done him a service the New Terra office had not intended. Okonjo went. The corridor outside was filling now, the day's voices rising in it, a trolley of folios rumbling past toward the assessors' room, the day's heat beginning to come up off the courtyard stone and in at the window with the smell of the dust the floor-keeper had not reached.

Sael Mokoena did not think of himself as a man with a network, because the word made it sound like a thing he commanded, and he commanded nothing. He had relations. A courier who carried what the registry could not carry and asked no questions because the questions were not his to ask, a man called Rashid who had moved paper for the office for years and moved a little more on the side because the side paid and the side was discreet. An attesting clerk two prefectures over who owed him a kindness done long ago and repaid it in attestations that did not look twice. The hand that had written the unsigned note, which was the only one of his relations that frightened him, because it was the only one above him, and a man's relations above him were the ones he did not get to manage. He did not let himself think long about the hand. The hand had warned him. A hand that warned him was a hand that wanted him intact, and he took the comfort of that and set the rest down.

The seat he had been passed over for, eleven years gone now, he thought of less often than he once had. It had been the prefectural registrarship at Adwa, the room he should have had, and the board had given it to a younger man with a louder family, and Sael had congratulated the younger man and meant the congratulation to be seen to be meant, and had gone back to the deputy's room he still occupied and understood, with the clarity that comes once or twice in a life, that the empire would never give him what his work had earned and that he would therefore take it by the means the empire had left lying about unguarded. The understanding had not been bitter. He had let the bitterness pass the way he let most things pass, and kept the understanding, which was the useful part. The younger man had since moved on to New Terra and a title. Sael had stayed, and learned the office to its roots, and the roots had turned out to be worth more than the title.

The session bell sounded from the assessors' floor, two strokes, the quarter-warning. He gathered the morning list and the banded contests and the initialed remainder of the schedule, the thirty-nine that would go up and the two that would wait, and he squared the stack against the desk's pale edge. He left the held list in the tray for the clerk to carry. He left the standardization circular in its pigeonhole with the Sennar sheets folded inside it. He left the unsigned note in his breast pocket, where it would stay until the walk home, when he would put it through the third terrace's public incinerator with the day's other private paper, one folded sheet among many, no ash anyone could question.

He stood at the window a moment before he went down. The courtyard below was in full sun now, the floor-keeper gone, a single clerk crossing it at an angle with a folio case under his arm and his head down against the brightness. Sael Mokoena watched him cross and reach the far arcade and pass out of the light into the shade of it, and he turned the warning over once more, and found it manageable, the way he had found such things manageable before. He had done this before. He did it now.

He picked up the stack and went down to the session.
