# Chapter 22 — The Second Mechanism

By the third day of the emergency window the investigation room had stopped being a room anyone left.

It had grown the way Asha's second room had grown, past the table it began on and past the next table after that, until the storage bay under the south wall held three tables end to end and a fourth set crosswise at the head, and the walls that had been bare in the spring carried the work in ranked sheets pinned in the order it was read. The disbursement registers stood banded along the long wall where Asha could reach them without crossing the room. The appointment files, the matter that had been the whole of the case in the spring and was now the smaller half of it, had been moved to a side table against the door, squared and closed, because the appointment files were proven and the appointment files were not where the danger lay. The danger lay in the new sheets, the ones Zola had begun to fill two days before and had not stopped filling, the three-column rule she worked in widened now to five, and the five columns climbed the wall in a hand that had gone from the slow clean rule of the spring to something faster and surer that was the same hand grown into the size of what it carried.

The light had been artificial for so long that no one in the room could have said what the hour was outside without the clock. There was a clock, a plain bureau face on the crosswise table at the head, and Kaviel kept it wound, and she looked at it the way a person looks at a thing they need and do not trust, because the clock was the edge of the window the Empress had bought them and the window did not widen. The emergency authority froze the channels for as long as the channels stayed frozen, and the channels stayed frozen for as long as no one with standing pried them open, and a resource held still was a resource that could be moved the instant the holding lapsed. They were not racing the faction now. The faction's move in the registry had fallen the day before. They were racing the freeze itself, the slow administrative thaw that would begin the moment the prerogative's reach was tested at its edges, and the edges were where the second mechanism lived.

Kaviel had the shape of it by the second hour of the third day, and the shape was worse than the appointments had been.

She had known there was a second mechanism since the corridor outside the neutral room where Eleanor had met the marshal, since the line that had come up the channel in Eleanor's hand the same night, three words and a fourth, that the money moved too, and that they had not found how. Eleanor did not write more than she had to and she had not written more than that. But the line had told Kaviel where to look, and looking was the thing the Quarter did, and so she had set Zola back into the cold-jurisdiction stacks with a new instruction, to stop reading the appointments as the thing and start reading them as the cover, and to find what the appointments had been built to hide.

What they had been built to hide was a flow.

"Lay it from the start," Kaviel said. She was in the chair at the head of the crosswise table, which she used now because the work had grown past standing, and Zola was at the wall with the five-column sheets, and Asha was at the long wall with the disbursement registers open under both hands, and across the room a second researcher and a clerk worked the cross-reference between the two, carrying sheets from the wall to the registers and back. "From the channel up. Not the count. The road."

"The appointments were never the point," Zola said. She did not turn from the wall. She had learned, in the weeks since the night she carried the folder up the corridor, to talk while she worked, and the talking and the working had become the same motion. "A false magistrate confirms judgments. A false registrar confirms entries. That is power, and the marshal's people wanted the power, and we read the case for two seasons as a case about the power, because the false appointments were the thing we could see. But a false officer in a working seat does a second thing that a true officer in the same seat does, which is to certify the movement of money. Disbursements. Reconciliations of accounts between jurisdictions. The settling of obligations across the prefectural treasuries that runs through the same offices the appointments run through, because it is the same officers who sign it."

"And a false officer signs it false," Asha said from the long wall. She said it flat, marking the fact, not asking. She had built the financial side in the spring and she had found the correction pass in it and she had hit the wall above it and logged the wall and not crossed it, and she had carried since then the knowledge that the correction pass meant someone senior was reaching down to clean the records after the fact, and she had not known until this third day what the cleaning had been cleaning. Now she knew. "The false officer certifies a disbursement that has a true face and a false back. The treasury reads the face. The face says a sum moved from one prefectural account to settle a true obligation, a contract, a levy, a reconciliation between two jurisdictions that both show the entry and both show it settled. The treasury reconciles it and closes it. And the sum that moved is short of the sum the face declared, and the short part went somewhere the face does not record, and the books balance because the officer who signed the false face is the officer who would have caught the short, and he is not catching it, because he is the one taking it."

"Seven years," Zola said.

The room was quiet for the length of that. The clerk's stylus stopped on the cross-reference sheet and started again. Somewhere above them the transcription engine ran its backlog, the patient clicking that had become the floor of every long day in the Quarter, and under it the dry close smell of too much paper held too long in a room with no window, and under that the cold that banked in the corners where the registers stood and never lifted while the door stayed shut.

"Show me the seven years," Kaviel said.

Zola brought down the head sheet of the five-column climb and laid it on the crosswise table under Kaviel's hand, and weighted the corners with the clock and the inkstone the way the work taught, and she stood back to the distance a researcher stood when a senior read.

"The first false disbursement I can place is seven years back, in the Sennar accounts, the same watershed where the first false appointment sat." Zola's finger moved down the first column, not touching. "A reconciliation between Sennar and the jurisdiction above it, a true obligation, a road levy that both sides show. The face declares the full sum. The Sennar account pays the full sum. The receiving account receives a sum that is short by a portion, and the portion is made up on the receiving side by a carry-forward credit, a reconciliation entry, that says the difference was settled under a prior authority and need not be paid again." She let her hand fall. "It is the appointments fraud. It is the same clause. The carry-forward reconciliation does not only let a false appointment into the record on the word of a prior authority that does not exist. It lets a false settlement into the books the same way. The provision was never about appointments. The appointments were one use of it. The money was the other use, and the money was the use that mattered, and we read the appointments first because the appointments were the part that left a face in a courtroom."

Kaviel read the head sheet the way Asha had taught her to read a junior's finding, hunting for the place it was wrong before anyone else found it, and she did not find the place, because there was no place, because the girl had run it the way she ran everything, blind and clean and back to the source. She read the first column and the second and the third, the face sum and the paid sum and the short, and she read the carry-forward credit that closed the short, and she read it against the disbursement register Asha had open on the long wall, and the register showed the face and the books showed balanced and the short was real and the short had gone somewhere.

"Where does the short go," Kaviel said.

"That is the second mechanism," Asha said. She came off the long wall with a register in her two hands and set it on the crosswise table beside the head sheet, open to a page banded with a marker she had set that morning, and she laid her finger on a line of it without touching the ink. "The short does not vanish. It cannot vanish, because a sum that vanished would show as a loss somewhere and a loss is the thing the treasury is built to find. So the short is not lost. It is moved, and it is moved into accounts that the treasury does not read as the same money, because the accounts are accounts of a different kind, in a different ledger, under a different office, and the office that moves it is the office that certifies that it is a true and separate sum and not the short from the disbursement two pages back."

"Three offices," Zola said.

Kaviel looked up from the register.

"Three channels," Zola said. She brought the second sheet down from the wall and laid it beside the first, and this one was the map, the way the first finding had ended in a map, the printer's grid of the prefectures extended past its own edges in her own slow rule, the adjoining jurisdictions to spinward and the river districts to the south sketched in beyond the printed border, and on it, in the small flat dots that were Kaviel's own mark, the dots ran the same spread the appointments had run, because they were the same offices, but threaded between the dots now were three heavier marks, three lines that gathered the short out of the dots and ran it inward. "The Third Appointments Commission for the Outer Prefectures is one. We knew the Third Commission. The Third Commission is the channel everyone has been looking at since the spring, because the Third Commission is where the appointments sat, and the faction was content to let it be looked at, because the appointments in it were the cover and the money in it was only a third of the money." Her finger moved to the second heavy line, off the printed grid, in the country she had drawn herself. "The adjoining jurisdictions to spinward settle their reconciliations under a separate commission that answers up the same bureau. That is the second channel. The short from the spinward disbursements runs into it. And the river commissions to the south, a third reporting line on paper, settle theirs under a third body, and the short from the river runs into that." She set her hand flat on the map. "Three channels. The appointments fraud touched all three because the appointments fraud was the cover for all three. We proved the appointments and we thought we had the case, and we had a third of the case, and the third we had was the third they were willing to lose."

"And the three run to one office," Kaviel said. She said it the way she had said it on the night Zola first carried the name up the corridor, half to herself, reading the map and not the girl. "The same as the appointments. The three reporting lines come together at one office that reaches all three."

"They come together at the marshal's office," Asha said. She did not lower her voice, because the room was the room and the case was past the point of unsaid names. "The same office. The appointments and the money run the same road to the same reach, because they were built by the same understanding for the same purpose, and the purpose was the money. The appointments were how you put your hands on the offices. The money was what the offices were for."

Kaviel sat with the two sheets and the open register, and the room ran on around her, the clerk carrying a cross-reference back to the wall and the second researcher pinning a fresh sheet into the climb and the engine clicking above, and she did the arithmetic, the slow real arithmetic with the clock running on the table at her elbow, and the arithmetic was not the count of the money, which was large past the point where the size of it meant anything, but the count of the danger.

"If we strike the appointments," she said, "the money moves."

"The money moves," Asha said.

This was the thing the third day turned on, and Kaviel had known it would be the thing since the second hour, and she had not let herself reach for the striking until the road was laid, because a striking made before the road was whole was a striking that scattered the resource into country the prerogative could not follow. The emergency authority froze the channels. It froze them inside the imperium, inside the reach of the Throne, inside the jurisdictions the prerogative named. The short that had been gathered for seven years sat now in accounts the freeze held still. But an account held still was not an account seized, and the people who had built the second mechanism had built into it, the way they built into everything, a use for the moment the cover failed. The moment the appointments were struck and the false officers fell, a true officer would read the disbursements clean and the carry-forward credits would not close and the short would show, and the short showing was the alarm, and the alarm was the instruction to move the resource out of the frozen channels and into the river districts at the spinward edge where the prerogative's reach ran thin and out past them into the adjoining Thrones where it did not run at all. A wrong move struck the cover and rang the alarm and lost the resource in the same hour.

"So we do not strike the cover," Kaviel said. "Not first. We close the road out before we touch the road in."

She stood, the first time she had stood since the second hour, and she went to the wall where the map climbed, and she read the three heavy lines, and she read where they ran thin at the spinward edge, and she read the river districts below where the third channel went to ground, and she began to build the thing she built best, which was the order of a striking that did not ring until it was complete.

"The correction pass," she said. "Asha. The correction pass is the signature. You found it in the spring and you logged the wall above it and you did not cross. The correction pass is a senior hand reaching down to clean the records after the insertions, and we read it then as cleaning the appointments. It was cleaning the money. Every place the correction pass ran, it ran to close a short that had begun to show, to lay a fresh carry-forward credit over a settlement that a true officer had started to read clean. The correction pass is the alarm rehearsed. It is the move they will make when the cover fails, made early, on a schedule, every time the books drifted toward showing the short. Find me every correction pass in seven years. Each one is a place the short threatened to show and was hidden again. Each one is a finger on the road out. If we map the correction passes we map the route the resource will take when it runs, because the route it will take is the route it has already taken, every time, for seven years, whenever the cover thinned."

Asha was already at the long wall, pulling the registers that held the correction-pass entries she had marked in the spring and not crossed, and she carried them to the crosswise table and laid them in the order she built, and she went to her knees on the cold stone among them the way she worked, because the floor was where the work went when the table was full, and the table was full. She read the first correction pass and set it on the map at the place it had run, and the second, and the third, and the dots of the correction passes fell along the three heavy lines exactly where Kaviel had said they would fall, at the thin edge spinward and at the river districts to the south, the rehearsed road out marked in seven years of cleaning, and Asha read them onto the map one at a time in the cramped exact hand that had filled a thousand ledgers, kneeling on the stone with the cold of the registers banked around her and the dry mineral smell of the old paper close, and she did not rise for an hour.

Kaviel built the order of the striking while Asha mapped the road. She built it the way she had built the replies for the registry morning, against a thing that had not happened yet, putting each piece where it would have to act before she knew the hour it would act in. The road out had to be closed first, the thin edge and the river districts, the accounts at the end of the rehearsed route held under a second instrument the Empress would have to sign, narrower than the freeze and aimed, naming the route the correction passes drew. Then the channels, the three of them, held still already by the freeze and now to be sealed at the gather points where the short pooled, so that the resource could not be summoned inward to a single account and run from there. Then, last, the cover, the false officers and the false appointments, struck only when the road out was closed and the channels sealed and there was nowhere for the alarm to send the money, so that when the short finally showed it showed into a system that had already shut every door it could run through.

It was the order of a thing that did not ring until it was done, and the building of it took the whole of the afternoon that no one in the room could see, and the room ran on through the building of it, the clerk and the second researcher carrying sheets, Asha kneeling at the road, Zola at the wall widening the columns as each new short surfaced and was placed. The clock turned on the crosswise table. A floor-keeper drew the mop along the corridor outside and the wet trail of it shone under the door and faded. The engine clicked above. The cold held in the corners. And the case, which had been a case about false appointments in the spring and had been a third of itself when they thought it whole, grew on the walls into the size it had always been, the appointments the visible face of it and the money the body, seven years of short gathered down three channels to one reach, and the order of the striking laid against it piece by piece so that the body could be taken without the face crying out in time to run.

A second line came up the channel from Eleanor at the close of the afternoon. The runner brought it and Kaviel read it and set it flat on the crosswise table and read it again, and it was as short as the first. It did not ask after the work. It did not tell her what to do with what the work found. It told her, in the form Eleanor wrote things, that Eleanor was positioned for the next phase and that the next phase was the marshal's, not the money's, and that Kaviel should give the Empress the complete picture and nothing held back, because the picture was what made the case stand in a court and a court was where the marshal was now going. Kaviel read the last of it and understood the shape of what Eleanor had taken on for herself in the country past this room, the cost she had written into her own column when she walked into the neutral room, and she did not write back, because there was nothing to write that Eleanor did not already hold, and she folded the line and put it in the drawer with the rest of the case and turned the key.

"Asha," she said. "Is the road closed."

"The road is mapped." Asha rose off her knees with a hand on the table edge, an old officer at the end of a long day on a stone floor, and she set the last correction pass on the map and stood back from it. "Every correction pass in seven years is on it. The road out runs where I said it would not run and where you said it would, the thin spinward edge and the three river accounts under the southern body, and the resource has run that road every time the cover thinned, which means it will run it again when the cover fails, which means the second instrument has to name those accounts and no others, narrow, so that a court reads it as aimed at a proven route and not as a seizure of a region's treasury. I have the account numbers. I have the dates of every run. I have the proof that each run followed a short that began to show. A court will read the route as a confession, because no honest office cleans a settlement on a schedule that tracks the threat of its own discovery."

"And the channels," Kaviel said. "The gather points."

"Three." Asha laid her finger on the map at the three places the heavy lines pooled before they ran inward. "Sealed at these. The short pools here before it is summoned to the marshal's reach, and if these three are sealed the moment the second instrument signs, the resource cannot be summoned, and a resource that cannot be summoned and cannot run is a resource that sits where it is until the court takes it." She lowered her hand. "It is surgical. It has to be. A wider seal touches true accounts and a true account frozen by error is a jurisdiction the faction can point to and say the Throne overreached, and the overreach is the thing the registry morning was rehearsing and the thing the complaint against Eleanor will argue. We touch the proven route and the three gather points and nothing else. The honest money never feels it. The short cannot move. And when the cover comes off, the short shows into a closed room."

Kaviel read the whole of the map a last time, the appointments and the money and the road and the channels and the order of the striking laid against all of it, and she read it the way she had read Zola's first finding, hunting for the place it was wrong, and there was no place, because the work was clean, because it had been run blind and read hard and built back to the source by a room that had not stopped in three days. The picture was complete. It was the thing she had carried since the corridor outside the neutral room, and it was whole now on the wall in front of her, and the completeness of it was the thing she had wanted and the thing she would have given a great deal not to be true.

She stood with it for a while. The room ran on. The clerk banded the last cross-reference and set it on the closed appointment files by the door. The second researcher pinned the final sheet of the climb into the wall and stepped back, and the five-column rule reached the ceiling now, the body of a seven-year theft set out where a court could read it from the floor up. Zola stood at the foot of the wall looking up the climb of her own hand, the brightness in her eyes gone glassy with the third day and the not-sleeping, holding the size of the thing the way she had been taught to hold it, in the head and not on the front of the face. Asha stood at the map with her hands at her sides. The engine clicked. The cold held. The clock on the crosswise table turned past an hour no one in the room had marked.

Kaviel went to the drawer and took out the case, the whole of it, the appointments proven in the spring and the money proven in three days, and she squared it in both hands the way Zola had squared the first folder in the corridor, and she set it on the crosswise table under the bureau clock, complete.

"Tell the Empress it is complete," Kaviel said.

Zola turned from the wall. "All of it?"

"All of it," Kaviel said.
