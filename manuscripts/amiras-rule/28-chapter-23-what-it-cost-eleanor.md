# Chapter 23 — What It Cost Eleanor

The oversight chamber sat on the third floor of the old administrative court, in the wing the establishment kept for the proceedings it held against itself.

It was a room built to make a single argument, which was that the imperial administration could be weighed in its own scales and found wanting without the throne having to lift a hand, and the room made the argument in stone and in furniture and in the placement of the light. The ceiling was high and plain. The walls were the same pale dressed stone as the corridors that fed it, and they carried no map, no portrait, no seal of any house, because the chamber answered to no house and was at pains to say so. A low rail of dark wood divided the floor. On the near side of the rail were the benches for those with standing to attend, three rows of them, hard and unupholstered, the wood gone smooth at the forward edges where a great many people had sat through a great many hours without comfort. On the far side, raised one shallow step, stood the arbiter's bench, long and bare, and before it, at the level of the floor, a single chair set facing the bench across an open distance that no table crossed. The chair had been placed so that a person answering from it sat alone in the middle of the room with the light coming down full on them from the tall clerestory windows and nothing within reach of either hand. The room had been designed by someone who understood that a person made to sit exposed in the center of a formal space will, given an hour, begin to argue against themselves. Eleanor had read the room before she crossed the rail, the way she read every room, and she had understood its argument, and she had decided, somewhere in the crossing, that she would not give it the hour it was built to take.

She came in on the cane and on Sefu's arm to the rail and no farther on the arm, because past the rail the proceeding was hers to answer and a person answering does not lean on another. She let go of Sefu at the rail. She crossed the open floor to the single chair at her own pace, the cane setting its even sound on the stone, and the sound carried in the bare room the way the room was built to carry it, and she let it carry, and she sat. She set the cane upright against the side of the chair where her hand could find it. She folded her hands in her lap. The light came down on her from the high windows, cold and white off the morning, and it found the lines of a face that had been worked by eighty years and gave nothing back, and she sat in the center of the floor as still as she had sat in the leased room a fortnight before, and waited for the proceeding to begin only when it began, because beginning it in her own head ahead of the arbiter would have been the room's first small theft of her and she did not intend to be robbed in increments.

Kaviel came in behind her and took the forward bench on the near side of the rail, alone, in the place set for an attending officer of the apparatus under review. She had been entered on the standing as present and not as a party, and the entry carried with it the one term that governed the whole of her morning, which was that she had no voice in the chamber. She could attend. She could not speak. She could not pass paper across the rail or answer a question put to her mother or correct a thing said wrong about the work she had run, and she had read the term the night before in the notice and had set the notice down without a word, and she sat now on the forward bench with her hands flat on her knees and her back straight and her eyes on the single chair in the middle of the floor, and she did the only thing the term left her, which was to be in the room.

Arbiter Sabel Doumbia entered through the door behind the bench at the hour the notice had named, neither early nor late, a spare upright woman of middle years in the gray robe the oversight office wore, and the chamber rose, and Eleanor rose with it on the cane, which cost her and which she paid without letting the cost reach the surface, and Doumbia took the bench and inclined her head and the chamber sat. She did not look long at Eleanor. She looked at the file open before her, a thick file, banded and tabbed, and she read the head of it in the flat unhurried voice of a person who has read ten thousand such heads and means to read this one no differently, and the reading set the frame of the morning out loud for the record that would outlast everyone in the room.

"This is a formal review under the standing oversight authority of the imperial administration," Doumbia said, "convened on a complaint lodged by the office of the Marshal of the Bureau of Establishments, authenticated as colorable by an officer of the court, and entered to the docket in due form. The complaint is against Eleanor De Grosvenor, head of the De Grosvenor intelligence and administrative apparatus, and it alleges three things. It alleges abuse of intelligence authority. It alleges the unauthorized surveillance of a senior official of the imperial establishment. It alleges procedural violations in the conduct of an inquiry not chartered by any standing body. This review does not adjudicate guilt and assigns no penalty. It weighs whether the complaint states a matter the administration must answer, and it makes a finding, and the finding goes up. Eleanor De Grosvenor, you have been served the complaint and you have declined the assistance of an advocate. You answer for yourself."

"I do," Eleanor said.

"Then you understand that what you say is the record."

"I have understood that about every room I have entered for sixty years," Eleanor said. "I do not expect this one to be different."

Doumbia let that sit without taking it up, which told Eleanor the woman was good, because a lesser arbiter would have heard a challenge in it and answered the challenge and lost a degree of the bench in the answering, and Doumbia heard it for what it was, an old woman stating the terms she had always worked under, and let it pass into the record unweighted and went on. She turned a tab in the file.

"The first allegation," she said. "Abuse of intelligence authority. The complaint holds that your apparatus conducted, over a period of months, a sustained collection effort directed at the offices and records of the Bureau of Establishments, that this collection was not authorized by any charter the Bureau recognizes, and that you directed it. Do you contest that the collection occurred."

"No."

The word went out flat into the bare room and the room held it. On the forward bench Kaviel did not move. Doumbia looked up from the file then, the first full look she had given the chair, and she took the old woman's measure across the open floor, and Eleanor gave her the same nothing she had given the marshal, and the two of them held the look a moment, the arbiter and the answering, and it was Doumbia who returned to the file, having taken from the look what she needed, which was the measure of a woman who would not be made to argue against herself.

"You do not contest it," Doumbia said.

"I will not spend the morning making you prove a thing I did," Eleanor said. "The collection occurred. I directed it. It was sustained and it was directed at the Bureau of Establishments and it was not chartered by the Bureau, and it would have been a poor collection if it had been, because a Bureau does not charter the reading of its own floor. I will save the chamber the labor of the first allegation by granting all of it that is fact. The collection happened. I am the hand it happened under. Where I will not go with you is the word abuse, and I will tell you why when you have read me the rest, because the answer to the word abuse is the same answer to all three and I would rather give it once and whole than three times in pieces."

"The chamber will hear the rest first," Doumbia said. "The second allegation. The unauthorized surveillance of a senior official. The complaint holds that the collection was directed in part at the person and movements of the Marshal of the Bureau of Establishments, Lord Marshal Tewodros Marek, that he was watched, that his correspondence was traced, that a meeting was sought with him under conditions you arranged, and that none of this was sanctioned by any authority competent to sanction the surveillance of an officer of his rank. Do you contest it."

"I contest one word of it," Eleanor said. "I will give you the rest. He was watched. His correspondence was traced, through the relays that carried it, by people who read the routing and not the words, because the words were not the thing and the routing was. I sought a meeting with him and I arranged its conditions, and I will tell the chamber freely that I arranged them so that he would hold no advantage in the room, because I was not going to meet a man of his reach on ground he chose. All of that is true and I set it on the record without qualification. The word I contest is unauthorized, and again I will hold the contest until the third allegation is read, because it is the same contest, and the chamber's time is worth more than my repeating myself."

Doumbia turned the last tab.

"The third allegation. Procedural violations in the conduct of an inquiry not chartered by any standing body. The complaint holds that the inquiry your apparatus conducted, the findings of which now stand under the protection of an extraordinary authority, was itself irregular in its foundation, that it operated without a charter from the Bureau or from any commission, that it gathered and held and acted upon material outside the procedures that govern such gathering, and that an inquiry so founded cannot produce findings the administration is bound to credit." She closed the file over her hand and looked up. "That is the complaint. You have granted the facts of the first and the second and reserved one word from each. Give the chamber your answer."

Eleanor was quiet for the space of a breath, and the breath was not hesitation, it was the ordering of a thing she had ordered many times in the nights since the leased room, and she let the room have the silence because the silence was hers now and not the room's, and then she spoke into it in the dry even voice that had read figures back across a thousand tables.

"The complaint is true in almost every fact it states," she said, "and I have granted those facts as they came, and I will not take them back to soften the morning. I watched the marshal. I read the routing of his correspondence. I conducted an inquiry that no Bureau chartered and no commission convened, and I held material in it and I acted on what the material told me, and I did all of it knowing that no standing procedure of the imperial administration had authorized a single hour of it. The chamber may write that down as my own word, and it is. Where the complaint is false is in the frame it puts around those facts, and the frame is the whole of the matter, because a fact set in the wrong frame argues for a thing that never happened."

"State the frame the complaint puts on it," Doumbia said.

"The complaint puts on it the frame of the standing administrative procedure," Eleanor said. "It says: here is conduct, measure it against the charters that govern the Bureau and its officers and its inquiries, and you will find the conduct exceeded them at every turn, and the conduct is therefore abuse, unauthorized, and irregular. And measured against that frame, every word of the complaint is sound. I will grant the chamber that too. If the procedures of the Bureau of Establishments are the proper measure of what I did, then I have abused, I have exceeded, I have proceeded without charter, and the finding writes itself, and you may send it up before the noon bell." She let her folded hands rest. "I say the procedures of the Bureau are not the proper measure. I say the proper measure is the compact, and that the compact is the frame the complaint has been built to keep out of this room, because inside that frame the same facts say something the complaint cannot survive."

Doumbia did not move to stop her, which Eleanor marked, because an arbiter protecting the Bureau's frame would have ruled the compact outside the chamber's notice and closed the door on it, and Doumbia left the door open, and an open door was a thing Eleanor knew how to walk through.

"The chamber knows the compact exists," Doumbia said. "The chamber does not know that the compact answers a complaint of administrative irregularity. Make the connection, if you can make it."

"The De Grosvenor compact is older than this chamber and older than the procedures the complaint invokes and older than the Bureau in the form that now governs appointments," Eleanor said. "It is the agreement under which my house serves the throne. It has spanned three imperial generations. It has survived three crises before this one, and in each of the three, one party extended to the other a trust the other had not yet formally earned, because the compact was built on the understanding that there would be hours the standing procedure could not reach, and that in those hours the house would act, and the throne would judge the acting afterward, and the judging would be the throne's and no one else's. That is what the compact is for. It is not for the seasons when the procedures work. In the seasons when the procedures work, the house does nothing the procedures cannot do, and the compact sleeps. The compact wakes when the procedures have been turned against the thing they were built to protect, and a hand is needed that the captured procedures cannot reach to stop, and the throne reaches for the house, because the house is the hand the compact built for exactly that hour."

"And you hold that this was such an hour."

"I hold that the procedures the complaint invokes as my measure were the very procedures the apparatus I investigated had captured and was using," Eleanor said. "The carry-forward reconciliation is a procedure. The authorization category the correction pass ran under is a procedure. The charter that would have had to authorize an inquiry into the Bureau is a charter the Bureau grants, and the Bureau, at the level that grants it, was the thing under inquiry. I could not have gone to the Bureau for a charter to investigate the Bureau without the charter reaching the desk of the man I was investigating, who would have read the request the way he had read every request for thirty years, and stopped it, and known. The complaint says I proceeded without the charter. It is true. The charter was held by the hand I was reaching for. To ask permission was to warn the thing. The procedures were not available to me, because the thing I was investigating governed the procedures, and that is not an accident of my case. It is the shape of every case the compact was built to answer, and it is the reason the compact was set to run beside the procedures rather than through them, with the throne as the only judge of whether the hour was real."

The room held it. High in the clerestory a pigeon shifted on a ledge and the small scrape of it carried down into the bare chamber and was gone. The light had moved a hand's width across the stone floor while Eleanor spoke and now lay across the foot of the single chair, and she sat in the edge of it and did not shift out of it.

"You are arguing," Doumbia said slowly, "that the chamber cannot find against you on the complaint's terms because the complaint's terms are the wrong terms. That the conduct should be measured against the compact, and that the compact reserves the judging of it to the throne, and that this chamber, weighing it against administrative procedure, is weighing it in a frame the compact was designed to override."

"I am arguing that," Eleanor said. "I am arguing it knowing the chamber may not have the authority to accept it, and that if the chamber does not have the authority to accept it, the chamber will find against me, because inside the only frame the chamber is competent to apply, the complaint is sound, and an honest arbiter applying the only frame she is given will find the thing the frame compels. I am not asking you to be dishonest. I am telling you that the frame you have been handed produces the wrong answer, and that the right answer lives in a frame above your bench, and that whether the right answer is ever reached depends on whether anyone with the standing to lift the matter into that frame chooses to do so before your finding goes up. That is not in my hands. It was never going to be in my hands. I knew that when I directed the collection, and I directed it anyway, because the hour was real and the compact is not a thing you invoke only when you are certain it will save you."

"You knew you might be sitting in that chair."

"I knew I would be sitting in this chair, or in one like it," Eleanor said. "There was no version of what I did that did not end here. The marshal told me himself, across a table I built so neither of us could reach the other, that my house had been named before I moved, and he was right, and I had priced the chair before he said it. I am eighty years old. I have spent sixty of those years learning what the procedures can and cannot reach, and I directed an inquiry into the place where they had been captured, and I did it through the only frame that authorized me to do it, which is the compact, and I will answer for it in the frame that authorized it and not in the frame that did not. If the chamber finds against me, the chamber will be correct within its competence and wrong above it, and I will have known both things going in, and I do not ask the chamber to spare me the finding. I ask the record to show that I did not hide what I did, that I claimed the frame I acted under, and that I claimed it on the morning I had the most to lose by claiming it, when it would have been easier to plead the charter I never had and let the chamber pity an old woman who had overreached. I did not overreach. I reached exactly as far as the compact reaches, into the hour the compact was built for, and I will not call it less than that to soften a finding."

She stopped. She had not raised her voice once. She had not bent toward the bench, had not gestured, had not reached for the cane, and the stillness of her in the center of the exposed floor was the thing the room had been built to break and had not broken, and on the forward bench Kaviel sat with her hands flat on her knees and her eyes on her mother's back and did not move, and the not moving was the hardest work she had done in the case and no one in the chamber would ever enter it on a record.

Doumbia was quiet a moment. She looked at the file and did not open it. When she spoke it was to the record and not to Eleanor.

"The chamber notes the answer," she said. "The respondent does not contest the facts of the first or the second allegation and grants the facts of the third. The respondent contests the characterization of those facts as abuse, as unauthorized, and as irregular, and grounds the contest in the De Grosvenor compact, asserting that the proper frame for the conduct is the compact rather than standing administrative procedure, and that the compact reserves the judgment of such conduct to the throne." She paused, and the pause was the careful pause of a person setting down a thing exactly, neither more nor less than it was. "The chamber observes that the question of whether the compact may displace administrative procedure as the frame of review is a question that exceeds the competence of this oversight authority to decide, and that the chamber cannot find on the complaint without first deciding it. The chamber will not pretend to a competence it does not hold."

It was the most she could give, and Eleanor knew it was the most she could give, and she received it sitting in the edge of the moved light with her hands folded and her face giving nothing, and she understood that the arbiter had just done a careful and honest thing, which was to refuse to dress the question smaller than it was so that her own bench could answer it. Doumbia had set the question where it had to go. She had not sent it there. She had only declined to keep it from going.

"This review is adjourned," Doumbia said, "pending formal consideration of the question of frame, which the chamber will refer upward in the ordinary course unless that course is itself displaced by an authority competent to displace it. The respondent is not detained. The complaint stands on the docket. The findings remain as the extraordinary authority left them. The chamber thanks the respondent for the clarity of her answer." She rose. "We are adjourned."

The chamber rose with her. Eleanor put both hands to the head of the cane and brought herself up, and it took the weight the way it took the weight now, slowly, and she did not hurry it and no one in the room moved to help her, and she gained her feet and stood a moment finding her balance in the center of the floor before she turned. Doumbia had already gone through the door behind the bench. The benches were emptying behind the rail, the few with standing rising and filing out in the low murmur of people who had watched a thing they were not sure they had understood the size of, and Eleanor crossed the open floor toward the rail at her own pace, the cane on the stone, and at the rail Kaviel was standing, having risen with the chamber, waiting on the near side, and she did not speak, because she still could not, because the term that governed her morning did not lift at the adjournment, it lifted when they were out of the building, and they both knew it, and so Kaviel said nothing and held the gate of the rail open the width it opened and let her mother through it.

They went out together, the old woman on the cane and the daughter beside her at the pace the cane set, through the door of the chamber and along the pale stone corridor that had fed it and down the wide shallow stair, and Sefu fell in behind them at the foot of the stair without a word and stayed the careful three steps back that was his whole life's distance, and the three of them crossed the cold entrance hall where the morning came in gray through the tall doors and smelled of wet stone and the dust of an old building and, faintly, the iron of the rain that had started while they were inside. Neither woman spoke in the hall. They did not speak through the doors. They came out onto the broad step of the administrative court into the thin cold rain, and the noise of the district came up to meet them, the engines and the wheels and the wet rush of a city going on with its morning, and Eleanor stopped on the step under the lip of the portico where the rain did not reach, and stood with both hands on the cane, and looked out at the gray wet square, and only then, with the building behind them and the open air in front, did she let the morning be over.

Kaviel stood beside her on the step. The rain came down on the square. A clerk hurried across it with a folder held over his head against the wet, and a transit car hissed past at the far edge. Eleanor watched the rain a while, and Kaviel watched the rain, and the distance between them on the step was the same distance it had always been, the careful width the work required, and neither of them moved to close it, because closing it was not a thing either of them knew how to do and they had stopped pretending, long ago, that they did not know that about each other.

"Sefu will bring the car to the south door," Eleanor said at last, to the rain. "The step is wet."

"I'll walk you to it," Kaviel said.

"I know," Eleanor said.

She set the cane forward onto the dry stone under the portico and started along it toward the south door at her own pace, and Kaviel went with her, the careful width between them held, and behind them Sefu went down the wet step into the rain to bring the car around, and the rain beaded on the cold dark stone of the court that weighed the administration against itself, and the cane set its even sound on the dry stone under the portico, and the two women walked it together toward the south door.
