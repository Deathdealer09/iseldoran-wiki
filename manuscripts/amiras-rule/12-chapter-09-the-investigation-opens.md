# Chapter 9 — The Investigation Opens

The room had been a store for forty years, and it gave that up reluctantly. They had pulled the broken assessment furniture out of it the day before, the chairs with the splintered legs and the cabinets whose locks had been cut and never replaced, and what remained when the carrying was done was a long cold space under the north wall of the compound with a smell that no airing had yet moved, the dry smell of board and binding left too long without handling, of old paper that had sat in stacks against stone and taken on the cold of it. Kaviel had asked for this room and not a better one. A better one would have had a use the building could name, and a use the building could name was a use someone could ask after.

She stood in the doorway a moment before she went in, the way she did with any space she meant to work from, reading it for what it would and would not let her do. There was one window, high and narrow, set into the thickness of the north wall where the masonry was deepest, and at this hour it gave a light that came in flat and gray and lay across the floor without reaching the inner half of the room, so that the far end stood in a dimness the single lamp on the long table did not take back. She had cracked the window already, out of the habit she kept in her own office, and the moving air had thinned the paper smell near the glass and left it banked at the back of the room where the stacks were.

Zola Adimu was at the long table when Kaviel came in, standing, not sitting, with her hands at her sides and a stillness to her that was the stillness of someone who had arrived early and decided that sitting before she was told to sit would be the wrong thing. She was young enough that the youth was the first fact about her and would be for another two years, until the work wore it down into something a stranger would not lead with. Kaviel had read her file twice. Top of her analytical cohort, slow where the others were quick and right where the others were fast, the kind of researcher Asha graded high and worked hard because the patience was already in her and only the method had to be put on top of it.

"You found it," Kaviel said.

"Asha sent me down at the first hour. The stacks were already here." Zola's eyes went to them and came back. "I sorted what I could read the labels on. The rest I left as it came."

Kaviel walked the length of the table. Someone, Zola or whoever had done the carrying, had laid the document stacks along it in an order, and the order was not bad. It ran by jurisdiction, the Sennar Prefecture nearest the lamp where the most of it was, and then the others falling away down the table into the dim end, each prefecture's records squared and weighted with whatever had come to hand, a cut of stone in one place, a dead lamp base in another. There was a map on the wall above the table, the only thing on any wall, a working map of the Outer Prefectures with the numbered jurisdictions marked in the dry imperial style and nothing on it yet in any hand but the printer's.

"This is the work," Kaviel said. She did not say it warmly. She said it the way Asha said a thing was the work, as a statement of where they now stood and what it would ask. "I am going to tell you what we are doing and the bounds of it, once, and then we begin, and you will hold the bounds as hard as you hold the method, because the bounds are the part that keeps the rest of it standing."

Zola nodded. She had a stylus already and a clean sheet, and she did not write yet, because she had been taught well enough to know that the first telling of a thing was for the ear and the second for the page.

"We have a mandate," Kaviel said. "It is real. The Lioness charter gives us standing to examine any imperial record where the integrity of the record bears on the protection of the throne, and the integrity of the appointments record bears on it, because a false magistrate rules in the empire's name and binds the empire's subjects, and a false administrator signs in the empire's hand. No one drafted that clause for this. But it holds for this. I have read it against three older uses and it holds." She set two fingers on the Sennar stack. "What it gives us is the record. We may pull the appointment files, the commission rosters, the prefectural registries, the transit manifests where they touch a record we are examining, and we may set them beside one another and read what the reading shows. That is a great deal. It is also the whole of it."

"And what it does not give us," Zola said. Not a question. She had heard the shape of the sentence coming.

"It does not give us a person. We do not question a serving official under this mandate. We do not enter a working office, we do not pull a man's correspondence, we do not put a watch on anyone who has not first walked up out of the record on his own. The mandate is over paper. The moment we reach past the paper to a hand, we are doing a thing the charter does not cover, and a thing the charter does not cover is a thing that has to be authorized from above the Quarter, and authorization from above the Quarter is a door, and a door can be watched from the other side." She let that sit. "Do you follow the distinction. Tell it back."

"We read the records. We do not touch the people." Zola's voice was even. "Until a person is already in the records, where we can read them without being told we are allowed to look."

"Until then." Kaviel moved down the table to the dim end and stood where the gray light gave out. "Now the part that is harder than the method, and I want it from you before we have logged a single entry, because once we are in it the work will tempt you to forget it. Who knows that this room is open."

Zola was quiet a moment. "I do not know. I was sent down. I was not told why, past sorting the stacks."

"Asha knows, because the room sits under her and a room does not open in the Quarter without her knowing what it is. Eleanor De Grosvenor knows, because I told her, and I will say more about that. You know, now. There is one more I will bring in when the financial records need her, and you will meet her and you will not ask what else she works." Kaviel turned. "That is the list. It does not grow without my word. You do not tell Meret. You do not tell the analysts you came up with. You do not tell your family that you have been moved to sensitive work, because a young researcher moved to sensitive work is a thing a careful person can read at a dinner table from the way you stop talking about your day. You will keep talking about your day. You will have a dull day to talk about, and I will see that you do, so that the dullness is true and you are not made to lie, because a person made to lie gets caught at it and a person with a dull true day does not."

"Understood," Zola said.

"The Empress does not know." Kaviel said it plainly and watched the young face take it, the small work behind the eyes as Zola set it where it belonged and did not let it show on the front of her. "That is not an oversight and you will not treat it as one. There is a reason the Empress is not told a thing at the hour we would tell her, and the reason is that what she knows she must be able to act on, and what she acts on becomes the empire acting, and the empire acting at this hour, before we have a shape that holds, would warn the thing we are reading the shape of. I have told the head of this house, and the head of this house has not stopped me, and that is the authorization we are working under. It is thin. I want you to hear that it is thin, from me, today, so that you are never surprised by how thin it is."

She did not say Eleanor's name a second time, and she did not say the rest of it, that thin was the truest word for it and that she lay awake on the thinness of it more nights than she would have told Asha. Eleanor had heard it in the west-facing office over a cup of tea gone cold, sitting with her hands folded and the maps behind her on the wall, and had listened the whole way to the end without a word, which from her was its own kind of answer. Then she had said only that Kaviel was to work it from the records and to come to her and to no one else when she had a name, and that she would not be told the daily shape of it, because the head of a house that is asked under oath what she knew should be able to answer that she knew the daughter was working and not the hour-by-hour of what the daughter found. It was permission given as a withholding, the way Eleanor gave most things. Kaviel had taken it for what it was and gone down to open the room.

"Then we begin from the record we can stand on," she said, and came back up the table into the light. "Interlude, you would call it, if you were the captain writing this down. The Third Appointments Commission filed a record. I have a copy." She drew it from the folder she had carried in, the full session record, header and seal, the one that had been filed plain and read by no one twice. She laid it before Zola at the lamp. "Read it. Not for what is wrong with it. There is nothing wrong with it on its face, and if you go looking for what is wrong you will find a thing that is not there and build on it. Read it for what it permits."

Zola read. She read slowly, which was correct, and she read it twice, which was better, and when she set it down her finger was on Item Four.

"The carry-forward reconciliation," she said.

"Tell me what it is. In your own words, not the record's."

"A way to enter an appointment into the standing record without reviewing it again, on the ground that a prior commission already reviewed it." Zola's voice had gone careful, the voice of someone walking a floor she had only just been told might give. "You produce the prior confirmation and an attestation from the originating office, and the entry is made citing the prior authority and the original date, and the commission does not look at it fresh because the whole point of the provision is to spare them looking at it fresh."

"And what does that give a person who wishes to put a name into the record that no commission ever confirmed."

Zola was silent the length of a breath. "It gives them a door that opens on the word that an older door was already opened. If the prior confirmation is true, the entry is clean. If the prior confirmation is false, the entry still reads clean, because no one at this commission is required to go back and check that the older door was ever there."

"Yes." Kaviel set her hand flat on the record. "That is the whole of the mechanism, and you found it in two readings, and I want you to remember that you found it that fast, because everything from here will be slower and you will need to remember that the center of it was plain once you knew where to stand. Now I am going to teach you the only method that gets us past it, and you are going to be the one who runs it, because I will be pulled into other rooms and you will be the patience this work has."

She pulled a clean sheet to herself and ruled it as she spoke, three columns, the rule going down in a hand that had ruled a thousand such sheets.

"Every carry-forward entry cites a prior authority. A prior commission, a session, a date, a folio. That is the claim the entry rests on. The entry says: I am clean because that older thing confirmed me. So we do not argue with the entry. We go and find the older thing." She wrote at the head of the first column, the entry. "We take each carry-forward entry, and we read off the prior authority it names." The second column, the claimed authority. "And then we go to the record of that prior authority, the older commission's own filed roster, and we look for the appointment the entry says it confirmed." The third column, and at its head she wrote nothing, and left it blank, and tapped it once with the stylus. "And one of three things is true. The prior roster confirms it, and the entry is clean, and we move on and we do not waste another hour on it. Or the prior roster exists and the appointment is not on it, and the entry rests on an authority that did not confirm it. Or the prior commission the entry names never sat at all, or sat and confirmed nothing in that jurisdiction, and the entry rests on an authority that does not exist."

Zola was writing now, fast and clean, getting the three columns down exactly.

"You see why it is slow," Kaviel said. "There is no single page that shows the fraud. The fraud is in the gap between two pages that are each clean on their own, and to find the gap you have to hold both pages at once, the entry and the older roster it names, and most of the older rosters are not where the entry says they are, because they are forty years of imperial backlog filed across four prefectures by clerks who are dead. You will spend a day finding a roster to spend a minute reading it. That is the work. The reconciliation trace, I call it, for want of a worse name. You trace the reconciliation back to the thing it reconciles to, and you find out whether the thing is there."

"How many of the entries do we trace," Zola said.

"All of them, in the end. We start with the ones that smell, the ones in the jurisdictions that have already gone hot, and we let those teach us what a bad entry looks like, and then we run the rest against what we learned." Kaviel looked at the map a moment. "But you do not skip a clean-looking one because it looks clean. The man who built this is good, and a man who is good salts the clean ones in among the bad to make the bad look like noise. You trace them all. When you find a bad one, you do not exclaim and you do not bring it to me running. You log it, you note the entry and the claimed authority and what you found in the gap, and you set it aside and trace the next, and when you have a dozen you bring me twelve and not one, because one is a coincidence I have been wrong about and twelve is a shape, and I would rather wait for the shape."

She heard the echo of it as she said it, Asha's grain in her own mouth, six holds and three does not, the line drawn at the place where reading stopped and deciding began. She let it stand. It was the right thing said and it did not matter whose mouth had worn it first.

They worked. The morning went out of the high window and the flat gray light climbed the wall and shortened on the floor as the sun got up over the compound, and the lamp that had mattered at the first hour stopped mattering and then mattered again as the inner end of the room held its dimness into the afternoon the way deep masonry holds the cold. Kaviel took the Sennar stack, because Sennar was where the thing had first stood up off a page months back, the magistrate whose confirmation date fell before the session that was supposed to have confirmed him, the line she herself had passed over once and then not passed over. She traced the carry-forward entries in Sennar back to the prior commissions they named, and she found the first gap before the light had moved a hand's width, an entry that rested on a session of a prior appointments commission, cited by date and folio, and when she went to the older roster the session had sat and had confirmed eleven appointments and not one of them was the name the carry-forward entry claimed it had carried forward.

She did not exclaim. She logged it. Entry, claimed authority, the gap. She set it aside and traced the next.

By the middle of the afternoon she had four out of Sennar, and Zola, working slower and on the jurisdiction next to it, had two, and they had not spoken in an hour past the small sounds of the work, the turn of a page, the scrape of the lamp drawn nearer, the dry whisper of a stack squared again after a sheet was lifted from it and returned. The paper smell had warmed with the day and the bodies in the room and lost its coldness, and under it now was the faint sharpness of the lamp oil and, when the door at the corridor's end opened and closed somewhere, the brief reach of the compound's ordinary noise, a voice, a cart, the building running as it always ran while in this room two people unmade a fraud one filed page at a time.

Asha came in once, near the fourth hour of the afternoon, without knocking because the room was hers to enter, and stood at the end of the table and read over Kaviel's shoulder the way she read everything the first time, for the shape and not the argument. She said nothing about the work. She looked at Zola a moment, and at the ruled sheets growing under Zola's hand, the three columns filling, and what she saw in them she kept to herself.

"How does she trace," Asha said to Kaviel, as though Zola were not in the room, which was a thing Asha did to a junior on purpose, to see whether the junior had the discipline to keep working through being spoken over.

Zola kept working.

"She traces the way I taught her this morning and better than I traced at her year," Kaviel said. "She is slow. The slow is the asset. Leave her on it."

Asha looked at the back of Zola's bent head a moment longer, and then she put a single mark in her own small ledger, the one bound in dark board, and went out, and that was the whole of the assessment, and Zola, who had felt every second of it and had not lifted her stylus, understood when the door shut that she had passed something and did not know its name and was correct not to ask.

It was past the eighth hour when Kaviel stopped. The gray window had gone dark and the lamp was the whole of the light now, the room shrunk down to the warm circle of it and the cold dim past its edge, and her hand had cramped twice and she had worked through both. She had nine out of Sennar. Zola had five out of the two jurisdictions beside it. Fourteen entries that rested on an authority that was not there, in three jurisdictions, in a single day's reading of the easiest stacks, the ones where the work had already been pointed.

She rose and stood at the wall under the map. She had brought a stylus to it without deciding to, and now she marked, against each jurisdiction where a bad entry had stood up off the page today, a small flat dot in her own hand, Sennar first, and then the two beside it, and she stood back from the three dots and looked at the printer's grid of numbered jurisdictions running away from them across the Outer Prefectures, the dozens of them, the ones she had not yet touched and the ones beyond the Outer Prefectures where the carry-forward provision had cousins under other commissions she had not yet thought to pull.

She had come into the room that morning believing it was Sennar and its neighbors, a regional thing, ugly and contained, the kind of rot that ran in one watershed because one office had gone bad. Three dots on the map at the end of a single day, and every one of them found in the first stacks, the stacks already pointed at, told her that the believing had been a comfort and not a finding. If the easiest reading in three jurisdictions gave fourteen, then the map was wrong to be so empty, and the emptiness was the measure of how few hands had yet read for the thing, no more than that. This was not one office gone bad. An office gone bad did not reach across three jurisdictions on three feeders that did not connect, and a man did not salt clean entries among bad ones across a watershed he did not control. Someone had built this wide, and built it slow, over years, with the provision read the same way in jurisdiction after jurisdiction by hands that had to have been pointed at the provision by the same understanding, and the map in front of her was not the picture of the fraud. It was the picture of how much of the fraud she had not yet found.

Behind her Zola was squaring the last of the day's stacks and weighting them, the dry whisper of it the only sound, the lamp throwing both their shadows long up the wall and across the map so that the three small dots sat in the moving dark of them. Kaviel stood with the stylus still in her hand and looked at the jurisdictions she had not marked, the white grid of them, and counted in her head what the finding meant was still waiting out there past it, and the count was larger than the room, larger than the mandate, larger than the thin permission she was working under and the head of the house who had given it to her and not asked to be told the daily shape.

"We start from the edges," she says to no one in particular.
