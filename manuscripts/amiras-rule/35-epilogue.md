# Epilogue — The Record Complete

I have come to the end of it, which is a thing I did not believe for several of the eleven years that I would live to write. The four shelves of the east vault are catalogued and cross-referenced and bound, and the duplicate set has gone to the second archive on the far side of the complex, because a record that exists in one place exists at the sufferance of whoever holds the key to that place, and I have learned not to trust a single copy of anything that took this long to assemble. The lamp is the same lamp. The cold comes up out of the courtyard stone the way it did the night I began, and my hands have grown no younger in the keeping of these pages, so that the stylus needs more pressure now than it did to leave the same mark.

I said at the outset that I would not give you the end in the beginning, and I have held to it. I will give you the end here, because here is where it belongs, and because a record that closes without stating what it cost would be a record written by someone who was not paying attention.

The corruption was dismantled. The mechanism beneath it, the financial one that fed the faction for seven years through three channels of which the Third Appointments Commission was only the most visible, was unwound entry by entry across the months that followed, slowly enough that the resources did not flee to jurisdictions where the prerogative could not reach them, and that slowness was its own labor, performed by people whose names appear in the depositions and not in the public accounts. Lord Marshal Tewodros Marek was charged before the Throne Court of Inquiry and answered to it. I have read the full transcript of that proceeding more times than I have read any other document in these vaults, because a man does not reach the Marshalcy of the Bureau by being a fool, and I wanted to understand how a mind of that quality talked itself across the line. The transcript does not tell me. The transcript tells me only that he did.

Three institutional changes outlived the crisis, and a historian owes them to the record more than he owes it any verdict on the people. The appointments process was rebuilt under a standing oversight body seated outside the Bureau of Establishments, so that no single marshalcy could again command both the entry and the confirmation of it. The compact between the Trinitarian Throne and the De Grosvenor house was reaffirmed in formal session, the fourth such reaffirmation across three imperial generations, each prior one having required one party to extend the other a trust that had not yet been earned. And the mandate of the Lionesses was revised, on the recommendation of their own intake officer, to give the apparatus standing authority to investigate the categories of seal it had been unable to touch when this began. I record, because the woman who drafted that revision did not put her own name forward and someone should, that a researcher named Zola Adimu was confirmed to full standing in the same season, on merit assessed against the new criteria she had helped to write.

That is the institutional end of it. I have set it down plainly, in the order an oversight clerk would want, so that whoever comes after me can find it without reading the rest. The rest is harder, and it is what the record cannot hold, and I will say only what I am sure of.

I met Eleanor De Grosvenor three times. I have said elsewhere that the meetings were not warm and that the respect I record is owed and not granted. The official notation in the house ledger, copied into this account under its proper reference, reads that Eleanor De Grosvenor, head of the De Grosvenor apparatus for sixty-one years, was relieved of her duties by reason of death in the third quarter following the close of the proceedings, the succession passing as the compact provides. That is the whole of what the record holds on the matter, and it is the whole of what I will add to it.

There is one further thing in my keeping, and it does not belong to me, and I have decided where it goes.

The letter was found in a locked compartment among Eleanor De Grosvenor's private effects, sealed, bearing no dispatch mark, written in her own hand and never sent. By the terms under which her papers came to inquiry, it should have been read first by an archivist with a ledger and filed by reference. I read enough of the first line to know whose hand it was and whom it was for, and then I sealed it again and I carried it myself to the one person entitled to it before any clerk, which is a small abuse of my office and the only one I will confess to in these pages.

She read it in a room I was not in. I know what I know of that hour the way I have learned to know things, from what was said afterward by people who were near and from the shape of the silence around it, and I will not invent the rest to make it whole for you. The envelope was unsealed when it reached her and unsealed when she set it down. She read it once through without stopping, which is how I am told she reads everything, the page held at the distance the work requires, her face giving the room nothing it could have filed. She came to the line her mother had corrected twice and read past it. She came to the name at the bottom, the signature that never carried a flourish, and she read that too. Then she folded the single sheet along the creases it already held, and she put it down on the table in front of her, and she sat. She did not call for anyone. She did not weep that any witness recorded, and I have not asked, because a record has edges and that is one of them. She sat in the chair with the folded letter under her hand, in the failing light of a room that had been someone else's and was now no one's, and after a while she rose and went back to the work, because the work did not wait, which is the one thing in this account that mother and daughter never once disagreed upon.

I will not tell you what the letter said. It is reproduced in this record under its own reference, in the place where the events of its writing belong, and a reader who has come this far has already read it or will, and either way it is not mine to summarize. I will say that it was meant to arrive and did not, and that the woman it was meant for read it years too late to answer it, and that this is the kind of thing the empire is made of and the histories almost never keep, because it leaves no clause in any minute and moves no money and unmakes no throne. It only happened. I have kept it because it happened.

So I come to the Empress, as I always meant to, because the record closes on her and not on any house or any officer or any letter sealed too long. Amira holds the Trinitarian Throne. She held it through the first crisis she faced with no one above her to absorb the cost, and she governs it still, with the appointments running clean under their new oversight and the compact renewed and the Lionesses watching the seals they could not watch before. She invoked an authority that had lain unused for thirty-one years, and she did it in the open, where it could not be taken back, and she did not waver while the room decided whether to follow her. The room followed. I have wondered, putting this down, whether she knew it would, and I have concluded that she did not know and acted anyway, which is the distinction between a ruler who reigns and one who governs, and the only verdict on her I will permit myself.

The lamp wants trimming again. The hall runs empty toward the stair, as it did, and there is no sound here but the stone settling and the stylus, and when I lift the stylus there will be no sound at all. I have stated what I saw and what I was told and which of the two it was. The account is complete to the limit of what I could learn and honest to the limit of what I could bear, and I leave the rest, as every honest record must, to whoever reads it after me with a colder eye.

Closed and sealed under the hand of the compiler, the account of the first crisis of the reign of the Empress Amira, this twenty-second day of the fourth imperial quarter.

A. Khan, Captain, retired; keeper of the Imperial Record.
