# AMIRA'S RULE

### The Iseldoran Sagas — Acts One and Two

_Prologue through Chapter 18, with Interludes 1 through 4. Draft._

---

# Prologue

I have set down the date in the upper margin of this page out of habit, the way I have begun every official record for forty years, and then I have sat looking at it long enough that the lamp oil has burned down a finger's width. The Imperial Record Office is quiet at this hour. The clerks are gone. The stone holds the cold of the courtyard outside and gives it back slowly, so that even in the warm months the lower vaults stay chilled, and a man writing here past midnight learns to keep his hands moving.

My name is Adulfus Khan. I hold the rank of Captain, retired from the field and assigned now to the keeping of records, which is a posting men of my service tend to regard as a courtesy extended to the old. I have stopped resenting it. There is more danger in a room like this one than most of the court would credit, because what is written here becomes the thing that happened, and what is left out stops having happened at all. I descend from the line of Harlow Khan. I mention this so that whoever reads it understands the discipline I was raised to, and for no reason of vanity in the name itself. A soldier does not embellish his report. He states what he saw, what he was told, and which of the two it was.

What follows is the history of the first crisis the Empress Amira faced without anyone above her to absorb the cost of failure. I have spent the better part of eleven years assembling it. The testimony fills four shelves of the east vault. I have read all of it more than once, the depositions and the duplicated minutes and the field reports that came in folded so many times the creases had worn through the ink, and I have set my hand against each page the way one sets a hand against a wall in the dark, to learn the shape of a thing you cannot yet see whole.

I will tell you now what I will not do. I will not give you the end of it here. I have read records that announced their own conclusions in the first lines, and a reader who knows the ending reads everything afterward as a man already braced for the blow, which is no way to understand how the people inside it lived through the not-knowing. They did not know. For a long time they did not even know there was something to be known. That is the truth I am most concerned to preserve, and it is the hardest, because I sit here with all four shelves behind me and the temptation to make them wiser than they were is a constant pressure against the stylus.

There is the matter of the De Grosvenor house, which this record places, perhaps for the first time in the sanctioned archive, under direct historical inquiry. I am aware of what that means. Houses of that standing are accustomed to being described, not examined. I have examined it. I have done so because the events I am recording cannot be understood without it, and because Eleanor De Grosvenor, whatever else may be said of her in the chambers where such things are said, built an apparatus over six decades that held the administrative spine of this empire upright through seasons that should have broken it. I met her three times. I will not pretend the meetings were warm. I will say that I have never in my life sat across from a mind that wasted so little, and that the respect I record here is owed, not granted.

The wax on the older depositions has gone hard and dark, and when I break a seal to copy a passage it gives off a smell like a doused candle, faintly bitter, that has come to mean work to me the way the smell of cut metal once meant the field. My fingers know the difference between the imperial vellum and the cheaper stock the prefectures used for their daily traffic before I have looked down to see which I am holding. I tell you these small things because they are the texture of the work, and because a record that admits its own making is harder to lie with.

I want to be plain about why this account exists at all, since the reign survived. It survived. The Trinitarian Throne stands, and the woman who holds it holds it still. But there was a stretch of months, the months this record concerns, when that outcome was not yet decided, and the thing that nearly undid it was not an army and not an assassin. It was a process. A clause in the minutes of a routine commission, a column of dates that did not agree, a confirmation entered before the body that confirmed it had met. Small things. The empire is administered by small things, and so it can be unmade by them, quietly, with everyone's signature in the proper place.

I have decided how to record this. That decision took me longer than the writing will. I have chosen to begin where the people in it began, with no warning of what they were standing on, and to let the floor go out from under the reader at the same pace it went out from under them. It is the only honest way I know to do it, and I have spent eleven years deciding it was honest enough.

The lamp wants trimming. Beyond the vault door the long hall runs empty toward the stair, and there is no sound in this building but the stone settling and my own stylus against the page, which is the sound I will leave you with before I begin. Turn the leaf. You have already agreed to know this, by reading even this far, and I would not have you stop now and carry only the half of it.

# Chapter 1 — The Weight of the Throne Room

The incense had been lit at the fourth hour and would burn until the court adjourned, and by the time the second petition came forward the smoke had settled into the high vaulting and given up trying to rise. Amira sat in it. The ceremonial overrobe across her shoulders carried the weight of a small grown person, the brocade stiffened with metal thread along the seams so that it held its own shape whether she moved or not, and she had learned in the first year of her reign to let it hold her rather than fight it. She kept her spine against the back of the throne. She kept her hands where the court could see them. The marble in front of the dais ran in long bands of polish, and through the polish, if the light from the high windows struck it at the angle it struck it now, a person could read the old repair lines, the seams where the floor had been broken and laid again across four hundred years of audiences. The light came down in cut columns from the eastern clerestory. Where it touched the floor the stone was nearly warm. A stride to either side and the cold came up through the soles of the feet like water.

The Clerk of Petitions stood at the foot of the dais with the day's register, a tall man named Obasi whose voice had been trained to carry the length of the hall without seeming to rise. He read the first matter into the room. A waterworks dispute out of the river prefectures, two guilds and a question of who had paid for a cistern and who now owned the right to charge for it. Amira heard it through. She asked Obasi for the date of the original grant, and when he found it she asked who had witnessed it, and when the witness proved to be a man both guilds had since buried she ruled the grant lapsed and ordered the cistern held in common under the prefect's seal until a new instrument could be drawn. The guildmasters bowed and withdrew, neither pleased, neither cheated. That was the work. She did not expect to please. She expected to be exact, and to be seen to be exact, because a court that believed the throne was paying attention behaved as though the throne was always paying attention, and that belief did more governing than any decree she could sign.

The forms between matters were their own small labor. An usher came forward with the closing of the first file and laid it on the register table to the Clerk's left, and the under-clerk who kept the running minute dipped his pen and wrote, and the soft scratch of it carried up into the smoke and was lost. The court breathed in the interval. Somewhere along the southern aisle a child of one of the standing delegations coughed and was hushed. A censer-bearer crossed below the dais on soft feet to feed the nearest brazier, and the incense thickened for a moment, sweet and resinous over the flat mineral cold of the stone, and Amira felt it catch at the back of her throat the way it caught every morning by the fifth hour and made no sign of it. Her water cup stood untouched on the small shelf inside the right arm of the throne. She had learned not to drink in court. A ruler who reached for her cup gave the hall a thing to watch that was not the ruling, and the hall would watch it, and remember that she had been thirsty, and forget what she had decided.

Obasi read the second matter while the under-clerk still wrote the first. A criminal review out of the coastal customs courts: a shipping factor convicted of falsifying weights against the duty, sentenced to the loss of his license and a term of labor, appealing the term as beyond the statute for a first offense. Amira asked for the statute and Obasi read it. She asked whether the man had stood a prior conviction under any name, and the customs advocate, a brisk woman in the green sash of the revenue courts, allowed after a pause that the prosecution had not searched the registers of the two neighboring jurisdictions where the factor had traded before. Amira held the appeal. She did not free the man and she did not confirm the term; she sent the matter back to the coastal court with an instruction that the neighboring registers be searched first and the term fixed to whatever the search returned, and she set a date by which the search must close so that the man would not rot uncharged while a clerk took his time. The advocate bowed. The court understood, as it was meant to, that the throne would neither be hurried into cruelty nor talked into laxness, and that it read the statute as carefully as it read the man.

Behind her, and a little to the right, set back of the formal dais where the carved screen threw a permanent band of shadow across the floor, Eleanor De Grosvenor sat in a plain chair that had been carried in for her and would be carried out when she left. From the body of the hall she was nearly invisible. A petitioner looking up at the throne saw the Empress and the canopy and the gold; he did not see the old woman in the shadow unless he already knew to look, and the ones who knew to look were careful never to be caught doing it. Amira did not turn to her. In the four years since she had taken the throne she had turned to Eleanor in open court exactly twice, both times in the first month, and she had decided after the second that she would not do it again. The compact between the De Grosvenor house and the Trinitarian Throne was older than either of them and would outlast both, and part of what it bought the throne was that Eleanor could sit in that chair and the court would feel her there without the Empress ever appearing to need her. Amira governed. Eleanor watched her govern. The arrangement suited them both and neither of them found it comfortable, which was, Amira had come to think, the proof that it was honest.

Near the western wall, where the colonnade ran back into the administrative passages, a woman in the dark working coat of the Lionesses stood with her hands behind her and her attention moving over the hall in a slow unhurried circuit. Kaviel. She was not on the rolls of the court and held no ceremonial place; she was there because Eleanor was there, and the room registered her the way it registered a load-bearing column, as a thing that was supposed to be present and so drew no eye. Twice Amira's gaze crossed the western wall in the course of the morning and twice it passed over Kaviel without stopping, which was how both of them preferred it.

The hall held perhaps two hundred that morning, a thin court for a working session with no ceremony attached. Along the standing rails the delegations waited in the dress of a dozen prefectures, the river guilds in their banded sashes, two magisters of the Bureau of Establishments in the gray of the administrative service, a delegation of upland herders whose business stood eighth on the register and who had come down out of the high country to wait however long the throne kept them. Faces dark and faces darker, the broad cheekbones and the long bones of the eastern steppe-blood mixed through the southern lines as they had been mixed for the four hundred years the floor had been broken and relaid, a court that looked like the empire because the empire had made it. Amira knew a third of them by name and another third by office and the rest she would know by the time they reached the foot of the dais, because Obasi would have told her, quietly, in the breath before each name was called, who they were and what they wanted and what they had wanted the last time. He stood now at her right hand with the register and waited for the under-clerk's pen to lift.

Obasi read the third matter.

"A petition of grievance," he said, "out of the Sennar Prefecture. The petitioner contests a ruling of the prefectural magistrate's court and seeks the throne's review of the judgment against him."

A man came forward into the cut light below the dais and went down to one knee on the warm band of marble. He was perhaps fifty, dressed in the good but unfashionable clothes of a provincial man of property, and his hands when he pressed them together were the hands of someone who had done physical work before he had done well enough to stop. He gave his name as Demba Tunde, a holder of orchard land in the Sennar uplands. He spoke plainly. The magistrate's court had found against him in a dispute over a water easement crossing his neighbor's ground, had awarded the easement to the neighbor, and had assessed Tunde the costs of the hearing. He did not contest the throne's right to let the ruling stand. He contested the court that had made it.

"On what ground," Amira said.

"On the ground that the magistrate who heard me had no business on the bench, Majesty." Tunde kept his eyes down, as the form required, but his voice did not waver. "Magister Iwan Kebe. He sat my case in the spring. I have since learned he was set in his office not three months before, over the head of the deputy magistrate who had served the district eleven years and whom every man in the prefecture took for the next to hold the seat. I do not say the ruling was bought. I say I do not know how the man came to be the one ruling, and when a stranger costs me my water and my coin both, I would know by what right he sat."

It was a common enough grievance and a weak one, in law. A litigant who loses will find fault with the judge; the throne heard a version of it most weeks. Amira had begun, almost, to frame the refusal. She turned to Obasi. "The magistrate's commission. Is it in the prefectural record?"

Obasi was ready for the question; he had read the file before he read the petition, as he read every file, and he had the relevant page marked. "It is, Majesty. Magister Iwan Kebe, confirmed to the Sennar prefectural bench by the Third Appointments Commission, seated under the Bureau of Establishments, by carry-forward reconciliation of a prior confirmation. The entry is regular. The seal is the Bureau's."

"Read me the dates."

"The carry-forward entry is dated the eleventh of Threshing. The session of the Third Appointments Commission under which it was entered convened on the second of the Reaping month." He paused only to be sure he had the months in the right relation, then read them again in order, as a clerk does. "Threshing precedes Reaping by some five weeks, Majesty."

It went past her. It went past the hall. A confirmation entered before the session that entered it was the kind of clerical disorder that filled the lower courts and the prefectural registries, a clerk transposing two dates out of a stack of three hundred, a copyist working from a draft instead of the engrossed minute. The Bureau of Establishments reconciled thousands of such entries a year and the carry-forward provision existed for exactly that reason, to let confirmations made good under one commission be carried into the record of the next without dragging every magistrate in the Outer Prefectures back through fresh review. Amira heard the disorder, weighed it, and set it where it belonged, among the small frictions of a machine too large to run without friction. The man's complaint was that he had lost. The commission was regular. The seal was the Bureau's.

"The commission's confirmation stands on its own authority, Magister Tunde, whatever the order of the clerk's pen," she said. "A magistrate's right to the bench does not turn on which day a copyist dated the carry-forward, and a ruling does not fall because the litigant who lost it dislikes the judge. The judgment of the Sennar court holds. I will not assess you the costs of this petition; you came in good faith and the throne does not punish that. But you will keep to the easement as the court has set it." She inclined her head a degree, the dismissal. "Go home and mind your orchard."

Tunde pressed his hands together once more, the disappointment closing over his face without surprise, as though he had not let himself hope for more than to be heard. He rose. He backed the three required steps out of the cut light, turned at the edge of it, and walked the long marble toward the western doors, and the cold came up around him as he left the warm band, and the sound of his good provincial boots came back off the high stone for the length of his going.

Behind the throne, in the band of shadow, Eleanor De Grosvenor moved.

It was a small thing. She had sat the whole morning with the stillness of the very old, the economy of someone who has learned that motion costs and pays for nothing, her hands folded over the head of a cane she did not strictly need, her eyes half-lidded in the smoke. When Obasi read the second date she opened them. When he set the two months in their right order her chin came up perhaps a finger's width, and her gaze, which had rested all morning on the middle distance, found the man Tunde and held to him, and tracked him, and did not let go of him as he crossed the floor. The half-lidded fatigue was gone out of her face. What replaced it was attention, the whole of it, gathered and pointed, and it cost her something to gather it; the lines around her mouth drew in and her knuckles whitened a shade against the dark wood of the cane. No one in the body of the hall could see it. The Empress, facing forward, could not see it. The petitioner, walking away, would never know that for the length of his walk out of the Trinitarian Throne Room the most formidable mind the empire had built in sixty years was bent on him entire, reading the back of his good coat, fixing the prefecture and the magistrate and the impossible pair of dates into a memory that had given up nothing important since before the Empress was born.

Across the hall, against the western wall, Kaviel had felt the change before she could have named its source. She had spent her life in rooms with her mother and had learned to read the temperature of Eleanor's attention the way a sailor reads a shift in a wind he cannot yet see on the water. She did not look at Eleanor. She looked where Eleanor was looking, at the orchard-holder crossing the floor, and found nothing in him, a provincial man who had lost his water and would lose his appeal and go home, and she filed the nothing away because her mother had filed something, and the difference between what each of them could see was the oldest fact of their lives.

She held the file in her head the way she held everything, by its parts. Sennar Prefecture. A magistrate named Kebe set over the head of a man who had earned the seat. A confirmation entered under a clause she knew from a hundred rosters, the carry-forward, the thing the Bureau used to move old work into new books without redoing it. A date in front of a date that was supposed to follow it. Taken one piece at a time it was the ordinary disorder of a registry, and Kaviel had read enough registries to know how ordinary that disorder was, how a single clerk on a long afternoon could put the months in the wrong relation and seal the error before anyone caught it. She turned the pieces over once more and they sat where they had sat, harmless, and she let them lie. What she did not let lie was her mother's chin coming up. Eleanor did not spend attention on a clerk's wrong afternoon. She had found no weight in the matter herself. She marked it anyway. The one person alive who could find weight where Kaviel found none had bent the whole of herself toward an orchard-holder walking out a door, and that was reason enough.

At the foot of the dais Obasi had caught it too, in his way. He had read the dates as a clerk reads, in the order the file gave them, and the order had snagged on something in him a half-beat before he smoothed it over and moved to the next matter; a man who handled the register every working day of his life felt a confirmation that preceded its commission the way a weaver feels a slubbed thread under the thumb. He had said nothing. It was not his office to say. He had marked the page with the small folded corner he used for entries the throne might want again, and he would carry the file back to the record room himself rather than send it down with the under-clerk, and that was the whole of what a careful man in his place could do without overstepping it. He waited at Amira's right hand and let the next name rise to his lips.

Obasi was already reading the next matter, a question of a guild charter and a disputed inheritance, and the court resettled toward it, and the smoke hung in the vaulting, and the angled light had moved a hand's breadth along the marble while the orchard-holder spoke so that the warm band fell now a little nearer the dais than it had. Amira heard the petition into the room. She did not look behind her. The morning went on the way mornings in that hall had gone on for four hundred years, the throne ruling and the court believing itself watched, and the work of it pressing down on her shoulders through the metal-threaded brocade with a weight she had stopped, long since, mistaking for the weight of the cloth.

At the western doors the petitioner Tunde reached the threshold, where two of the door-wardens stood the watch in their formal gray, and passed between them out of the cut light and the cold stone and the incense into the colonnade beyond, and the great doors took him, and he was gone.

In the shadow behind the throne, Eleanor De Grosvenor watched the doors swallow the man, and held her eyes a moment longer on the dark where he had been. Then she lowered her chin the finger's width it had risen, and folded one hand more closely over the other on the head of the cane, and looked again at nothing.

# Chapter 2 — The Lioness Quarter

The compound did not sleep, and it did not announce that it was awake. Asha had served in buildings that performed their readiness, that put armed men at every door and polished the brass twice a day so that a visitor would feel watched the moment he crossed the threshold. The Lioness Quarter was not such a building. From the imperial district a man walking past would see a long administrative block of gray stone with shuttered windows and a single unmarked door, the kind of structure that housed a records bureau or a tax registry, and he would walk on. Inside, the corridors ran on a rhythm that never stopped. Two floors below the assessment level the kitchens had been at work since the third hour, and the smell of them came up through the stairwells in slow drifts, bread and the burnt-sugar edge of something left too long over heat, so that the whole upper floor carried a faint kitchen warmth under the harder smell of metal polish and the oil they used on the door hinges to keep them from speaking. Asha had stopped noticing it years ago. She noticed it this morning only because the candidate, when she came in, lifted her chin a fraction toward the door as though to track where the smell had come from, and Asha marked that, and set it aside, and said nothing yet.

She had come down to the assessment level a half hour early, as she always did, and walked the floor before the candidate arrived. That was an old habit and she had stopped pretending it was anything else. The Quarter ran in shifts that overlapped at the edges so that no hour found the building unmanned, and the early walk let her take the temperature of it the way a watch officer takes a sounding. In the analysis hall the long tables had been at work since before her, a dozen of the junior researchers bent over rosters under the green-shaded lamps, the scratch of their pens and the dry whisper of turned pages making the only sound on that floor, and she had passed through without a word and been noted and not greeted, which was correct. Two of the wall maps had been re-pinned overnight against a revised jurisdictional chart, and someone had left the old sheet folded on the side table with a note clipped to it for the archivist, and Asha had read the note in passing and approved of the hand that wrote it. Below her the kitchens worked. Above her, in the records vault she did not go up to that morning, the cross-reference clerks held the files no single bureau was permitted to hold all together, and somewhere out past the noon transit Silva was already in the field, sending up by the standard channel the periphery summaries that would be on Asha's table by the time the assessment was done. The building did its work in all its parts at once and made no sound about any of it, and Asha had given the better share of her life to it and walked its floor in the early hour the way another woman might walk a garden.

The room was built to be plain. A table of dark wood worn pale at the edges where forearms had rested across forty years of these conversations. Two chairs. A third against the wall for Meret, who sat in it now with a folder closed on her knee and her hands folded over it, present and saying nothing, which was how Meret was present in most rooms. On the wall behind Asha hung the organizational maps, sheet after sheet of them pinned in overlapping ranks, the protective rosters and the jurisdictional charts and the personnel assessments reduced to columns of initials and date-codes that meant nothing to anyone who had not spent a decade learning to read them. The candidate's eyes had gone to the maps first, before the chairs, before Asha. That too Asha marked. A person could be told a great deal by what they looked at when they thought the looking did not count.

"Sit," Asha said.

The candidate sat. She was young, though not so young as the rawest of them, perhaps twenty-six, with the carriage of someone who had been a soldier and the steadiness of someone who had been good at it. Her file lay open in front of Asha, the standard-issue intake ledger with its ruled columns and its space at the foot of the page where the assessing officer entered the disposition in a hand the candidate would never see. Asha had read the file the night before and again that morning. Maryam Sow, born in the river prefectures, eight years in the Fourth Imperial Cohort, two commendations she had not asked anyone to mention and a third she had declined. A clean record. A recommendation from a cohort commander whose judgment Asha respected and whose handwriting cramped at the end of the page where he had run out of room for the things he wanted to say about her. Technically, on the page, the woman was past qualified.

"You know what we are," Asha said. It was not a question, and she did not pose it as one. She watched the candidate decide whether it was.

"I know what people say you are, Officer."

"Then tell me that. I will correct the parts that are wrong."

Maryam Sow took a breath that was a degree too careful, and gave it back. "That you guard the Empress. The senior house. That the Lionesses are the throne's own hand, and that when the regular guard cannot be trusted with a thing, it comes to you."

"That is the story they tell in the cohorts," Asha said. "It is flattering and it is most of it false." She turned a page of the file she did not need to turn, to give the woman a moment to settle into being wrong. "We do stand protective detail. It is perhaps a fifth of what the Quarter does, and the least of it. The rest you would find dull. We read appointment rosters out of prefectures you have never heard of, line by line, until the line that is wrong stands up off the page. We sit in the back of commission hearings and write down who arrived together and who left apart. We hold the records that the bureaus would rather no single office held all at once, and we cross them against one another, and when two of them disagree we find out why before anyone with a reason to lie has the chance to reconcile them. We are an instrument of oversight that answers above the military and below the throne, and most days the most dangerous thing in this building is a discrepancy of dates in a ledger." She let that sit. "If you came here to guard the Empress with your body, the cohorts have men enough for that, and they will let you keep your commendations. We are looking for something else."

The candidate held still under it. That was good; the ones who flinched at being told the work was paperwork were no use, and the ones who brightened at it were sometimes worse. Maryam Sow did neither. She absorbed the correction and adjusted, the way a competent soldier adjusts to a change in the order of march, and when she spoke again her voice had reset to the new fact.

"Then I came for the something else, Officer."

"We will find out what you came for," Asha said. "That is what the morning is."

She began the way she always began, with the parts that had answers. There was a structure to the morning that the candidate could not see and that Asha had built and rebuilt across four decades until it ran without her having to think where it went next. The first part was easy on its face and was meant to be, a set of problems with clean solutions, so that the candidate would settle and stop managing her face and start showing Asha how she thought when she was not watching herself think. The hard part came after, when the candidate believed the morning had relaxed. The cohorts ran their assessments the other way around, the difficulty front-loaded so that the recruit broke early or held, and that suited the cohorts, who were sorting for men who would hold. The Quarter was not sorting for that. The Quarter could find people who would hold in any barracks in the empire. What it could not easily find, and what no examination on a page would surface, was the thing Asha was listening for now, and so she put the easy problems first and waited.

She put situations to the candidate the way the cohort exams put them, a protected individual at a transit point, a contradiction in an advance schedule, a subordinate whose account of a movement did not match the manifest, and she listened less to the answers than to the shape of how the woman reached them. Maryam Sow was quick. She did not guess; she said what she could establish and marked the edge of it where she could not, which was rarer than the cohorts believed and harder to teach than anything else the Quarter taught. On the question of the mismatched manifest she found the discrepancy and then, unprompted, named the two innocent explanations before the guilty one, and Asha felt the small clean satisfaction of watching a mind work the way the work required. By the standard of the page the woman was already through. By the standard of the page she had been through before she walked in.

Asha did not assess by the page.

She had told the truth to a great many candidates over forty years, that she knew within the first ten minutes, and most of them had taken it for the kind of thing an old officer says to seem formidable. It was not. The first ten minutes were before the candidate had learned what the room rewarded; everything after was performance, and performance could be coached, and a coached answer told her about the coach. So she watched the early minutes, and in the early minutes Maryam Sow had done one thing.

She had wanted to be right.

That was the thing, and it had shown itself in the first ten minutes the way such things always showed themselves, before the candidate had learned what the room rewarded. Asha had watched a thousand of them sit in that chair, and she had built her whole reading of a person on the early minutes for a reason she could have explained to no cohort commander in the empire and did not try to. A person walked into the Quarter carrying the habits the cohorts had paid them to keep, and the cohorts paid for one set of habits and the Quarter needed another, and the place where the two parted was not in competence. Maryam Sow was competent past the line. It was in what the competence served.

Not to get it right. The two were close enough that the cohorts never separated them, and a cohort had no reason to; a soldier who needs to be right and a soldier who needs to get it right will both work hard and both do well, and the need is fuel either way. But the Quarter was not a cohort, and the difference, which cost nothing on a good day, cost everything on a bad one. When Asha had handed her the mismatched manifest the candidate's first motion had been a small straightening, a readying, the set of a person who has been given a chance to demonstrate. When she had named the two innocent explanations she had named them well, and she had named them fast, and under the speed had been the faint pressure of a person clearing the obvious so she could arrive at the answer that would land. She had not been turning the problem over to see what it was. She had been getting to the place where she could be seen to have solved it. It was a hairsbreadth thing. On the page it did not show at all.

Asha set down the file.

"I am going to put one more to you," she said, "and I want you to take your time. There is no clock on this one." She watched the candidate not relax, because the absence of a clock was itself a pressure to a certain kind of mind, and Maryam Sow's mind was that kind. "You are running an analysis on a cluster of appointments out of an Outer Prefecture. Forty confirmations. You have been at it three weeks. Your assessment, when you file it, will go up the chain and shape what the Quarter does next, and the officer above you has made it known, not in words, that she expects you to find the irregularity, because the whole reason the file came to you is that someone senior already smells one." She kept her voice level, an exercise and nothing more. "You finish your review. The forty confirmations are clean. There is no irregularity. What do you file?"

"I file that they are clean," the candidate said.

She said it well. She said it almost at once, and that was the trouble; Asha had built the question to have a right answer that any honest person would reach, and an honest person would reach it after sitting in the discomfort of disappointing the officer above her, and would file the clean finding with the small private cost of having found nothing. Maryam Sow had reached the right answer with no cost at all, because she had heard the question as a test of her integrity and had given the answer the test wanted. She had performed the incorruptible analyst the way she would have performed the loyal soldier, the way she had reached for the answer that would land. The content was right. The thing under it was the same thing it had been in the first ten minutes. She would file the clean finding when filing it cost nothing. Asha had spent forty years learning what such a person filed when the truth was clean and inconvenient and no one would ever check, when finding nothing meant three weeks wasted and a senior officer disappointed and the quiet suspicion that a sharper analyst would have found the thing that was not there. The candidate did not know what she would file on that day. That was the disqualification. Not a flaw of character. A flaw the work would find before she did.

Across the room Meret had not moved. But she had heard the question, and she had heard the answer, and when Asha glanced at her, once, in the pause after, Meret met her eyes and held them for the length of a slow breath and then looked down at the folder on her knee. They had sat enough of these together. Asha did not need her to say it and Meret would not have said it in front of the candidate in any case. The look was the whole of the exchange.

"That is a good answer," Asha said to Maryam Sow, and meant it, because it was. "Thank you."

She asked four more questions after that, none of them assessments, all of them courtesies, the kind a person deserved who had served eight years and earned commendations she did not boast of and come into a strange building and answered honestly everything she was asked. Asha asked after the river prefectures, which she knew, and after the cohort commander, whose hand she had recognized, and she let the candidate talk, and she watched the woman ease into the talking now that the testing seemed to be behind her, not knowing it had ended ten minutes into the morning and that everything since had been Asha making sure. She was sure. The certainty was the same weight it always was, and it did not get lighter with practice, and Asha had stopped expecting it to.

"You will hear from the Quarter within the month," she said, when the courtesies were done. "However it falls, you served well to be sitting in that chair, and the cohort is the better for you. That is not a thing I say to fill a silence."

The candidate rose. She had the grace not to ask, though Asha could see the asking move behind her face and then settle, a soldier accepting that the result was not hers to be told in the room. She inclined her head to Asha and then, after a beat, to Meret, who returned it, and she let herself out, and the oiled door gave back nothing as it closed. Her boots went down the administrative corridor, even and unhurried on the stone, and Asha listened to them go and could hear, under them and behind them, the other sound of the building, the harder report of boots on the training floor two doors down where a class was working through a protective drill, the heavier rhythm of it carrying through the wall, so that for a moment the corridor held both, the candidate leaving at a walk and the work going on without her.

Meret stood and brought the folder to the table and set it down by Asha's hand and did not open it.

"Silva sent the periphery summaries up," Meret said. "They are in there. There is one she flagged." She did not say what, because Asha would read it, and Meret did not waste a sentence telling a superior the contents of a folder she was about to hand her. "She is back out by the noon transit. She said to tell you she will have more by the week's end and that you would know what she meant."

"I will," Asha said.

Meret went. The door gave back nothing again. Asha sat alone in the plain room with the smell of the kitchens still threading up through the floor and the drill going on behind the wall, and she drew the intake ledger back in front of her and turned it to the foot of Maryam Sow's page, to the ruled box where the disposition went. She uncapped the pen. She did not write a paragraph; the box did not hold one, and the Quarter had learned long ago that a long disposition was an officer arguing with herself on the page. She held the pen a moment over the box, and then she set it down to the paper and made a single notation in the cramped exact hand the candidate would never read, and capped the pen, and closed the ledger over it.

# Chapter 3 — What Eleanor Keeps

The west window let the late light in low and long, the way it always did at this hour, laying a bar of pale gold across the floor of the office and up the leg of the writing table. Eleanor De Grosvenor did not look at the light. She had chosen this room sixty years ago for the way that window faced, then learned to work with her back half to it so the glare would not fall on the page, and the courtyard below it she had not walked through in a decade. The glass showed her a square of paving, a dead fountain, a bench nobody used. She kept the window for the light it gave the room and for nothing else.

The room held what work required and refused everything else. A long table scarred from generations of inkwells. Two chairs for visitors, one of which had not been sat in for years. Behind her, shelving that ran the wall to the ceiling, the upper registers reachable only by a ladder she no longer trusted her hip to climb, so the documents she needed daily had migrated downward over time, a slow tide of paper finding its level. On the remaining walls, maps. The Outer Prefectures in one frame, hand-corrected in three colors of ink as borders and jurisdictions shifted under successive bureaus. New Terra and its administrative wards in another. A third map showed nothing a stranger could read, only a grid of cells she had drawn herself, each marked with a code, each code a person or a channel or a debt.

In front of her sat the correspondence of the day, sorted into three stacks by a hand that was not hers and then resorted by a hand that was.

She worked through the first stack without hurry. A petition from the Bureau of Establishments requesting her endorsement of a procedural revision; she read it twice, found the sentence that mattered buried in the fourth clause, and set it aside to refuse. A note from a provincial administrator who wanted her to know he had named his third son for her late husband, which told her the man was either sincere or beginning to angle for something, and she would learn which by what arrived next from his office. A summary of grain reserves she had not asked for and did not need, sent by someone hoping she would remember he existed. She remembered. That had never been her difficulty.

The cold tea sat at her elbow where the morning had left it, a skin forming on its surface. She drank a mouthful out of habit and did not taste it past the bitterness, set the cup down a finger's width from where it had been, and reached for the second stack.

The second stack was the one that mattered, and she had taught herself young to leave it for the middle of the day, when the morning's edge had worn off her judgment and the evening's tiredness had not yet found it. The first stack was theater, the things people sent so that a record would show they had sent them. The second was the things people sent without meaning to send them, the letters where the writer believed he was reporting one matter and was in fact reporting himself. She read those slowly. A request for a transfer told her who a man had quarreled with. A report filed three days late told her the office it came from had something it preferred to file in a hurry behind it. The art she had spent her life on was the art of the margin, the part of a document the writer had not thought to guard because he did not know it was speaking.

The letter from Kaviel was near the bottom, which was where it would be. Her daughter wrote to her through the same dispatch chain any senior Lioness used, on the same gray paper, in the same fixed hand they were all trained toward so that no document could be read off its penmanship. The letter concerned a verification Kaviel had completed at a peripheral waystation, a protected individual confirmed safe and moved on to a secondary location. It reported the operation closed and clean. It described the difficulty Kaviel had encountered in plain operational language, the advance record wrong, the individual not where it said, a relocation managed quietly and without incident. It did not ask for anything. It did not say more than the work needed it to say.

Eleanor read it the way she read everything, once for the words and once for what the words were built around. There was nothing personal in it, which was correct, and she felt the correctness of that the way one feels a joint that has set well after a break, the ache that means the bone knit straight. She had taught the woman to write like this. She had taught her to close an operation in four sentences and waste no fifth on reassurance. Somewhere in the years between the child who could not sleep without a lamp and the officer who reported a relocation as if reporting weather, Eleanor had done that, deliberately, with her eyes open, and she did not let herself look away from it now any more than she had let herself look away from it then.

She wrote three words on the margin, an acknowledgment that the dispatch office would log and file, and that was the whole of her reply. She set the letter on the stack of completed business and reached past it to the third stack, where the report had sat for eleven days.

It was in its case, a flat hinged box of black document leather worn pale along the spine, and she had moved it from the table to the drawer and back to the table more times in eleven days than she had moved anything in months. She did not open it now. She knew what it said. She had read it on the first day until she could recite the figures, and the figures had not changed since, and would not.

She heard the footsteps in the corridor before the knock, knew them, and said come before the knuckles touched the wood.

Sefu Adebayo had served her for thirty-one years and had learned in the first of those that she did not want to be greeted. He came in, closed the door against the draft, and stood until she gestured him to the chair that still saw use. He was a narrow man gone gray at the temple, exact in his dress without vanity, carrying a slim folder he set on the table squared to its edge. He waited.

She had hired him out of the Bureau of Establishments when he was younger than Kaviel was now, hired him for a quality she had recognized at once and had never found a clean word for, a kind of refusal to be impressed. He could read a roster of a thousand names and tell her which three had been added in a different season of ink. He did not gossip, did not speculate past his evidence, did not bring her a conclusion when a fact would do. In thirty-one years he had been wrong in front of her four times, and each time he had come to her himself to say so before she found it, which was the reason she had stopped checking his work after the first decade and the reason she trusted nothing he could not show her. He was the nearest thing the apparatus had to a second mind, and he knew it, and it had never once made him careless.

"The reconciliation schedules," Eleanor said.

"All four prefectures, as you asked." He opened the folder and turned it so it faced her. "Sennar is the one that will hold your attention."

"It already has." She drew the folder closer and read down the column. Confirmations entered under carry-forward, each tagged to the authority of a commission session, each session dated, each appointment dated. The arithmetic was meant to be tedious. Tedium was the cover the whole arrangement wore. She had spent six decades learning to read the places where a document worked hardest to be boring, because that labor was a tell, the bureaucratic equivalent of a man holding very still. "These three." She set a fingernail beside three lines. "The confirmation predates the session that authorized it. Here, here. And this one by nine days."

Sefu leaned and looked. He did not say that he had found the same three. He had marked them in faint pencil at the foot of the page, and she saw the marks, and they were the same three, and neither of them remarked on it because the agreement that they would not waste words on what was obvious between them was older than most marriages.

"The clause permits it on its face," he said instead. "Carry-forward exists to reconcile a backlog. An appointment confirmed under a prior commission, entered late under the current one. On paper there is no irregularity. The dates only fail if you check them against the session record, and the session record sits in a different bureau, and no one checks one against the other because no one has ever needed to."

"Someone built it knowing that."

"Someone built it knowing that," he agreed.

She sat back. The light had moved while they spoke, the gold bar climbing the table leg and onto the wood, reaching now toward the black case where it lay closed. She watched it come without watching it, the way she watched most things.

"The field report says the same in fewer words," she said. "A cluster of confirmations in one peripheral jurisdiction, all of them tracing to carry-forward entries, all of them recent. The report does not name the clause. The man who wrote it did not have the schedules to see what the clause was for. He saw the pattern and flagged it, and he was right to, and he has no idea how right." She paused. "If I release the report now, the Lionesses have a thread inside a week. Kaviel will pull it. She works documents. She will have the shape of this faster than anyone I could put on it, and she will not need to be told where to look, which is its own protection, because nothing in the order will point at how I came to suspect it."

"And the report's source."

"Yes." She did not move her eyes from the case. "And the report's source."

He waited. This was the part he could not carry for her, and they both knew the shape of it, and he did not pretend to weigh it on her behalf.

The source had fed the apparatus for twenty-one years. Not directly, never directly; through three intermediaries and a channel she had built so that no single link in it knew more than one other, and the man himself believed, as far as she could measure belief at that distance, that his material went nowhere and meant nothing and was read by a clerk and filed. He was a senior administrative figure with access to the bureaus where the schedules lived. He was the reason she had the schedules now. He was, in the architecture of twenty-one years, irreplaceable, and the report in the black case had come up through his channel, and it was unusual enough in its content that a careful reader on the other side, asking who had access to flag exactly this, exactly now, would draw a short list, and his name would be on it.

Release the report and the thread opened and the threat could be met while it was still small. Hold the report and the source stayed dark and the thread stayed closed and the thing on the other end of it kept growing in the dark with him.

She had made this calculation before, in other rooms, with other names in the place where his now sat. The arithmetic never changed and never got easier, because the variables refused to hold still long enough to be solved. A source was worth what he could tell you tomorrow, and you could not know tomorrow's worth today, and so you guessed, and the guess was the work. She had guessed wrong twice in her life in ways that had cost her people, and she carried the names of those two the way she carried the key in the dish, in plain view of herself, where she could look at them whenever she was tempted to think she had grown infallible with age. She had not grown infallible. She had grown only patient, and patience was a poor substitute for being right, but it was the one she had.

"There is a thing you are not saying," she said.

"There is a thing I am letting you reach," Sefu answered, which was the same thing said honestly.

"Eleven days," Sefu said. It was not a reproach. He was the only person living who could have said it without it being one. He was telling her she had let the figure climb in that drawer for eleven days, and that he had watched her do it, and that he understood why, and that he would say nothing further about the why.

"The threat does not wait for me to feel ready," she said. "I know what it costs to move now. I have not yet decided it costs more than waiting." She closed the folder of schedules and squared it to the edge as he had left it. "What does the source's traffic look like this month."

"Ordinary. He sent the report through the usual interval, nothing irregular before or since. If we act on it slowly, with other matter around it, the path back to him stays long."

"With other matter around it." She turned the phrase over. "We would need the other matter to be real. A general review of reconciliation across all four prefectures, ordered for an ordinary reason, so the report becomes one input among a dozen and not the input. That takes weeks to stand up. Weeks are what I do not have to spend if the report is right."

"And if you stand up the review without the report, to have it ready."

She looked at him then. It was the first useful thing either of them had said in a quarter of an hour, and he had said it the way he said all his useful things, sideways, as a question he already half knew the answer to.

"Then the review exists before I move," she said slowly, "and when the report joins it the report is unremarkable, and the source is buried in the noise of a process I began for my own reasons, on paper, weeks earlier." She was quiet a moment. "It costs time. The threat grows during the time. But it grows slowly, and the source survives the moving, and a source who survives is worth more over a year than a week of advantage now."

"It is your judgment."

"It is my judgment." She did not thank him. She had stopped thanking him for the thing he did best, which was to lay the choice out clean and then stand clear of it, twenty years ago, when he asked her once to stop, and she had honored it since. "Order the review. Routine language, my authority, the ordinary reason. Sennar last in the list, not first; if it leads, someone reads the list as pointing at it. Bury it third."

He wrote nothing down. He would remember it precisely, and the absence of a written instruction was its own discretion. He rose, gathered the folder, and squared his chair to the table without being asked.

"There is one more thing in the day's correspondence you have not reached," he said. "It is from the office of a councillor. It is flattery, and it is well done, and that is what I wanted you to see in it. The flattery is too well made for the man it comes from. Someone is writing his letters for him now."

"Leave it on top," she said. "I will read who is writing it."

"Eleanor." He stood at the door with his hand on it. He used her name perhaps twice a year, and always to mark a thing he judged she might not be marking herself. "You held it eleven days because the man on the other end is worth holding for. That is not a fault in the work. It is the work."

She said nothing to that. He had not asked for an answer, and giving one would have made it larger than it was. He opened the door, and the draft came in cool and smelling faintly of the corridor's stone and old wax, and his footsteps went away down the passage at the even pace they had kept for thirty-one years, growing fainter, gone.

The light had reached the case. It lay across the worn black leather and warmed it to brown, and the small brass clasp threw a thread of brightness onto the table.

Eleanor sat with it a while. She did not open it again. She knew the figures; opening it would only let her read them once more and pretend the reading was deciding, when the deciding was done. The review would go up under her name in routine words. The report would wait a little longer in its case, joined later to a process built to hide where it came from, and the man twenty-one years in the dark would stay in the dark and stay alive in it, and the thing on the far end of the thread would grow some measured amount in the weeks that bought him, and she had weighed the one against the other with her eyes open, as she had weighed every loss she had ever chosen to take.

She drew the case toward her, set both hands flat on its lid for a moment, the leather cool under palms that had held thousands of such cases over six decades and had learned the exact weight at which a document stopped being information and became a debt. Then she lifted it, opened the drawer at her right knee, and laid the case inside on its bed of felt. She closed the drawer. She turned the small key in the lock and drew it out and set it in the dish where she always set it, in plain view, where anyone might take it and no one ever would.

The bar of gold had slid off the table and onto the floor again and was thinning toward the window as the sun dropped behind the wing. Eleanor reached past the locked drawer for the councillor's letter Sefu had left on top, turned it to the failing light, and began to read whose hand was hiding inside another man's name.

# Chapter 4 — Kaviel

Sabra Crossing had been built for movement, and it punished anyone who stood still. Kaviel had learned that on her first posting and never unlearned it, so she walked. She kept to the third concourse, where the light came down from panels set into the ceiling at intervals close enough that nothing ever cast a true shadow, and the air carried the burnt-oil weight of engine exhaust that the ventilation never cleared, only stirred. Around her the crowd moved with the rhythm of people who knew where they were going and how long they had to get there. That rhythm was the thing she read. A body that broke it, that slowed or doubled back or held a doorway too long, told her more than any face.

The advance record said the protected individual would arrive on the eastern transit at the seventeenth hour and present at the rebooking counter under a travel name, and that Kaviel would confirm the arrival, observe the onward boarding, and file the movement closed. Routine. She had run the same shape of operation forty times and could have written the report before leaving the compound. The report was already half-written in her head, in the flat administrative voice she used for these things, the voice that gave nothing away and recorded only what could be defended later.

The seventeenth hour came. The eastern transit unloaded its passengers in a long exhalation of bodies and luggage and the smell of food carried in cloth, and the protected individual was not among them.

Kaviel did not stop walking.

She let the disappointment register and pass, the way she let everything register and pass, and she turned the fact over. A missed transit was not yet a problem. People missed transits. The record could be wrong; advance records were compiled by people, and people were the weakest part of any system. She walked the length of the concourse to the rebooking counter and stood at the periphery, near a vendor stall where a woman was turning skewers over coals, the fat catching and snapping, and she watched the counter for the eleven minutes it took to be sure the travel name was not going to present itself.

Silva found her there. Silva had been working the upper gallery, where the rail looked down over all three concourses, and she came down the stair without hurry and stopped at Kaviel's shoulder, close enough to speak under the noise.

"Eastern transit ran on time," Silva said. "I had the platform the whole way. Nobody matching presented at the gate."

"The record says eastern."

"The record can say what it likes."

Kaviel let that sit. Silva did not offer theories, which was one of the reasons Kaviel had requested her. Silva observed and reported and left the meaning to whoever was paid to assign it. The meaning, in this case, fell to Kaviel.

"Pull the arrivals board," Kaviel said. "Everything inbound this jurisdiction since the fifteenth hour. I want origins, not destinations."

Silva went. Kaviel stayed with the vendor and the coals and let her eyes do the slow work along the counter line, the queue, the benches where people waited with the worn patience of those who had waited often. She bought a skewer she did not want, because a woman buying food was a woman with a reason to stand where she stood, and she held it and let it cool in her hand and thought about the advance record.

Advance records were assembled from booking data, and booking data could be entered wrong, or the protected individual could have been moved up a transit by a nervous handler, or the booking could have been made under a name nobody had passed to Kaviel because somebody upstream had decided she did not need it. The first was carelessness. The second was reasonable. The third was the one that made the back of her neck cool, and she set it aside because she had no evidence for it and inference that ran ahead of evidence was how operations went wrong.

She knew this work. That was the trouble Kaviel carried into every concourse like a stone in a coat pocket. She was good at it in a way that had nothing to do with wanting to be, good the way her mother was good, and the knowledge that the talent had come down the same channel as the name made it heavier to use. She read crowds because reading crowds was in her hands the way a craft sits in the hands of someone raised to it. She had not chosen the craft. She had chosen, at twenty-two, only to stop pretending she could do anything else.

There was a version of this work that other operatives performed, one she had watched and never managed to imitate. They ran a movement as a problem to be closed and went home empty, the file shut, the day done. For them the protected individual was a parcel with a heartbeat, a thing routed from one safe point to another, and there was no shame in that; the parcels arrived safe, the reports stood up, the system held. Kaviel closed her files as cleanly as any of them and could not make herself empty afterward. She carried the faces. She had a memory built for it, the same memory her mother had, the one that filed origins and arrival times and the angle of a stranger's attention without being asked, and the same memory would not let her set a face down once she had picked it up. She had moved a man through a crossing like this one four years ago and could still describe the way his hands shook on the rail and the routing she had written for him and the gate he had vanished through, and she had no use for any of it. The system did not require her to remember. She remembered anyway. It was the cost of being suited to the thing, and she had stopped expecting it to come due less often.

She thought, standing in the yellow light, of the last time she had stood in the Empress's presence on business of her own. It had been a briefing, brief and exact, Amira reading the movement summary Kaviel had filed and asking two questions, both of them the right questions, neither of them warm. Amira had thanked her by name, De Grosvenor, the name doing its work in the room the way it always did, and Kaviel had stood there carrying it and answered as the officer she was and not as the daughter the name announced. The Empress treated her as competent, which was the only currency Kaviel trusted, and the Empress was careful with her in the way one is careful with a person whose house one cannot afford to misjudge. That care was not friendship. Kaviel preferred it to friendship. Friendship asked things she did not know how to give. Competence she could deliver to the last decimal, and competence was what the room beyond the engine wash and the boarding gates was asking of her now.

Silva returned. "Three inbound transits since fifteenth hour. Eastern, which we had. A southern feeder out of the prefecture line, came in at the sixteenth hour, off the main board because it terminates here. And a re-route, northern stock, posted late, arrived sixteen-forty."

"The southern feeder. Where does it originate?"

"Sennar." Silva said it without weight, because to Silva it was a place name. To Kaviel it landed differently, though she could not yet have said why; only that the word had been crossing administrative desks lately in connection with appointments and magistrates, and her memory filed things whether she asked it to or not. She put it in the same drawer as the missing name and closed the drawer.

"A feeder that terminates here doesn't post to the onward board," Kaviel said. "Anyone arriving on it who needed to rebook would have to come to a counter on foot. They wouldn't appear in the transit arrivals at all. They'd appear in the rebooking queue."

"Which we're watching."

"Which we're watching for a name that may not be the name they used."

Silva absorbed this. "Then we're watching the wrong thing."

"We're watching one thing. We need the other." Kaviel set the cold skewer down on the edge of the vendor's stall. "Describe what we have. Not the name. The person."

It was a thin description, the kind that came down through three handlers and lost detail at every pass. A woman, middling height, traveling alone, would be carrying a document case of a kind issued by a peripheral records office, would not want to be looked at and would therefore have practiced not being looked at, which to a trained eye was its own signal. Kaviel had moved enough frightened people to know how the practiced ones held themselves: too still, or too busy, the stillness of someone counting exits.

The advance brief had given her a name and a transit and an hour, and two of the three were already wrong, and Kaviel turned that over again without letting it become a theory. A brief was a tool, and a tool that came back wrong told you something about the hand that had made it. Either the hand was careless or the hand was working from bad material or the hand had been given bad material on purpose, and she had no way yet to tell which, and so she did the thing the work required when the paper failed, which was to stop trusting the paper and start trusting the floor. The floor did not lie. People on a floor did what frightened people did, what bored people did, what waiting people did, and they did it in patterns older than any record, and if she read the patterns instead of the brief she would find the woman whether the brief had told the truth or not.

"What did the handler chain look like," she said. "On the advance."

"Three names before it reached us," Silva said. "I didn't pull the third. It was a peripheral office, the routing came through a relay I didn't recognize."

"Don't pull it now. Note that you didn't recognize it." Kaviel kept her eyes on the queue while she said it. A relay Silva did not recognize was a thread, and threads were for later, for a desk and a lamp and a file, not for a crowded concourse where pulling on one might tighten something around the woman they had come to move. "We close the movement first. The relay keeps."

"The southern feeder terminus is the second concourse," Silva said. "If she came in on it and nobody met her, she's had ninety minutes to learn she's alone."

"Then she's done one of two things." Kaviel was already moving, and Silva fell in a half step behind and to the left, the position that let her watch what Kaviel's back could not. "She's found a corner with sightlines and stayed in it. Or she's tried to solve the problem herself and is in the rebooking queue under whatever name she could buy."

They took the second concourse first, because the queue was the place a person went to act, and frightened people who had been trained at all preferred acting to waiting. The rebooking hall down here was older, the ceiling lower, the light yellower and humming faintly in a register Kaviel felt behind her eyes more than heard. The queue ran four deep along a rope line worn smooth by hands. She walked it slowly, a traveler reading the board, and she went through the faces the way she went through a roster, discarding most at a glance, holding a few for a second pass.

The woman was not in the queue.

Kaviel made the full length of it, turned, and made it again from the other side so that no one she had passed once saw her pass twice from the same angle. Nothing. She stepped out of the flow and stood by a pillar where the light pooled and let her gaze go soft and wide, taking the whole hall as a field rather than a set of individuals, which was how the breaks in rhythm showed themselves.

There. Not the woman. A man.

He was standing at the far end of the hall near the document desk, and he had been standing there too long, and he was doing it well, with a folded transit slip in his hand that he consulted now and then the way a man consults a slip while he waits for someone. But he was not waiting the way the others waited. The others waited toward the counters, toward the thing they wanted. He waited toward the hall, his attention turned outward across the floor, and twice in the time Kaviel watched him his eyes went to the same bench against the side wall and came back. He had a courier's satchel across his body, the flat hard-cornered kind that carried documents and nothing else, and a face that would not survive a description, even features over an unremarkable build, the sort of face a witness would later swear they could not recall and would be telling the truth.

Kaviel did not look at the bench. She looked at the board, and at the vendor, and at the floor, and she let the bench arrive at the edge of her vision on its own.

A woman sat at the end of it. Middling height, document case on her knees held with both hands, very still. Too still. Counting exits.

"Side wall, the long bench," Kaviel said, low. "Far end. Case on her lap."

"I have her," Silva said. "There's a man by the document desk who keeps finding her."

"I know."

"Is he ours?"

"No." Kaviel watched the man consult his slip again. He was not advancing on the woman. He was not signaling her. He had the loose attention of a person noting something for later, a person whose work was to see and to carry what he saw somewhere else, and that was all Kaviel could read in him, and she filed it without a frame because a frame was more than the evidence would hold. A man waited near a bench at a transit crossing. People did that. She let him be a face in the crowd, which was what he was, and she moved her attention back to the only thing that was hers to solve, which was getting the woman off that bench and onto a transit without either of them ever appearing to have met.

There was a decision in it that she made in the space of a breath and would have defended to any board that asked. She could move on the man. She could have Silva mark him, follow him, learn the satchel's contents and the relay he answered to, and there was a version of the night where that was the right call and the woman was bait set on a bench to draw exactly that response. But the woman was real and frightened and hers, and a protected individual exposed in a crowded hall did not get more protected while her escort chased a face she could not even describe. The brief was to move the woman. So she would move the woman, and the man would go on being a fact she had no frame for, and she would carry his face the way she carried the others, into a file she might never open. She had learned the hard part of the work years ago, that the thing in front of you you were sent to do came before the thing beside it you were curious about, and that curiosity indulged at the wrong moment was how protected people stopped being protected.

The cleanest method was the oldest. Kaviel did not approach the woman. She approached the rebooking counter, joined the short queue at the adjacent desk, and while she stood there she took from her coat a transit voucher of the kind the compound issued for exactly this, blank but for the imperial chop, and she filled in an onward routing in the small precise hand she had been taught. Then she did the thing that frightened people responded to better than rescue: she became boring. She finished at the counter, turned, and walked toward the bench at the unhurried pace of a traveler whose business was done, and as she came level with the far end she sat, leaving a respectful gap between herself and the woman, and set her own case down, and exhaled the way a tired person exhales.

The woman's hands tightened on her case.

"They overbooked the northern re-route," Kaviel said, to the air, to no one, the complaint of a stranger sharing a bench. "Three hours I've lost. If you're waiting on the same routing, save yourself the queue. They're sending people round to the seventh gate." She set the voucher down on the bench between them, face up, the chop visible, and stood, and gathered her case. "Seventh gate," she said again, mildly, and walked away without looking back.

It was a small thing and it depended on training. An untrained person would have left the voucher or called after her, confused, or worse, would have stood and followed, fixing the two of them together in the memory of everyone on the bench line. A trained one understood a handoff when it sat down beside her and complained about a transit. The woman picked it up. Kaviel did not see her do it; Silva, from the gallery she had climbed back to without being told, saw it and said so through the bead in Kaviel's ear, one word. "Taken."

The relief of that word was a thing Kaviel did not let herself feel until it was already over. The handoff was the part that could not be redone. If the woman had panicked, if she had read the voucher as a trap and frozen, Kaviel would have had to go back, and a second approach to the same bench was the kind of pattern that turned a face in a crowd into a witness with something to tell. The woman had not panicked. She had been trained well enough by whatever peripheral office had sent her up the line that she knew how to be handed a future and not look at the hand that gave it. Kaviel filed that too. A frightened woman who took a cold handoff cleanly was a woman someone had taken trouble over, and trouble taken was a thing worth knowing about a person you were moving, though it was not, again, hers to chase tonight.

Kaviel walked the long way to the seventh gate, through the first concourse and back down, so that anyone tracing her path would trace a traveler killing three hours and not an operator running a line. She bought tea she did not drink, the paper cup hot through her glove, the smell of it sharp and overbrewed. By the time she reached the seventh gate the woman was already there, standing at the margin of the boarding crowd with the voucher in her hand and the document case held a fraction less tightly, and Kaviel passed within two strides of her and did not slow and did not look, and confirmed in that passing only that the woman was on her feet, in the right place, holding the right paper.

The northern re-route boarded in a press of bodies and luggage, the gate attendant calling routings in a flat voice over the noise, the engine wash beyond the glass throwing heat and the reek of fuel back into the hall. Kaviel stood at the pillar nearest the gate and watched the woman move into the crowd. She watched the document case go past the attendant. She watched the practiced stillness of the woman's shoulders ease by a degree she doubted the woman knew she carried, the small unclenching of a person who has stopped counting exits because she has found one. The crowd took her. The boarding line compressed and shuffled and closed, and the gap where the woman had stood filled with other travelers, other shoulders, other bodies each bound for somewhere of its own, and the woman was gone into them as completely as a stone into water.

Kaviel filed the movement closed in her head, the flat voice, the defensible record. Protected individual arrived southern feeder, originating Sennar, sixteenth hour. Located, verified, onward routed northern re-route, seventh gate. Movement closed.

She did not move from the pillar. Silva came down and stood with her and did not speak, which Kaviel valued, because the thing in her now was not a thing she had words for and would not have spoken if she had. The advance record had said eastern transit, and the woman had come in on a feeder out of Sennar that did not post to any board, and no one upstream had passed Kaviel that origin, and a man with a face she would never be able to describe had stood by the document desk finding the same bench with his eyes. None of it was evidence. All of it was inference running ahead of what she could prove, and she knew the difference and held to it.

She watched the place in the crowd where the woman had been. The bodies kept moving through it, indifferent, each of them bound for somewhere of its own, and under the engine noise and the yellow hum of the light Kaviel stood very still and wondered, for the first time in the whole operation, whether the woman she had just moved was the one she had been sent to move.

The assessors confirmed that each of the six appointments in question bore a prior confirmation and an attestation from its originating office. The registrar directed that the six be entered under the carry-forward reconciliation, citing in each entry the prior Commission's authority and the date of original confirmation rather than the date of present entry, as the provision requires. The motion carried. The clerk read back the disposition of all one hundred and sixteen appointments, and the record was found correct as read.

# Chapter 5 — The Old Agreement

The room had been chosen long before either of them was born, and both of them knew it. It sat between the Empress's working quarters and the corridor where her household slept, small enough that no third chair would have fit without crowding the table, and arranged so that whatever was said in it left no trace in the registers. There were two chairs. There was a low table with a tray, and on the tray two cups. A window looked out onto an interior garden whose path nobody had walked in a season, and neither woman so much as turned her head toward it.

Amira poured. The Empress of the Trinitarian Throne lifted the pot herself, with no servant in the room to do it, because the absence of servants was the point of the room. Steam rose off the cups and broke against the cold of the stone walls. The garden window let in no warmth at this hour; the heat went straight up out of the liquid and disappeared, and the cups were already cooling before either of them touched them.

Eleanor sat with her hands folded over the head of a cane she did not lean on. She had walked into the room without it earlier in her life. She had walked in with someone's arm later in her life. Now she walked in with the cane and used it for neither, holding it the way she held everything else, as an instrument kept ready in case it became required.

"You held a commission review eleven days," Amira said. It was not a question, and she had not phrased it as one. She set Eleanor's cup nearer to her, an inch, no more.

"The Bureau of Establishments holds a great many reviews," Eleanor said. "I read more of them than I act on."

"I did not ask which you read."

Eleanor took the cup. She wrapped both hands around it and did not drink. The warmth would last perhaps a minute against her palms in a room this cold, and she let it last.

The first move had been made and answered, and both of them recognized it as a single exchange rather than two. Amira had told Eleanor that she knew Eleanor was sitting on something. Eleanor had told Amira that she would not be drawn by a date count. Neither sentence had carried its meaning on its surface. The surfaces were courtesy. Underneath the courtesy two people who had spent years measuring each other were taking the measure again, because the measure changed, and a year-old reading of Eleanor De Grosvenor was a dangerous thing to govern by.

"There is an arrangement between your house and this throne," Amira said. "Older than my grandmother. Older than your tenure."

"There is."

"I have read the terms of it perhaps four times. Each time I find them written so that they can be honored without ever being comfortable."

"They were drafted by people who did not trust each other," Eleanor said, "and who needed each other regardless. Comfort was not among their aims."

Amira drank. She had a way of drinking that gave nothing, the cup raised and lowered without expression, and Eleanor watched it the way she watched all such things, filing the absence of information as a kind of information.

The compact was not a document Amira could amend by signature, and Eleanor was not a subordinate Amira could compel by command. That was the whole shape of it. The De Grosvenor house served the throne, and the service was real, and it had teeth and reach and six decades of accumulated obligation behind it. But it served on terms the throne had agreed to before the throne understood what it was agreeing to, and one of those terms was that the house decided what the throne was told, and when, and whether the telling left the house exposed. An Empress could ask. The asking was honored. The answer was the house's to time.

"I want the report," Amira said.

"I know."

"Then we understand each other."

"We understand the half of it that you have stated," Eleanor said. "There is another half. I will state it when you are ready to hear it as a condition rather than a request."

Amira set the cup down. The faint sound of porcelain meeting wood was the loudest thing the room had produced. Outside, beyond the unwatched garden, a bell somewhere in the administrative wing marked an hour neither of them counted, and faded, and left the stillness heavier than before.

"You taught me," Amira said, "in the second year, that a ruler who demands the contents of every report ends with reports written to be demanded. I have not forgotten it. I am not asking you to empty your drawers."

"No. You are asking me to empty one drawer."

"I am asking you to tell me whether what is in that drawer touches the throne."

Eleanor turned the cup a quarter in her hands. The tea had gone tepid against her palms; she could feel the heat leave it, the way she could feel most things leave, by degrees and without announcement.

"Everything touches the throne," she said. "That is not evasion. It is the condition of the work. The question is not whether. The question is how far back the touch reaches, and that I do not yet know with the precision I require before I bring it to this room."

"Precision," Amira repeated.

"If I bring you a shape, you will rule on the shape. You cannot help it. It is what you are for. And a ruling on a shape commits the throne before the throne knows what it has committed to. I have watched that mistake unmake better houses than mine."

There was the second half, or the edge of it, brought out far enough that Amira could see it had a body without yet seeing the body. Eleanor wanted something. She had wanted it from before she sat down, and she had let Amira open the conversation so that the wanting would arrive as the answer to a question rather than as a demand into silence. Amira understood the maneuver because she used it herself, in the throne room, with petitioners who did not know they were being maneuvered. It was strange to sit on the receiving side of it and recognize the technique and choose, for the length of this conversation, not to resent it.

"Say the condition," Amira said.

"Not yet."

"Eleanor."

The name, used plainly, was itself a move. Amira did not use it often. In the throne room she said De Grosvenor, or she said nothing and let a glance carry it. Here, with no scribe to record the familiarity and read into it, the given name was a pressure applied at a precise point, and Eleanor felt the point.

"When I give you the report," Eleanor said, "I will have spent something to give it. There is a person whose continuance depends on this matter being handled in a way that does not announce how I learned of it. I have protected that person for longer than you have governed. I will not trade them for the satisfaction of resolving your curiosity eleven days early."

"It is not curiosity."

"I know it is not. I said it to hear you deny it, and you denied it the way you deny things that are true. You are worried."

Amira did not answer that. She poured more into her own cup, though the first had not been finished, and the act of pouring filled the silence the way a person fills a silence when the alternative is conceding a point. The fresh liquid steamed and the steam died against the cold, and the smell of it, faint and bitter and herbal, hung a moment in the still air between them and then was gone.

"There is a matter from the Sennar Prefecture," Amira said. "A petitioner in court. A magistrate's ruling contested on procedure. I sat through it three weeks ago and signed off on the procedural finding and thought no more of it. You were behind the dais. You did not move the entire session, except once."

Eleanor's face did not change, which was its own confirmation, and Amira read it.

"You will not tell me what you saw," Amira said.

"I will tell you that I saw something. I will tell you that I am not yet certain it is one thing rather than three coincidences that resemble one thing. And I will tell you that if it is one thing, it is not confined to Sennar, and the work of confirming that is the work I am doing in the eleven days you object to."

"I did not object. I noted them."

"You noted them in a room with no scribe," Eleanor said. "That is an objection with the record removed. I have done the same to people I worked under. It does not offend me. It tells me you have begun to suspect that the throne's information is being shaped before it reaches you, and that you have begun to suspect it might be shaped by the very house you rely on to keep it clean."

The accusation that had not been made hung now fully in the room, named by the only person who could have named it without it becoming an incident. Amira had not said it. Eleanor had said it for her, and in saying it had drawn its teeth, because a suspicion spoken aloud by its object is harder to hold as a suspicion. It was a generous move and a defensive one at once, and Amira marked it as both.

"I rely on your house," Amira said, "because the alternatives are worse, and because in forty years it has not failed the throne in a way I could prove. That is not affection. You have never asked me for affection."

"No."

"You have asked me for trust, which is a colder thing and a more expensive one, and I have extended it on the understanding that it is conditional and that you would tell me when the conditions changed. Have they changed?"

Eleanor was quiet for a long moment. The cold of the room had reached her hands fully now; the cup gave back nothing. Somewhere past the window a gardener's tool had been left against the wall a season ago and had begun to rust, and the iron smell of it would have reached the room if either of them had opened the window, and neither of them ever would.

"They are changing," she said. "I cannot tell you yet whether they have changed, which is a different statement, and the difference is the eleven days."

"And the condition you are not yet ready to state."

"Is that when I tell you, you let me tell you in full and at once, and you do not act on the first third of it because the first third is the part that exposes my source and resolves nothing. I am asking you to receive a wound and not flinch until I have shown you the whole of the body. Most rulers cannot do that. They flinch at the first cut and the surgeon loses the patient."

Amira considered the cup in her hands rather than the woman across from her. To look at Eleanor while deciding was to give Eleanor the decision in advance; the old woman read faces the way Amira read petitions.

"You are asking me to surrender the timing of my own response," Amira said.

"I am asking you to lend it to me, briefly, the way the throne lent my house the timing of its reports two hundred years ago, in this room, for reasons that have not improved with age and have not gone away either."

"And if I will not lend it."

"Then you will have the report tomorrow," Eleanor said, "incomplete, and you will act on it because you cannot help acting, and you will be right about the small thing and blind to the large one, and the person I have protected for twenty-one years will be the cost of your being right early. I will give you the report either way. The compact requires it. The compact does not require that I give it well."

That was the heart of it laid bare, as bare as Eleanor would lay anything. The compact bound her to deliver. It did not bind her to deliver in good order, with the source shielded and the shape complete. The difference between a report flung onto the table to satisfy the letter and a report carried in whole to serve the throne was a difference of perhaps two weeks and one human life, and Eleanor was telling Amira the price of each, and letting Amira choose, and not pretending the choice was anything other than hard.

Amira did not answer at once. She crossed to the window she had been ignoring all evening and stood before it without seeing the garden, her back to Eleanor, her hands joined behind her at the small of her back in the posture she took on the dais when a petition ran long and she wished to consider it without a hundred faces reading her. The glass had fogged faintly where the cold of the pane met the warmer air of the room, and her breath added to it, and through the blur the dead path and the rusting tool and the unpruned hedge had no edges at all.

"My grandmother distrusted your house," she said to the glass. "She told me so. She said the De Grosvenors had served four reigns and ended three of the four better off than they began, and that no servant grows richer than the master and stays a servant. She said it the year before she died. She also said that she would sooner be blinded by your house than given true sight by any other, because your house at least knew the value of the eyes it took. I did not understand her then. I have come to."

"Your grandmother and I disagreed about a great many things," Eleanor said. "We did not disagree about that."

Amira turned from the window. "You knew her."

"I served under the woman who served under her. I was young. She frightened me, which I did not let her see, and she knew it anyway, which I did not let her see that I knew. We managed each other for nineteen years and never once said a true thing in a room with a scribe in it. The arrangement we are sitting inside, you and I, is the arrangement she and I made habitable. It was drafted by the dead. We are the ones who have to live in the house they built, and the roof leaks where they wanted windows."

It was the most Eleanor had volunteered in the conversation, and Amira understood it was not warmth. It was provision of context, the laying of one more fact on the table so that the trade between them could be priced correctly. Eleanor gave nothing without a reason, and the reason here was to remind the Empress that the timing she was being asked to lend had been lent before, in this room, by a harder woman than Amira, to a younger and more dangerous Eleanor, and that the throne had survived the lending.

"You are telling me it has worked before," Amira said.

"I am telling you it has held before. Those are not the same. It held because both parties understood the cost of breaking it and chose the cost of keeping it, which is always higher in the moment and lower across the years. Your grandmother chose to keep it on a night she wanted to break it more than I have ever wanted anything since. I was in the room. I watched her choose. I have never told anyone what she gave up to make that choice, and I will not tell you, because the keeping of it is part of what makes my house worth the compact you dislike."

Amira returned to her chair and sat. She had not meant to sit again; standing had been the closing posture, the signal that the meeting was ending, and to sit again was to reopen what she had begun to close. Eleanor noted the reversal and gave no sign of noting it. The tea on the tray had passed beyond tepid into the flat cold of liquid that has taken the temperature of the room, and the steam was long gone, and the smell of it had thinned to almost nothing.

"There is a thing I have not said," Amira said.

"There is. You have been deciding whether to say it for the length of this conversation. Say it or do not. I will not pull it out of you. I am too old to enjoy the work and you are too able to make it easy."

Amira looked at her hands. "If your house is the source of the shaping, I cannot use your house to find the shaper. I would be asking the hand that moved the record to tell me which fingers were dirty. I have lain awake on that. It is the worst version of the problem and I have no instrument for it that does not run through you."

There. The thing beneath the thing, the fear under the suspicion, brought into the open by the Empress herself rather than left for Eleanor to name a second time. It cost Amira to say it, and the cost showed only in the slowness of the saying, the way a person sets down something heavy with care because dropping it would be worse than carrying it.

Eleanor let it sit a moment before she answered, which was respect, or the form of respect that she had instead of the feeling.

"If my house were the source," she said, "I would not be sitting in this room asking you for two weeks. I would have given you the incomplete report on the first day, let you act on the small true thing, watched you congratulate yourself, and steered you past the large thing while you were busy being right. The fact that I am asking you to wait, that I am spending my standing to buy time I could have used against you, is the only assurance I can offer that I am not the danger. It is not proof. There is no proof you could verify without an apparatus you do not have. It is the behavior of a house that is not betraying you, offered in place of a guarantee that does not exist. You will have to weigh it as that."

"I have weighed it as that," Amira said. "It is why you have your two weeks and not your dismissal."

"Then we are agreed, and we have spent an hour saying so."

"We have spent an hour making certain the agreement would hold under weight," Amira said. "That is not the same as saying so. You taught me that as well, though I do not think you meant to."

A sound passed between them then that was almost, in Eleanor, the ghost of amusement, gone before it could be confirmed, leaving only the slight settling of the lines at the corner of her mouth into a fraction less severity than before. She lifted the cold cup, looked at it, and set it down again without drinking, having confirmed for herself only that it was cold.

Amira stood. She did it without haste, smoothing the front of her robe with one hand, and the movement returned the room to its proper order, the Empress on her feet and the head of a serving house seated, the familiar names set down and the titles taken up again.

"You will have your two weeks," Amira said. "I will not flinch. When you bring it, you bring it whole, and you bring it to this room, and there will be no scribe. After that the timing is mine again."

"After that the timing is yours," Eleanor agreed.

Amira moved toward the door that led to her quarters. With her hand near it she paused, not turning fully, her profile lit cold from the garden window she still did not look at.

"The thing you saw in the throne room," she said. "When you bring me the whole of it. I will want to know whether I should have seen it too."

"You will not like the answer."

"I rarely do."

Eleanor rose then, slowly, the cane taking weight for the first time since she had entered, and she set the untouched cup back on the tray with both hands so that it would not rattle, and she inclined her head the precise depth that the head of the De Grosvenor house inclined it to the Trinitarian Throne, and no further.

# Chapter 6 — The Report Arrives

The report came up through the standard chain a little after the tenth hour of the night, which was the hour Kaviel preferred for anything that would ask something of her. The compound had gone quiet by then. The training floors were dark, the day's intake assessments filed, the mess two levels down scrubbed and shuttered, and the building had begun the slow settling it did once the people in it stopped moving through it, the small reports of cooling stone and contracting metal that an unfamiliar ear would have taken for footsteps and that Kaviel had long since learned to hear as the place breathing. The night runner who brought the dispatch knew better than to knock hard. He set the gray case in the slot built into her door for exactly that purpose, and she heard the soft double tap of the flap, and the runner was gone before she rose to take it.

She had been at the desk since the eighth hour with the lamp turned low and the window cracked the width of two fingers. She thought better with something moving, had since she was a girl, and the office held its air too well; with the window shut the room grew close by midnight and her attention thickened with it. So she kept the gap, and the cold came in off the inner court in a thin steady draw, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone from the afternoon's rain and, under it, the lamp oil, and under that the cold remains of the meal she had not finished, a heel of bread and a smear of bean paste going hard on its dish at the corner of the blotter. She had meant to eat it hours ago. She had meant to do a number of things hours ago.

She brought the case to the desk and opened it.

She knew the hand on the cover sheet before she read a word of it. It was the routing hand of the central dispatch office, fixed and characterless, the same hand every senior file wore so that nothing could be read off its penmanship, and it told her only that the document had passed through Eleanor's authority and been released downward to the Lionesses for action. It did not say released by Eleanor. The chain did not work that way and was not meant to. A file came down with an authority stamped on it and a routing assigned, and the officer who received it worked the file and not the question of who had decided she should have it. Kaviel had been trained to that discipline by the woman whose authority sat on the cover sheet, and she did not waste the night's first clear hour wondering why the report had come now and not a month ago. The report had come now. That was the fact the work began from.

She read it once through without stopping, the way she read everything the first time, for the words and nothing else.

It was a field report out of a peripheral records jurisdiction, filed by an administrator whose name had been redacted to an office code before it reached her, which was ordinary for material of this grade. The body of it was short. The writer had noticed, in the course of routine work, a cluster of judicial and administrative appointment confirmations in his jurisdiction that shared a single trait: every one of them had been entered into the standing record under carry-forward reconciliation rather than fresh review, and every one was recent, and the density of them in a short span sat oddly against the writer's experience of how the clause was normally used. He did not name the clause as a fault. He did not name it at all in the language of suspicion. He set the pattern down, flagged it as worth a second eye, and left the meaning to whoever read above him. Kaviel recognized the restraint. It was the restraint of a man reporting one thing and aware he might be reporting another, and unwilling to put a frame around a fact he could not yet stand behind. She had written reports in that voice. She had stood at a transit counter not three weeks past and built a movement summary in that voice, the flat one that recorded only what could be defended later.

She read it a second time, slower, for what the words were built around.

The thing the report did not have was scope. The writer had his own jurisdiction and the confirmations that had crossed his own desk, and that was the whole of his window. He could see a cluster. He could not see whether the cluster was a local accident, a clerk in one office overusing a convenience, or the visible corner of a pattern that ran wider than any single desk could hold. He had no way to test it, because the test would have asked for records that lived in a bureau he had no business inside, and a careful administrator did not go pulling at records outside his remit on the strength of an unease. So he had done the one thing his position allowed. He had written it down and sent it up, and the sending up was the limit of what the report could do on its own.

The limit was the part that held Kaviel at the desk.

Eleanor would have read this differently. Kaviel knew that with the certainty of a person who had spent her childhood watching the reading done. Her mother worked from the living shape of things, from who fed whom and who owed whom and which channel had gone quiet in a month it should have spoken; she would have held this report against twenty years of names and traffic and known within an evening whose silence it confirmed and whose it broke. Kaviel did not have the network. She had never wanted it, had built herself sideways out of the house precisely so that she would not be the keeper of the cells and the codes and the debts. What she had instead was paper. She read documents the way her mother read people, and where her mother would have asked who, Kaviel asked which records, and both of them would arrive, and the difference between the routes was the whole of the difference between them.

She rose and went to the wall of reference shelving and took down the compound's standing index of active matters, and she carried it back to the lamp and ran her finger down the entries with the cold air moving at her wrist. She was not looking for the report. She was looking for the things the report touched without naming, the way a stone dropped in water touches the bank a stride off from where it fell.

Three matters lay in the index where her finger stopped.

She did not pause to ask herself whether she was entitled to pull them. She was a senior Lioness and the report had come to her for action and the action began with the records nearest it, and the entitlement was not a thing she spent thought on. She went to the door and put her head into the dark of the corridor, where the duty lamp burned at the far end above the night clerk's station, and she saw that the figure at the station was Meret, which was good, because Meret would bring her what she asked and would not ask her why.

"Meret."

Meret looked up from the ledger she kept the night log in. She was a square-built woman with a still face and a way of holding a pen that suggested she had never in her life made an unconsidered mark with one. "Senior."

"Three files from the central record." Kaviel gave her the index references in order, reading them off the open book against her forearm. "The Sennar Prefecture magistrate matter, the grievance heard in open court this season; the petitioner was an orchard-holder, the magistrate a man named Kebe. A transit manifest, Sabra Crossing, the southern feeder line, the movement closed three weeks back under my own filing. And the roster of a prior appointments commission, the session that ran before the one now sitting. Whatever the record room has on all three."

Meret wrote the three references in her own hand, read them back once, exactly, and closed the log. "It will take the better part of an hour. The night archivist will have to wake for the commission roster; it is held below the active line."

"The hour is fine. Wake him gently."

Meret rose. She did not ask what the three had to do with one another. She did not ask why a movement Kaviel had filed closed with her own hand was being pulled back up at the tenth hour of the night, or what a transit manifest had to say to a magistrate's commission, or whether Kaviel wanted anyone told. She gathered the lamp from the station and went, and her boots made the even unhurried sound down the administrative hallway that the building's boots made, nothing like the harder report they made on the training floors two levels off, and the sound thinned and turned the corner and was gone. Kaviel went back to her desk. The not-asking was not incuriosity. Meret was as sharp as anyone on the rolls and would have her own shape forming of why a senior wanted these three files at this hour, and she would keep it to herself and bring the files, because the discipline of the place was that you did not pull on a thread a superior was holding until the superior handed you the end of it. Kaviel had given that order to others. She knew exactly what it cost to obey it well, and Meret obeyed it better than most.

She used the hour. She read the field report a third time and made no notes, because notes this early would only fix a shape before the shape had earned its lines. She turned the lamp up a half-turn against the strain on her eyes and turned it back down when the brightness made the room feel smaller. She ate the cold bread without tasting it, because a body that had not eaten read worse than a body that had, and the work needed her reading at its best. The bean paste had gone to a paste of a different kind and she ate it anyway. Outside the cracked window the rain had stopped and the court below held its puddles up to whatever light reached down into it, and once a bird she could not name said something twice into the dark and fell silent.

Meret brought the files a little before the eleventh hour and set them on the desk squared to its edge, three folders of varying thickness, the commission roster the heaviest and bound in the dun board the deep archive used. "The archivist asks that the roster go back by the sixth hour; it is wanted for a reconciliation review that stands open under a senior authority." She did not say whose. "He flagged it. The review is recent."

"It can go back by the sixth." Kaviel heard the rest of it land and set it where she set things she could not yet use. A reconciliation review, recently stood up, holding the prior commission's roster as one of its inputs. She did not know whose authority kept it open and she did not let herself reach for the name, because the reach would be inference and the inference would run ahead of the page in front of her, and she had learned the hard way that an early name was a thing the mind then bent every later fact to fit. "Thank you, Meret."

Meret inclined her head a degree and went back to her log, and Kaviel was alone again with the lamp and the cold draw of the window and four documents.

She built it the way she built everything, by its parts, laid flat where she could see them at once.

She opened the Sennar matter first because it was the one with a face on it. Demba Tunde, orchard-holder, grievance against the ruling of the prefectural court, the easement lost to a neighbor, the costs assessed and then waived by the throne. The petition contested the judge and not the judgment, which was a weak ground in law and had been refused. She read down to the part she wanted, the commission entry that gave the magistrate his bench. Magister Iwan Kebe, confirmed to the Sennar prefectural bench by the Third Appointments Commission, seated under the Bureau of Establishments, entered under carry-forward reconciliation of a prior confirmation. She read the two dates the file gave, the carry-forward entry dated the eleventh of Threshing and the commission session it was entered under convened on the second of Reaping, and she set the months in their order in her head, Threshing then Reaping, five weeks between, and saw that the entry stood five weeks ahead of the session that was supposed to have authorized it. A confirmation entered before the thing that confirmed it.

She had seen this date in open court. She had stood against the western wall and heard Obasi read it and watched it go past the whole hall, and she had let it lie because taken on its own it was the ordinary disorder of a registry, a clerk transposing two months out of three hundred, and because her mother had not let it lie, and the gap between those two facts was the thing she carried out of the throne room and had not set down since. She knew now why it would not set down. One wrong date was a clerk's long afternoon. A clerk's long afternoon did not call to a field report out of a different jurisdiction flagging a cluster of carry-forward confirmations that should not cluster. The report gave the throne room date a second instance to stand beside, and two instances were not a clerk; two instances were a method.

She opened the prior commission roster next, the heavy dun folder, and worked it against the report and the Sennar entry both. This was the test. The carry-forward clause did one honest thing and could be made to do one dishonest one, and the difference lived in the roster of the prior session, because the whole legitimacy of a carry-forward turned on a prior commission having confirmed the appointment first. The clause permitted a confirmation made good under one commission to be carried into the record of the next without fresh review. It did not permit a confirmation that no prior commission had ever made. So she went through the prior roster name by name, the way Sefu Adebayo would have gone through it though she had never watched him do it, looking for Iwan Kebe among the confirmations the prior session claimed to have made.

He was not there.

She went through it again, slower, allowing for variant spellings and the chance of a clerk's hand turning a letter, and he was not there the second time either. The carry-forward entry on the Sennar magistrate cited the authority of a prior commission and a date of original confirmation, as the clause required, and the prior commission's own roster held no confirmation under that name on that date or any other. The thing the carry-forward was reconciling did not exist. The clause had been fed an appointment it could dress in the authority of a session that had never seen the man, and the dressing was the date discrepancy the throne room had passed over, because an honest carry-forward and a fabricated one looked identical on the face of the current record and parted only when you laid the current entry against a prior roster that lived in a different bureau and that no one ever thought to lay it against.

Someone had built it knowing that. The phrase arrived in her mother's cadence and Kaviel did not chase where she had heard it; it was the shape of the thing speaking, and the shape was a designed one.

She sat back and let the cold move across the back of her neck and turned to the third file, which was the one she had pulled on an instinct she could not yet justify to a board, and which she had half expected to come up empty. The transit manifest from Sabra Crossing. The southern feeder line, the movement she had run and filed closed with her own hand, the frightened woman with the document case who had come in off a board that did not post and gone out the seventh gate on a voucher Kaviel had filled in herself. She had filed the woman's origin in that report because the work required an origin and the feeder had given her one. Sennar. The woman had come up out of the Sennar Prefecture, on a line that terminated at the crossing and posted to no onward board, carrying a records-office document case, practiced at not being looked at.

Kaviel laid the manifest beside the Sennar magistrate file and the field report and the prior roster, and she made herself go slowly, because this was the place the night could go wrong, the place where a tired mind reached across a gap because the reaching felt like seeing. The manifest did not name the woman; the woman had traveled under a name that was not hers, and Kaviel had moved her under a third. There was no line on the manifest that touched a line in the magistrate file. What touched was the prefecture. A protected witness moved quietly out of Sennar, in the same season that a fabricated magistrate's confirmation out of Sennar surfaced in open court, in the same season a field report flagged a cluster of fabricated confirmations and a senior authority stood up a reconciliation review and buried it deep enough that a night archivist had to be woken to surrender a roster. And a man at the document desk in the second concourse with a courier's flat satchel and a face built to be forgotten, who had found the woman's bench with his eyes twice and never moved on her, who had the loose outward attention of a person whose work was to see a thing and carry what he saw somewhere else.

She had filed his face without a frame three weeks ago because the evidence would not hold a frame, and she had been right to, and she was careful now not to give him one he had not earned. A face in a crowd at a transit hub was a face in a crowd. But a witness does not get moved out of a prefecture for nothing, and the someone who took the trouble to move her had taken it because of something she could tell, and a witness with something to tell out of Sennar, in this season, was a fact that lay against the others with a weight Kaviel could feel and could not yet prove. The relay Silva had not recognized in the handler chain sat in the same drawer. Three names before the advance reached them, the third unpulled, the routing through a channel Silva did not know. Kaviel had told Silva to note it and leave it. She wished now she had a name for the relay and was glad she did not, because a name she wanted this badly was a name to distrust.

She had the shape. She turned it over twice more and it held its lines and added none it had not earned. A clause built to be boring, fed appointments no prior commission had made, dressed in dates that failed only against a roster no one cross-checked, surfacing in Sennar and in at least one jurisdiction more, with someone senior enough to stand up a review that hid where the first suspicion came from, and a witness lifted quietly out of the middle of it, and a courier with a forgettable face standing where she had been lifted. It was a method and it had a hand behind it and the hand had taken pains to be a process and not a person, so that anyone who pulled on it would find clauses and clerks and the ordinary friction of a machine too large to run without friction, and no one to charge.

She did not have the hand. She had the shape of the glove it left in the record, the size of it and the reach of it and the patience of it, and where the shape narrowed she could feel the absence at its center the way you feel a missing stair in the dark. She knew better than to fill it. Inference that ran ahead of evidence was how operations went wrong, and she had told a younger officer that in a stairwell once and meant it, and she was not going to spend the credit of a clean night's reading on a name she wanted because the shape ached for one.

The roster had to go back by the sixth hour. She squared the four files, the manifest atop the magistrate matter, the report atop those, the heavy roster beneath, and she turned the lamp down until the flame stood low and blue at its base and the corners of the room went to dark. She did not turn it out. She sat in the near-dark with the cold still drawing past her wrist and the wet-stone smell thinning as the court below dried, and she let the shape sit with her without working it further, the way she had learned to let a thing settle before she trusted what she had built.

Then she took a clean half-sheet from the drawer and uncapped the pen and held it over the paper a moment. She wrote one word. She read it once. She capped the pen, folded the sheet twice into a small square, and put it in the inner pocket of the coat that hung on the back of her chair, against the lining, where her own hand would find it and no one else's would. She drew the coat on. She gathered the three files for return and left her own where it lay, and she went out into the corridor toward the duty lamp where Meret kept the log, the folded paper riding cold against her ribs through the lining.

# Chapter 7 — The Court Moves

The state hall had been built to hold a thousand and tonight it held perhaps four hundred, which made it worse rather than better, because four hundred people in a room built for a thousand each carried their own pocket of air with them and moved it through the others, and the heat of them rose into a ceiling too high to give it back. They had come for the announcement of the autumn commissions, the routine confirmations of the bodies that would sit through the cold half of the year, and the throne had chosen to mark it with a dinner because a dinner cost less than a ceremony and bought as much, and because Amira had learned that the court behaved best when it was fed and watched at once. The long tables ran the length of the hall under the high windows, dark now and giving back the lamplight in the glass, and the smell of the food came up the room in layers, the roasted meat first and under it the sweet of the spiced wine and under that the wax of four hundred lamps burning down toward the second course.

Amira entered after the hall was seated, which was the form, and the hall rose, which was also the form, and she crossed to the raised table at the head of the room with the slow exact step that the overrobe required of her and that she had stopped resenting somewhere in her second year. She did not look at the room as she crossed it. She looked at the chair she was walking toward, because a ruler who scanned the faces while she walked told the room she was counting it, and the room would count itself back, and the dinner would become a census instead of a meal. She let them watch her arrive. She let the watching do its work. When she reached the head table she turned and stood a moment and let the hall hold its rising a breath longer than it expected, and then she sat, and the hall sat, and the long exhale of four hundred people lowering themselves onto benches ran the length of the room and was lost in the music from the gallery.

The musicians played from above and behind, set in the gallery over the doors so that the sound came down into the hall without a source the eye could fix, and the effect of it was that the talk had to climb over the music to be heard, and so every conversation in the room was pitched a half-step louder than its speakers wished, and leaned closer than they wished, and gave more away. Amira had ordered the musicians placed there in her first year and had never moved them. She ate little. She watched the room without appearing to watch it, in the glass of the dark windows where the hall hung reversed and dim, and in that reflection she could follow a man crossing the floor without turning her head to do it.

---

Councillor Hadi Marun came to the head table between the first course and the second, which was the right time, early enough to show eagerness and late enough to show he understood he was not the only man who wished to be seen. He was a broad, easy man somewhere past fifty, with the kind of face that gathered other faces toward it, and he made his bow to the throne with a fraction more depth than the floor required and held it a fraction longer, and Amira marked the fraction and did not hold it against him. Some men flattered because they wanted and some because they could not help it, and Marun, as far as the throne had ever been able to determine, was the second kind, which made him useful and slightly dangerous in ways he did not himself suspect.

"Majesty," he said, straightening. "The hall is in good spirits."

"The hall has been fed," Amira said. "Spirits follow the kitchen more reliably than the throne."

He laughed, the real laugh of a man who enjoyed being answered, and the laugh drew two heads at the nearest table and he was aware of them drawing and let himself be aware of them, comfortable in the small gravity of his own presence. "I had hoped to put a word in for the river prefectures before the commissions sit," he said. "The waterworks matter from the spring. There is feeling in the guilds that the cistern ruling left things unsettled."

"The cistern ruling settled the law," Amira said. "It left the feeling unsettled because the feeling was never about the law. Bring me the instrument they want drawn and I will read it. Do not bring me the feeling. I have feeling enough of my own about cisterns."

Marun inclined his head, taking the correction the way a man takes a coin he half expected, and he asked after the autumn commissions then, lightly, as a man asks after the weather of a country he means to travel through, and Amira told him what would be announced because it would be announced within the hour regardless. The Bureau of Establishments would seat its review bodies. The Third Appointments Commission would carry forward into the cold session. He nodded along to the names of the bodies with the attention of a man who found them as dull as he was meant to find them, and there was nothing in his face when the Third Appointments Commission was named, no flicker, no settling, and Amira marked the nothing the way Eleanor had taught her to mark such things, and filed it where she filed all the nothings, which was in a place she did not yet have a use for. He bowed again and withdrew, and the second course came up the room behind him on the shoulders of the servers, and the smell of it changed from meat to the sharp green of the dressed bitter leaves that the southern tables loved and the northern delegations left untouched on their plates.

---

In the colonnade along the eastern wall, where the lamplight thinned and the music came through as a pressure more than a melody, Lady Provost Imara Shandt stood with a cup she was not drinking and conducted three conversations at once without appearing to conduct any. She held the provostship of the upland schools, which gave her the placing of every magister who came out of them, and so she was never without men around her, and tonight there were four, and she moved among the four of them with the economy of a woman who had long since stopped finding any of it interesting and had not let the boredom reach her face.

A magister of the Bureau of Establishments stood at her left, a younger man in the administrative gray, and he was telling her about the autumn rosters in the low fast voice of a man who believes he is being indiscreet and is enjoying it. "The carry-forward list is longer this session than last," he was saying. "Half again as long. The reconciliation backlog out of the Outer Prefectures has grown faster than the commissions can clear it, and so it carries, and carries, and the books grow fat with it."

"The books have always grown fat," Lady Provost Shandt said. "That is what books are for." She turned her cup a quarter and did not drink from it. "Which prefectures."

"Sennar chiefly. The eastern uplands. The whole of the Outer establishment, but Sennar leads it." The young magister lowered his voice further, pleased with his own caution. "Between us, Provost, I have stopped reading the carry-forward entries with any care. There are too many. You confirm what the prior commission confirmed and you move to the next. To read each would take a year and confirm nothing the prior body did not already confirm."

"Then you confirm what the prior commission confirmed," she said, "and you trust that the prior commission read what the one before it confirmed, and so on back to a commission that read nothing because it was the first and had nothing behind it but a clean book." She set the cup down on the ledge of the colonnade window and left it there. "You have described a chain in which every link trusts the link before it and no link bears weight. I taught logic before I placed magisters. Go and enjoy the wine. You have given me something to be tired about."

The young magister did not understand that he had been dismissed, or understood and did not mind it, and he drifted toward the wine, and Lady Provost Shandt stood a moment longer at the window with her undrunk cup beside her and looked out at the dark grounds where nothing moved, and then she turned back into the room and was claimed at once by the next of her men, and the thing the young magister had said went into her the way a splinter goes in, unfelt at first under the press of the evening, set to ache later.

---

Amira left the head table during the third course, which a ruler was permitted to do once in an evening, to move down the room and let the lower tables feel the throne pass near them. She went with two of the Lionesses at a distance that read as escort to those who needed it to read that way and as nothing to those who did not, and she stopped at the table of the upland herders' delegation, who had waited a season for their petition and had been seated, by her order, no lower than the river guilds, and she asked the eldest of them about the autumn drive and the state of the high pasture, and she listened to the answer, the whole of it, while the man's hands described the grass with the unconscious eloquence of a man who had spent his life reading it. The southern oils and the foreign scents of the court tables gave way here to wool and woodsmoke carried in the herders' clothes, the smell of the high country that did not wash out in a single season at court, and Amira breathed it and was, for the length of the answer, somewhere other than the hall.

It was while she stood at the herders' table that she saw Eleanor.

The old woman had been brought a chair, as she was always brought a chair, set near the eastern colonnade where the light failed, and she sat in it with her hands folded over the head of the cane and her eyes half-lidded against the smoke of the lamps, and from the body of the hall she was furniture, an old administrator placed where the room could feel her without being asked to attend to her. Amira did not cross to her. In the four years of her reign she had crossed to Eleanor in a public room exactly never, and she would not begin tonight. She let her gaze pass over the colonnade the way it passed over the colonnade's lamps, and in the passing she saw what Eleanor was watching, because Eleanor's stillness had a direction to it the way still water has a current under the surface, and the direction was the long service door at the foot of the eastern wall through which the wine came up from the cellars. Amira looked at the door and saw a server pass through it with a tray and saw nothing else, and she returned her attention to the herder's hands and the high grass, and filed the door, and the angle of the old woman's attention, and the nothing she had seen in the doorway, into the place where the nothings went.

---

Behind the eastern wall the service passage ran the length of the hall, low-ceilinged and lit by working lamps set in iron brackets, and the music did not reach it; it stopped at the wall as though the wall had been built to stop it, and what crossed into the passage instead was the duller sound of the kitchens below and the feet of the servers on the stone and the occasional swing of the door letting in a wash of the hall's roar before it closed again and cut it off. The air here was different, cooler and wetter, the cellar air rising through the floor, and over it the smell of the kitchens, the fat and the smoke and the scorched edge of a sauce held too long over the fire.

Two men spoke in the passage near the wine store, a steward of the household and a man in the plain dress of a contracted carrier, the sort of man the household used to bring goods up from the lower city and who came and went through the service entrances by the hundred across a season. They spoke of the wine. The steward was counting it against a list and the carrier was confirming the count, and it was the dullest conversation in the building, and a third man stood a little apart from them and waited, and he was the kind of man the eye declined to hold. He was of middle height and middle years, with a face the demographic of the empire had stamped out a hundred million times, neither dark enough nor pale enough nor marked enough to fix in the memory, and he wore the same plain carrier's dress as the man at the list, and he carried a flat leather case of the sort the bureaus used to move documents, and he waited with the patience of a man who had waited in a great many passages. When the steward finished his count and turned back toward the hall, the forgettable man stepped to the carrier and the two of them exchanged a word too quiet for the passage to carry, and the carrier took something from the flat case and put it inside his coat, and the forgettable man took the case back and turned and walked the length of the passage away from the hall, toward the lower doors and the city, and no one marked his going because there was nothing in his going to mark. A man with a case had come and gone through a service passage. The passages were full of such men. That was what the passages were for.

---

Magister Faraj Oduya found Councillor Otho Bey at the wine table during the lull before the final course, and the two of them stood with their backs to the hall in the way men stand when they wish the hall to see only their backs. Oduya held a magistracy of the appellate courts and Bey held a seat on the council that voted the bureau budgets, and between them they could move a great deal of the empire's middle machinery, and they knew it, and they were careful with each other the way two men are careful when each holds a thing the other wants.

"The autumn budget," Bey said, low, into his cup. "The Bureau of Establishments has asked for more clerks. Reconciliation clerks. To clear the carry-forward backlog before it carries another year."

"And you will give them the clerks," Oduya said.

"I will give them the clerks. It is the dullest request in the budget and it will pass without a word said. More clerks to clear more backlog. Who votes against clerks." Bey drank. "I tell you because you sit appeals out of the Outer Prefectures, and the magistrates you hear appeals on are the magistrates these clerks will be confirming, and I would have you know that the books behind your bench are about to grow faster than any one man could read them, so that when a thing comes up out of Sennar that does not sit right, you will not waste a year of your court looking for a clerk's error in a stack no court could clear."

Oduya was quiet a moment, turning his cup. He was a careful man, and the care had kept him on his bench through three reigns of the appellate courts, and the care heard something in what Bey had said that Bey had not meant to put there, or had meant to put there and would never admit to. "You are telling me," he said slowly, "to expect things out of Sennar that do not sit right."

"I am telling you," Bey said, "to expect a great deal of backlog and a great many clerks, and to keep your bench clean while both grow, because a clean bench is the only thing a man in your seat or mine will have left to stand on if the books behind us are ever read by someone with a year to spend and a reason to spend it." He set down his cup. "I am telling you nothing. I am a councillor talking about a budget at a dinner. Go and find better company. You have made me say more than the wine should let a man say."

He moved off into the room, and Oduya stood at the wine table with his cup and looked at the hall for a while, and the music came down from the gallery over the dark windows, and somewhere the last course was rising from the kitchens on the shoulders of the servers, and the magister of appeals filed away what he had heard in the place a careful man keeps the things he cannot yet act on and cannot afford to forget.

---

Amira gave the announcement from the head table before the last course was cleared, standing, with the hall stilled under her and the music dropped to nothing at her lifted hand. She named the autumn commissions in the flat exact voice she used for instruments of state, the review bodies of the Bureau of Establishments, the seating of the appellate rosters, the carry-forward of the Third Appointments Commission into the cold session, and the hall received the names with the polite half-attention the names deserved, because they were the dullest possible news and the dinner had been good, and the only sign that anything in the recitation carried weight was that nobody anywhere in the room reacted to it at all, which was itself a kind of stillness, and which Amira felt from the dais the way a swimmer feels a cold layer of water rise from below. She finished the announcement. She wished the hall a good cold season. She sat, and the music came back up out of the gallery, and the last course went down the room, and the dinner began the long settling toward its end.

She did not look for Eleanor again. When she let her gaze cross the eastern colonnade a final time, near the end of the evening, the chair that had been brought for the old woman stood empty, carried out or soon to be, and Eleanor was gone, and the place where she had sat was only a place again, a chair in the failing light at the edge of a room. Amira marked the absence the way she had marked the presence, and put it where the rest of the evening had gone.

---

The hall emptied the way such halls empty, slowly and then all at once, the delegations leaving in the order of their precedence and the precedence dissolving near the doors into the ordinary press of people who had eaten and drunk and wished now to be elsewhere. The servers came in behind the leaving to strip the long tables, and the music stopped in the gallery without ceremony, the players packing their instruments into the cases that fit them, and the great room that had held four hundred pockets of moving air gave the air back to the high cold ceiling and grew still, and the smell of the food went stale and flat as the plates were carried out, the rich layers of it collapsing into the single tired smell of a room where a great deal had been eaten and the windows had not been opened.

The Clerk of Petitions, Obasi, came through near the end with the household stewards, walking the length of the hall to confirm that the throne's tables had been struck in the right order, and at the foot of the eastern wall, near the service door through which the wine had come up all evening, he found a small side table that had not been cleared, set apart from the long tables in the lee of the colonnade, laid for one. A single place. A cup, a plate, the small folded card the household used to mark a seating, the name on it in the steward's hand, and a chair drawn out and never pushed in. The food on the plate had not been touched. The wine in the cup had not been poured. The steward beside Obasi looked at his list and looked at the table and could not find, in the list, the reason the table had been set, and Obasi told him to leave it, and the steward left it, and they went on down the room.

The household put out the lamps as it went, the long rows of them quenched in order from the head of the hall toward the doors, and the great dark came down the room behind the stewards in a slow tide, and the last of the servers passed out through the eastern door with the last of the plates, and the hall stood empty under its high invisible ceiling. At the foot of the eastern wall, in the lee of the colonnade, beside the chair that had been drawn out and never pushed in, one lamp had been left burning over the small table laid for one, where the card stood in the steward's hand and the cup held no wine and the plate held a cold untouched meal set for someone who had not come.

# Chapter 8 — What Meret Knows

Meret had asked for the room at the second hour, before the compound came to itself, and Asha had granted it the way she granted most things Meret asked for, without question and with the understanding that Meret did not ask for much. The room sat at the end of the assessment corridor, the small one, built so that a voice raised to its ordinary pitch did not reach the wall and a voice dropped below that reached nothing at all. The walls were doubled and the door hung on oiled hinges that gave back no sound, and there was a table and two chairs and nothing on the walls, none of the columns of initials that hung in the larger rooms, because the room had been built for the kind of conversation that left no record until an officer decided to make one. Asha came down to it cold from her own quarters, the corridor still dark, the duty lamps burning low at their stations, and she let herself in and sat in the chair that faced the door, as she always did, and waited.

The compound at this hour had a smell it lost by the fourth, before the kitchens woke and the day's oil went onto the hinges. It was the smell of stone that had cooled all night and the faint mineral edge of the wash-water the night crew ran through the lower corridors, and over it, very thin, the cold ash of yesterday's lamps not yet relit. Asha had spent more mornings than she could count in this stillness before the building filled, and she knew that a person who asked for a room at the second hour had been awake in it a long time before the asking.

Meret came in at the second hour exactly. She closed the door with the flat of her hand, felt it seat, and sat in the chair across the table without being told to, square to it, her forearms coming to rest on the worn wood. She had a folder with her. She set it down and kept her hands on it, folded, the way she kept her hands on things, and did not open it.

"You have had this a while," Asha said. It was not a question. Meret did not ask for the small room to bring something a day old.

"Six weeks," Meret said.

Asha let that sit between them. Six weeks was a long time for Meret to carry a thing without sending it up the chain, and the length of it was the first piece of information in the room, before the folder, before a word of what was in it. Asha did not ask why Meret had waited. She would hear the why in the shape of what came next, or she would not, and either way the asking would have been an officer wanting to be told she was owed an explanation. Meret had her reasons and they had a discipline to them, and that was enough to begin from.

"Then tell me what it is," Asha said, "and tell me how it came to you, in that order."

Meret nodded, once, and began the way she did everything, from the bottom, with the parts that had dates on them.

"I keep the periphery summaries that Silva sends up," she said. "Not her field notes; the clean summaries, after she has cut them down for the file. I log them in and cross them against the transfer registers from the outer waystations, the arrivals and departures the stations post up the standard channel. That is the routine work, the reconciling. I have done it three years and I know its grain." She turned the folder a quarter on the table, not opening it, only squaring it nearer her own hands. "Six weeks ago I logged a summary out of a waystation on the Sennar feeder, and there was a face in it Silva had noted in passing, a man at a document counter, no flag on him, just a description she put down because she puts everything down. A courier's satchel. The loose look of a man waiting for nothing in a place where everyone waits for something. She moved on. It was not her matter; she was working a different line that day."

"Go on."

"I had seen the description before." Meret said it flat, without weight, the way she would have read a register entry aloud. "Not the man. The description. Three weeks earlier, in a summary out of a different waystation, a different jurisdiction, on a feeder that does not connect to the Sennar line and has no traffic that crosses it. Same satchel. Same loose attention. A man at a transfer point with no onward booking the station could post, present long enough to be noted and gone before the next register cycle closed." She paused, and Asha understood the pause was Meret deciding how much of her method to put into the room and choosing to put all of it. "I do not trust a description twice. A satchel is a satchel and a man waiting is half the people in a waystation. So I did not flag it. I laid the two summaries beside each other and looked for the thing that would tell me it was the same man or the thing that would tell me it was two, and there was not enough on the page to do either. So I left it open and kept logging, and watched the registers as they came up, and waited to see whether the description came a third time."

"And it came a third time," Asha said.

"It came four more times in six weeks." Meret opened the folder now. She lifted the cover and turned it back and laid her hand flat on the top sheet, one she had ruled herself, columns down it, dates and station codes and a short line of description against each, the same phrases recurring down the column because she had used the same words each time on purpose, so that the eye could catch the repetition. "Six instances, six weeks. Five waystations. Every one a transfer point, where a thing moves from one line to another and the register has to be re-cut by hand. Every one on a feeder that touches a jurisdiction where the field work has flagged something in the appointment records this season. I did not put that last part together myself. I keep the summaries, not the appointment files. But I keep the jurisdiction codes, and the codes on my six are the ones I have heard senior officers ask the analysis hall about in the last two months, and I am not deaf in a corridor."

Asha drew the folder a hand's width across the table and read the ruled sheet without touching it, top to bottom, the way she read everything the first time, for the shape and not the argument. Six rows. Six dates, the earliest seven weeks back, the most recent eleven days. Five station codes, one of them appearing twice. A column of descriptions in Meret's square hand, the same four or five phrases worked across all six rows so that they stacked when you ran your eye down them. It was the cleanest kind of work the Quarter did, the kind no examination on a page would teach a person to do, because the doing of it was patience and the patience could not be coached.

"You are reconciling registers," Asha said, reading still, "and a face you have no name for keeps standing up off the page in the places the appointment work is hot. And you have laid it out so that if you are wrong, the wrongness shows as plainly as the rightness."

"If I am wrong it is because six men in six waystations carried the same satchel and waited the same way and booked nothing the station could post," Meret said. "I do not think that is what happened. But I have built it so that it could be, because the day I build it so that it cannot be is the day I have stopped reconciling and started to decide."

Asha looked up at her then. The room was small enough that there was nowhere to look but at the other or at the table, the closeness the point of it, two people set near enough that nothing passed between them the room would let escape. Meret met the look and held it, plain and unhurried, and Asha saw in it none of the thing she watched for in the assessment chair, none of the wanting to be right, only a woman who had carried a finding carefully for six weeks and decided this morning she had carried it as far as one person should.

"This is why you waited," Asha said.

"I waited because three instances is a coincidence I have seen before and been wrong about," Meret said. "Four would have been a pattern, but two of the four I could not stand behind; the descriptions were thin and I would not pad them. I waited until I had six I could stand behind. I have six." She closed the folder over her own sheet, gently, her hand flat on the cover. "I did not wait because I was afraid to be wrong in front of you, Officer. I waited because if I bring you a thing before it will hold, and you act on it, and it does not hold, I have spent the Quarter's reach on my unease, and there is an operation in the field this season the unease could foul. I do not know what it is. I know there is one, because the analysis hall has not slept and a senior was woken for a roster eleven days ago and Kaviel has been in her office past the tenth hour more nights than not. I keep the night log. I see who burns the lamp. I did not need to be told there was something running to know that a wrong move from me could put a foot through it."

Asha sat with that. It was the longest she had heard Meret speak in a year, and Meret had spent it telling her, in operational language and no other, a thing a lesser officer would have dressed as conscience and a worse one as apology. There was no apology in it, only a woman accounting for a decision the way she would account for a register, and Asha respected the accounting more than she would have respected any speech about duty. She did not say so. Meret had not brought the finding to be told she had done well with it.

"You were right to wait," Asha said instead, the same thing said as a finding rather than a comfort. "And you are right to bring it now. Six holds. Three would not have. You know the difference and you held the line at it. That is the work."

Meret inclined her head a degree and said nothing, which was the correct amount to say.

Asha opened the folder again and read it the second way, slower, against what she carried that Meret did not. Meret kept the summaries and the registers and not the appointment files, and so had brought a pattern in transport and could not see the pattern in the records it lay against. Asha sat high enough in the analysis chain to have heard the jurisdiction codes spoken in the hall and to know which had gone hot and why, and she laid Meret's six station codes against that knowing now, row by row, and watched them fall into the hot jurisdictions one after another with the regularity of a thing that was not chance.

"Tell me about the man," Asha said. "Not the satchel. You have given me the satchel. What does this face do, in your reading, that puts him in five waystations in six weeks at the transfer points and nowhere the register can post."

"He carries," Meret said. "He does not travel. A traveler books an onward leg and the station posts it; this man has no onward leg the station can show, which means he is handing off. A transfer point is where a thing leaves one line and joins another and the register is re-cut by hand, and a thing handed across at the moment the register is open can be moved without a clean line from where it started to where it went. He is at the seam every time, and the seam is where the record can be made to lose the thread." She said it without heat, a register entry still. "I think he moves paper, because a courier's satchel carries paper and the jurisdictions are the appointment jurisdictions, and a record-trouble that runs across jurisdictions needs a hand to carry the false thing from where it is made to where it is filed, by a route that does not post. I cannot prove the satchel holds appointment papers; I have never seen inside it. I am telling you the shape my reading takes and where it stops being something I can stand behind."

It was the same discipline Kaviel kept, the line drawn hard between what the page held and what the mind reached past it for, the thing the Quarter selected for above all the rest, the thing she had watched a competent soldier fail to have in the assessment chair weeks back. She set the thought aside.

"You have a face and a method and six instances and no name," Asha said.

"I have no name," Meret agreed. "Silva did not have one. The registers do not carry one for him, because a man who hands off at the seam does not give the counter a name it logs. I have a description and a route and a rhythm. I do not have a man I could put a hand on."

Asha read the rightmost column again, the one Meret had not spoken to, a narrow column at the edge where she had set down, against four of the six rows, a cluster of marks that were not description and not date. Asha had taken them for register codes on the first reading. They were not. They were initials, partial ones, a single letter standing alone in three of the rows and a letter and a half-formed second in the fourth, set down small and tight against the rule.

"What are these," Asha said, and put her finger to the column without touching the page.

"That is the thing I am least sure of," Meret said, "and the reason I almost brought you only the pattern." She did not reach for it; she let Asha hold it. "Three of the waystations keep a hand-signed transfer chit when a thing is moved across by a person rather than down a posted line. It is an old practice, kept in the outer stations and let go in the central ones, a man putting his mark to say the thing passed through his hand. It carries no legal weight. No one reconciles it; it sits in a box at the station until the box is full and then it is burned. Silva does not collect it because it tells you nothing on its own. But three of my six stations keep it, and on the days my face was in those three, a chit was signed across the seam, and the mark, where I could read it, began with the same letter."

Asha was very still.

"Which letter," she said.

"B," Meret said.

The room held it. Asha did not move and did not let her face move, the not-moving a discipline she had kept for forty years and kept now, because a single letter set down in a small room at the second hour was either the first true edge of a thing the Quarter had been reading the shape of for two months or a coincidence of a courtesy practice in three outer stations, and her face would not decide which before her reading had.

"Where you could read it," she said. "Not all three."

"Two I read plainly. The third the chit was smudged and I would not swear past the letter, which is why the half-mark is in the fourth row and not a full one. The fourth station did not keep chits; that row has no mark and I left it empty rather than carry the letter across to it, because the letter belongs to the chits and not to the man, until I have a reason to join them." Meret's hands had come back to folded on the closed folder. "It is the first letter of a name signed on a courtesy chit by a man who handed a thing across a seam, on the days my face was in the station, at three stations of the five. It is not the man's name. It could be the man. It could be a station hand who signed for a transfer that had nothing to do with him. The chit says nothing past the one mark. I laid it at the edge of the sheet on purpose, so that it sits beside the pattern and not inside it. If you take the letter into the pattern you are deciding the courier's name begins with it, and I have not earned that, and I will not put my hand to it until it is earned."

Asha read the column one more time, the lone B set tight against the rule in three rows, the empty fourth where a lesser hand would have filled the letter in to make the page look finished. She thought of a roster pulled from below the active line eleven days back and a reconciliation review stood up under an authority no clerk would name, and of the appointment work that ran across exactly the jurisdictions whose codes were stacked in front of her in Meret's square hand. She held enough of their shape to know that Meret had brought her, without being able to see the far side of it herself, the first thread in two months that ran from the records into a hand, a hand that moved at the seams where the record lost its thread, in the jurisdictions where the false appointments surfaced, leaving a single letter on a courtesy chit no one was meant to read and that Meret had read because Meret read everything.

It was the most incomplete thing she had been handed in weeks, a letter and a satchel and a rhythm, and the incompleteness was the part she would have to carry now, because she sat where the deciding was. Act on a letter as though it were a name and the operation already running in the field, the one whose lamp Meret saw burning past the tenth hour, could turn on the wrong man, or on the right man too soon, before the thing behind him had shown its reach. Wait for the name and the man kept handing off at the seams while they waited, and the longer he handed off the more the record lost. Asha had sat with that choice a hundred times and it did not get lighter with the hundredth.

This was not a day to move on the man. A courier handing off at the seams was a node and not a head, the kind of figure a process used and replaced, and to reach for him now was to spend the surprise on the part of the machine that mattered least and warn the part that mattered most. But the letter was a true edge, and a true edge logged did not have to be found again, and the finding of it had cost Meret six weeks she had spent well.

"You will keep doing exactly what you have been doing," Asha said. "Log the summaries, reconcile the registers, change nothing in how you do it, because the value of what you brought me is that it came up out of the routine and not out of a man looking for it. If you start looking for him, the looking will change the shape of what you see, and I would lose the one reading in this building that is not bent toward an answer. You watch. You log. The next time the face comes up, you note the station and the date and whether a chit was kept, and you bring it to me here, to no one else, and you put it in no standing file. A thread this thin breaks if it is handled by more hands than need to hold it, and right now the hands that need to hold it are yours and mine."

"Understood," Meret said.

"The sheet stays with me."

"I made it to give to you."

Asha drew it the last of the way across the table and squared it to the folder's edge. "Kaviel is working a shape of this from the records side. She does not have what you have; you do not have what she has; neither of you is to be told what the other holds until I decide the two halves are worth joining, and that decision is mine and it is not made this morning." She said the name once and let it lie, because Meret kept the night log and knew whose lamp burned and would have her own shape forming of why Asha named her, and she would keep that shape to herself the way she kept everything. "You have not spoken Kaviel's name in connection with any of this. You will not."

"I have not," Meret said. "I will not."

Asha rose and went to the low cabinet against the doubled wall, unmarked, locked with a key she carried and not one the building held. She took out a ledger that was hers and not the Quarter's, narrow, bound in plain dark board, the log a senior officer kept of threads too thin to file and too true to lose, and carried it back and uncapped her pen. Meret had risen when Asha rose, and stood at the far side of the small table with her closed folder under her hand, waiting to be released, watching without watching the way she did.

The room had warmed a half-degree with the two of them in it, the ash smell of the dead lamps thinning, and somewhere far down through the doubled walls the first sound of the kitchens reached them as a vibration more than a noise, the day beginning to assemble itself two floors below while the small room held its silence a moment longer. Asha turned to a clean page near the back of the ledger, past the older threads in her own cramped hand, and set the date at the head of it, and the station codes from Meret's sheet beneath, the rhythm, the absence of an onward booking, the courtesy chit and what it did and did not prove. She wrote it short, because a long entry was an officer arguing with herself on the page and she was not arguing; she had decided. At the foot of the entry, in the narrow space she left for the thing each thread came down to, she wrote a single letter, and looked at it a moment in her own hand, the lone B sitting at the bottom of the page the way it had sat at the edge of Meret's sheet, an edge and not a name, true and not yet enough.

She capped the pen. She closed the ledger over the letter and set her hand flat on the cover for the length of a breath, and then she stood.

"We work from what we have," she said.

# Interlude 2 — Personal Correspondence, Eleanor De Grosvenor to Kaviel De Grosvenor

The following was recovered from a locked compartment in Eleanor De Grosvenor's private effects, sealed but bearing no dispatch mark, written in her own hand. It was never sent, and there is no record that Kaviel ever read it.

---

Kaviel,

I have started this letter four times across as many years and burned three of the attempts. I am keeping this one because I am old enough now to know I will not write a fifth, and because the things I have left unsaid have begun to outweigh the things I have managed to say to you, which were never many.

I want to tell you first what I saw when they brought you to me. You will not remember it, and I have never described it, because to describe it would have meant telling you what I decided in the same room, within the same hour. I held you for the length of time it takes to read a short report. Then I gave you to the house, to be raised by people I trusted to do it better than I would, and I went back to work that did not wait. I have told myself for thirty years that this was the correct allocation. It was correct. It was also a thing I did, with my hands, and the correctness of it has never once made it lighter to carry.

You should understand that I did not give you to the work because I did not want you. I gave you to the work because I knew what I was, and I knew what I would make of a child kept close. I had already watched the house take in promising people and grind the soft parts off them. I did not want to be the hand that did that to you across a breakfast table for fifteen years. So I arranged for it to be done by strangers who were kinder than I am, and by a discipline that does not pretend to love you while it shapes you. I believed you would be more whole that way. I still believe it. I am only no longer certain the belief was about you.

I have read your field assessments. All of them, going back to your probationary year, including the ones Asha marked low and the ones you appealed. You were right to appeal two of them and wrong to appeal the third, and the fact that you knew which was which before the review board did is the thing that told me what you would become. You do not work the way I work. I have spent six decades learning who owes whom, who can be moved by debt and who by vanity, where a man keeps the favor he is ashamed of. You learned instead to read a page until it confesses. I used to think the document was the smaller instrument. I was wrong about that, and the wrongness has cost the house time I cannot recover. The networks I built are aging out with me. The records will outlast every name I ever cultivated. You saw that before I admitted it.

I want to say something now that I have never said inside the walls, where it would be heard as weakness and filed as such. I am afraid. The apparatus I assembled was built around the assumption that I would always be in the room. I am not always in the room any longer. There are afternoons I lose, hours I cannot account for, decisions I have to make twice because I do not trust the first version. When the test comes, and it is coming, I will not be at full strength to meet it. You will. And I put you where you stand, knowing this hour would arrive and that you would have to hold the thing I built without me holding it with you. That was not an accident of your talent. It was a choice I made when you were small, and I dressed it in words like obligation so that I would not have to call it what it was.

The compact between this house and the throne is older than Amira and older than me, and you have inherited a place in it that you did not ask for and could not refuse, because I arranged your life so that refusing was never available to you. I am not going to pretend that was fair. It was not fair. It was necessary, by my reckoning, and I have learned that necessity and unfairness sit at the same table more often than anyone in my position will admit aloud. You have every right to resent the position. I would think less of you if some part of you did not.

Here is what I cannot say to your face, and so I am saying it to a page I may never give you. You did not become what you are because of the name. The name opened doors. It did not write the assessments. It did not catch the things you have caught. Whatever I arranged, the work itself is yours, and no part of it was inherited. I have watched a great many people fail to be equal to what was demanded of them. You have not been one of them. That you are not warm toward me, that you keep the careful distance the work requires between us, I understand and do not hold against you. I built that distance. I would only ask, if you ever read this, that you not mistake my distance for indifference. It was the opposite, kept under discipline so long that it learned to look like nothing at all.

I am going to seal this and put it where the rest of my private papers go, and I expect it will be read, if it is read, by someone going through my effects with a ledger after I am gone. If that someone is you, then know that I went over every line and meant each one, and that I corrected the punctuation twice, because even here I could not let it go untidy.

Be careful with the documents. They are telling you the truth before the people do. Trust them, and trust yourself reading them, ahead of anything I taught you about loyalty.

E. De Grosvenor

# Chapter 9 — The Investigation Opens

The room had been a store for forty years, and it gave that up reluctantly. They had pulled the broken assessment furniture out of it the day before, the chairs with the splintered legs and the cabinets whose locks had been cut and never replaced, and what remained when the carrying was done was a long cold space under the north wall of the compound with a smell that no airing had yet moved, the dry smell of board and binding left too long without handling, of old paper that had sat in stacks against stone and taken on the cold of it. Kaviel had asked for this room and not a better one. A better one would have had a use the building could name, and a use the building could name was a use someone could ask after.

She stood in the doorway a moment before she went in, the way she did with any space she meant to work from, reading it for what it would and would not let her do. There was one window, high and narrow, set into the thickness of the north wall where the masonry was deepest, and at this hour it gave a light that came in flat and gray and lay across the floor without reaching the inner half of the room, so that the far end stood in a dimness the single lamp on the long table did not take back. She had cracked the window already, out of the habit she kept in her own office, and the moving air had thinned the paper smell near the glass and left it banked at the back of the room where the stacks were.

Zola Adimu was at the long table when Kaviel came in, standing, not sitting, with her hands at her sides and a stillness to her that was the stillness of someone who had arrived early and decided that sitting before she was told to sit would be the wrong thing. She was young enough that the youth was the first fact about her and would be for another two years, until the work wore it down into something a stranger would not lead with. Kaviel had read her file twice. Top of her analytical cohort, slow where the others were quick and right where the others were fast, the kind of researcher Asha graded high and worked hard because the patience was already in her and only the method had to be put on top of it.

"You found it," Kaviel said.

"Asha sent me down at the first hour. The stacks were already here." Zola's eyes went to them and came back. "I sorted what I could read the labels on. The rest I left as it came."

Kaviel walked the length of the table. Someone, Zola or whoever had done the carrying, had laid the document stacks along it in an order, and the order was not bad. It ran by jurisdiction, the Sennar Prefecture nearest the lamp where the most of it was, and then the others falling away down the table into the dim end, each prefecture's records squared and weighted with whatever had come to hand, a cut of stone in one place, a dead lamp base in another. There was a map on the wall above the table, the only thing on any wall, a working map of the Outer Prefectures with the numbered jurisdictions marked in the dry imperial style and nothing on it yet in any hand but the printer's.

"This is the work," Kaviel said. She did not say it warmly. She said it the way Asha said a thing was the work, as a statement of where they now stood and what it would ask. "I am going to tell you what we are doing and the bounds of it, once, and then we begin, and you will hold the bounds as hard as you hold the method, because the bounds are the part that keeps the rest of it standing."

Zola nodded. She had a stylus already and a clean sheet, and she did not write yet, because she had been taught well enough to know that the first telling of a thing was for the ear and the second for the page.

"We have a mandate," Kaviel said. "It is real. The Lioness charter gives us standing to examine any imperial record where the integrity of the record bears on the protection of the throne, and the integrity of the appointments record bears on it, because a false magistrate rules in the empire's name and binds the empire's subjects, and a false administrator signs in the empire's hand. No one drafted that clause for this. But it holds for this. I have read it against three older uses and it holds." She set two fingers on the Sennar stack. "What it gives us is the record. We may pull the appointment files, the commission rosters, the prefectural registries, the transit manifests where they touch a record we are examining, and we may set them beside one another and read what the reading shows. That is a great deal. It is also the whole of it."

"And what it does not give us," Zola said. Not a question. She had heard the shape of the sentence coming.

"It does not give us a person. We do not question a serving official under this mandate. We do not enter a working office, we do not pull a man's correspondence, we do not put a watch on anyone who has not first walked up out of the record on his own. The mandate is over paper. The moment we reach past the paper to a hand, we are doing a thing the charter does not cover, and a thing the charter does not cover is a thing that has to be authorized from above the Quarter, and authorization from above the Quarter is a door, and a door can be watched from the other side." She let that sit. "Do you follow the distinction. Tell it back."

"We read the records. We do not touch the people." Zola's voice was even. "Until a person is already in the records, where we can read them without being told we are allowed to look."

"Until then." Kaviel moved down the table to the dim end and stood where the gray light gave out. "Now the part that is harder than the method, and I want it from you before we have logged a single entry, because once we are in it the work will tempt you to forget it. Who knows that this room is open."

Zola was quiet a moment. "I do not know. I was sent down. I was not told why, past sorting the stacks."

"Asha knows, because the room sits under her and a room does not open in the Quarter without her knowing what it is. Eleanor De Grosvenor knows, because I told her, and I will say more about that. You know, now. There is one more I will bring in when the financial records need her, and you will meet her and you will not ask what else she works." Kaviel turned. "That is the list. It does not grow without my word. You do not tell Meret. You do not tell the analysts you came up with. You do not tell your family that you have been moved to sensitive work, because a young researcher moved to sensitive work is a thing a careful person can read at a dinner table from the way you stop talking about your day. You will keep talking about your day. You will have a dull day to talk about, and I will see that you do, so that the dullness is true and you are not made to lie, because a person made to lie gets caught at it and a person with a dull true day does not."

"Understood," Zola said.

"The Empress does not know." Kaviel said it plainly and watched the young face take it, the small work behind the eyes as Zola set it where it belonged and did not let it show on the front of her. "That is not an oversight and you will not treat it as one. There is a reason the Empress is not told a thing at the hour we would tell her, and the reason is that what she knows she must be able to act on, and what she acts on becomes the empire acting, and the empire acting at this hour, before we have a shape that holds, would warn the thing we are reading the shape of. I have told the head of this house, and the head of this house has not stopped me, and that is the authorization we are working under. It is thin. I want you to hear that it is thin, from me, today, so that you are never surprised by how thin it is."

She did not say Eleanor's name a second time, and she did not say the rest of it, that thin was the truest word for it and that she lay awake on the thinness of it more nights than she would have told Asha. Eleanor had heard it in the west-facing office over a cup of tea gone cold, sitting with her hands folded and the maps behind her on the wall, and had listened the whole way to the end without a word, which from her was its own kind of answer. Then she had said only that Kaviel was to work it from the records and to come to her and to no one else when she had a name, and that she would not be told the daily shape of it, because the head of a house that is asked under oath what she knew should be able to answer that she knew the daughter was working and not the hour-by-hour of what the daughter found. It was permission given as a withholding, the way Eleanor gave most things. Kaviel had taken it for what it was and gone down to open the room.

"Then we begin from the record we can stand on," she said, and came back up the table into the light. "Interlude, you would call it, if you were the captain writing this down. The Third Appointments Commission filed a record. I have a copy." She drew it from the folder she had carried in, the full session record, header and seal, the one that had been filed plain and read by no one twice. She laid it before Zola at the lamp. "Read it. Not for what is wrong with it. There is nothing wrong with it on its face, and if you go looking for what is wrong you will find a thing that is not there and build on it. Read it for what it permits."

Zola read. She read slowly, which was correct, and she read it twice, which was better, and when she set it down her finger was on Item Four.

"The carry-forward reconciliation," she said.

"Tell me what it is. In your own words, not the record's."

"A way to enter an appointment into the standing record without reviewing it again, on the ground that a prior commission already reviewed it." Zola's voice had gone careful, the voice of someone walking a floor she had only just been told might give. "You produce the prior confirmation and an attestation from the originating office, and the entry is made citing the prior authority and the original date, and the commission does not look at it fresh because the whole point of the provision is to spare them looking at it fresh."

"And what does that give a person who wishes to put a name into the record that no commission ever confirmed."

Zola was silent the length of a breath. "It gives them a door that opens on the word that an older door was already opened. If the prior confirmation is true, the entry is clean. If the prior confirmation is false, the entry still reads clean, because no one at this commission is required to go back and check that the older door was ever there."

"Yes." Kaviel set her hand flat on the record. "That is the whole of the mechanism, and you found it in two readings, and I want you to remember that you found it that fast, because everything from here will be slower and you will need to remember that the center of it was plain once you knew where to stand. Now I am going to teach you the only method that gets us past it, and you are going to be the one who runs it, because I will be pulled into other rooms and you will be the patience this work has."

She pulled a clean sheet to herself and ruled it as she spoke, three columns, the rule going down in a hand that had ruled a thousand such sheets.

"Every carry-forward entry cites a prior authority. A prior commission, a session, a date, a folio. That is the claim the entry rests on. The entry says: I am clean because that older thing confirmed me. So we do not argue with the entry. We go and find the older thing." She wrote at the head of the first column, the entry. "We take each carry-forward entry, and we read off the prior authority it names." The second column, the claimed authority. "And then we go to the record of that prior authority, the older commission's own filed roster, and we look for the appointment the entry says it confirmed." The third column, and at its head she wrote nothing, and left it blank, and tapped it once with the stylus. "And one of three things is true. The prior roster confirms it, and the entry is clean, and we move on and we do not waste another hour on it. Or the prior roster exists and the appointment is not on it, and the entry rests on an authority that did not confirm it. Or the prior commission the entry names never sat at all, or sat and confirmed nothing in that jurisdiction, and the entry rests on an authority that does not exist."

Zola was writing now, fast and clean, getting the three columns down exactly.

"You see why it is slow," Kaviel said. "There is no single page that shows the fraud. The fraud is in the gap between two pages that are each clean on their own, and to find the gap you have to hold both pages at once, the entry and the older roster it names, and most of the older rosters are not where the entry says they are, because they are forty years of imperial backlog filed across four prefectures by clerks who are dead. You will spend a day finding a roster to spend a minute reading it. That is the work. The reconciliation trace, I call it, for want of a worse name. You trace the reconciliation back to the thing it reconciles to, and you find out whether the thing is there."

"How many of the entries do we trace," Zola said.

"All of them, in the end. We start with the ones that smell, the ones in the jurisdictions that have already gone hot, and we let those teach us what a bad entry looks like, and then we run the rest against what we learned." Kaviel looked at the map a moment. "But you do not skip a clean-looking one because it looks clean. The man who built this is good, and a man who is good salts the clean ones in among the bad to make the bad look like noise. You trace them all. When you find a bad one, you do not exclaim and you do not bring it to me running. You log it, you note the entry and the claimed authority and what you found in the gap, and you set it aside and trace the next, and when you have a dozen you bring me twelve and not one, because one is a coincidence I have been wrong about and twelve is a shape, and I would rather wait for the shape."

She heard the echo of it as she said it, Asha's grain in her own mouth, six holds and three does not, the line drawn at the place where reading stopped and deciding began. She let it stand. It was the right thing said and it did not matter whose mouth had worn it first.

They worked. The morning went out of the high window and the flat gray light climbed the wall and shortened on the floor as the sun got up over the compound, and the lamp that had mattered at the first hour stopped mattering and then mattered again as the inner end of the room held its dimness into the afternoon the way deep masonry holds the cold. Kaviel took the Sennar stack, because Sennar was where the thing had first stood up off a page months back, the magistrate whose confirmation date fell before the session that was supposed to have confirmed him, the line she herself had passed over once and then not passed over. She traced the carry-forward entries in Sennar back to the prior commissions they named, and she found the first gap before the light had moved a hand's width, an entry that rested on a session of a prior appointments commission, cited by date and folio, and when she went to the older roster the session had sat and had confirmed eleven appointments and not one of them was the name the carry-forward entry claimed it had carried forward.

She did not exclaim. She logged it. Entry, claimed authority, the gap. She set it aside and traced the next.

By the middle of the afternoon she had four out of Sennar, and Zola, working slower and on the jurisdiction next to it, had two, and they had not spoken in an hour past the small sounds of the work, the turn of a page, the scrape of the lamp drawn nearer, the dry whisper of a stack squared again after a sheet was lifted from it and returned. The paper smell had warmed with the day and the bodies in the room and lost its coldness, and under it now was the faint sharpness of the lamp oil and, when the door at the corridor's end opened and closed somewhere, the brief reach of the compound's ordinary noise, a voice, a cart, the building running as it always ran while in this room two people unmade a fraud one filed page at a time.

Asha came in once, near the fourth hour of the afternoon, without knocking because the room was hers to enter, and stood at the end of the table and read over Kaviel's shoulder the way she read everything the first time, for the shape and not the argument. She said nothing about the work. She looked at Zola a moment, and at the ruled sheets growing under Zola's hand, the three columns filling, and what she saw in them she kept to herself.

"How does she trace," Asha said to Kaviel, as though Zola were not in the room, which was a thing Asha did to a junior on purpose, to see whether the junior had the discipline to keep working through being spoken over.

Zola kept working.

"She traces the way I taught her this morning and better than I traced at her year," Kaviel said. "She is slow. The slow is the asset. Leave her on it."

Asha looked at the back of Zola's bent head a moment longer, and then she put a single mark in her own small ledger, the one bound in dark board, and went out, and that was the whole of the assessment, and Zola, who had felt every second of it and had not lifted her stylus, understood when the door shut that she had passed something and did not know its name and was correct not to ask.

It was past the eighth hour when Kaviel stopped. The gray window had gone dark and the lamp was the whole of the light now, the room shrunk down to the warm circle of it and the cold dim past its edge, and her hand had cramped twice and she had worked through both. She had nine out of Sennar. Zola had five out of the two jurisdictions beside it. Fourteen entries that rested on an authority that was not there, in three jurisdictions, in a single day's reading of the easiest stacks, the ones where the work had already been pointed.

She rose and stood at the wall under the map. She had brought a stylus to it without deciding to, and now she marked, against each jurisdiction where a bad entry had stood up off the page today, a small flat dot in her own hand, Sennar first, and then the two beside it, and she stood back from the three dots and looked at the printer's grid of numbered jurisdictions running away from them across the Outer Prefectures, the dozens of them, the ones she had not yet touched and the ones beyond the Outer Prefectures where the carry-forward provision had cousins under other commissions she had not yet thought to pull.

She had come into the room that morning believing it was Sennar and its neighbors, a regional thing, ugly and contained, the kind of rot that ran in one watershed because one office had gone bad. Three dots on the map at the end of a single day, and every one of them found in the first stacks, the stacks already pointed at, told her that the believing had been a comfort and not a finding. If the easiest reading in three jurisdictions gave fourteen, then the map was wrong to be so empty, and the emptiness was the measure of how few hands had yet read for the thing, no more than that. This was not one office gone bad. An office gone bad did not reach across three jurisdictions on three feeders that did not connect, and a man did not salt clean entries among bad ones across a watershed he did not control. Someone had built this wide, and built it slow, over years, with the provision read the same way in jurisdiction after jurisdiction by hands that had to have been pointed at the provision by the same understanding, and the map in front of her was not the picture of the fraud. It was the picture of how much of the fraud she had not yet found.

Behind her Zola was squaring the last of the day's stacks and weighting them, the dry whisper of it the only sound, the lamp throwing both their shadows long up the wall and across the map so that the three small dots sat in the moving dark of them. Kaviel stood with the stylus still in her hand and looked at the jurisdictions she had not marked, the white grid of them, and counted in her head what the finding meant was still waiting out there past it, and the count was larger than the room, larger than the mandate, larger than the thin permission she was working under and the head of the house who had given it to her and not asked to be told the daily shape.

"We start from the edges," she says, more to the map than to Zola.

# Chapter 10 — The Administrator's Breakfast

The Bureau of Establishments' Outer Prefectures office had been awake since before the sun reached the city of Adwa, because the work it did had never depended on light. Sael Mokoena arrived at it the way he arrived every morning, on foot from the third terrace, through the lower gate the clerks used, into the long ground-floor corridor where the night registry was already handing off to the day. He liked the hour. He liked the building before it filled, when the great wheel of it turned slowly enough that a man could hear its parts. Somewhere above him a transcription engine clicked through a backlog, patient as rain on a roof. A floor-keeper was drawing a wide mop across the stone of the entrance hall, and the wet trail of it shone and faded behind her as the dry air took it back. The smell that met him at the door was the smell that had met him for nineteen years, lamp oil and old paper and the faint mineral cool of stone that the sun did not reach until midday.

He went first to the registry window, where Adisa had the night's intake stacked and banded.

"Morning, Deputy Registrar," she said, not looking up from the band she was tying. She was new, six weeks in the office, and she still used the full title where the others had let it wear down to nothing.

"Anything that won't keep until the session?"

"Two contests out of Lomé. A query from the Sennar registry about a folio reference, I put it on your desk. And the carry-forward schedule came up from reconciliation overnight, they want your initial before it goes to the assessors."

"Leave the schedule with me. The Lomé contests can go to the morning list." He took the banded query without breaking his stride toward the stair, and she went back to her tying, and that was the whole of it, a deputy registrar collecting his correspondence from a registry clerk who thought no more about him after he had passed than she thought about the door.

His office was on the second floor, at the end where the corridor narrowed and the windows looked down into the inner courtyard the building had been built around. He had earned the room by length of service and held it the same way. It was not large. A desk worn pale at the front edge where two generations of registrars had rested their forearms, a wall of pigeonholes he had organized himself and trusted no one to touch, a side table for the kettle and the small enamel pot he kept there. He set the night's correspondence down, lit the lamp though the window gave him enough to see by, because he liked the lamp, and put the water on.

Breakfast came up from the kitchen at the half-hour, as it had every working day since the kitchen had learned his habits. A boiled egg, two flat rounds of the dark prefectural bread, a wedge of the hard yellow cheese the south sent up by the crate, and the bitter red tea he had learned to drink in his first posting and had never afterward been able to do without. He ate the way he did most things, without haste and without pleasure shown, the egg first, the shell going into the small dish for the purpose, the salt from the twist of paper he kept in the drawer. The bread he tore rather than cut. He read while he ate, because the morning was the only time the office gave him to read without interruption, and what he read was the carry-forward schedule reconciliation had sent up.

It was a good schedule. He could tell that at a glance, the way a man who has built a thing many times can tell sound work from poor. Forty-one entries, each one citing a prior commission's authority and attested by an originating office, the dates set down in the order the provision required, the date of original confirmation cited and the date of present entry left to the clerk. Thirty-nine of the forty-one were exactly what they appeared to be, lawful appointments long delayed in the record, reconciled now because the backlog had at last reached them. He had reviewed thousands like them. The work of the office was real work, and most of it was honest, and the honesty of the most was the ground the rest stood on.

Two of the forty-one were his.

He did not look at them differently from the others. That was the discipline, and the discipline was the whole of the craft. He had learned long ago that a man who lingered over his own entries taught the page to betray him, that the eye of a later reader was drawn to wherever the writer's eye had lingered, and so he read his two the way he read the thirty-nine, no slower, no faster, his pen moving down the margin in the small even ticks that meant a deputy registrar had seen each line and found it in order. The two appointments were a magistrate's seat in a jurisdiction three prefectures east and a deputy assessor's post in a registry office no contest had ever troubled. Both cited a prior commission that had sat, that had kept minutes, that had confirmed appointments under exactly the authority now cited. The prior commission was real. Its authority was real. Only the two appointments had never been before it. He had given them the commission's authority the way a man borrows a true name to carry a false errand, and the errand passed because the name was good.

He initialed the schedule, all forty-one, and set it in the tray for the assessors, and finished his egg.

There was no part of this that felt to him like the thing the word for it would have made it. He had turned the word over once, years ago, alone, the way a man turns over a stone to see what it is, and had put it down again because it did not fit what he did at the desk in the early light. What he did at the desk was careful. It was patient. It harmed no one he could see, took nothing from anyone he could name, moved a few seats into hands he judged steadier than the empire's own indifferent procedures would have found for them, and did it inside the very provision the founding instrument of the commission had written for the purpose of moving things into the record without fresh review. The provision was the door. He had not built the door. He had only learned, better than the men who built it, where it led.

He poured the second cup of tea and let it cool while he opened the rest of the night's correspondence.

The Sennar query was nothing, a folio reference entered transposed, a clerk asking which of two numbers was right; he wrote the right one in the margin and set it for return. A circular from the Bureau's New Terra office on the standardization of attestation forms, which he read twice because anything that changed the forms could change what an attestation had to show, and put aside satisfied that it touched nothing of his. A note from the assessor Okonjo about the order of the morning session.

The last item in the band had no office mark.

It had come in as private correspondence, addressed to him by hand in a script he knew, routed through the courier service the office used for matter that did not go through the registry. He knew the hand because he had received perhaps a dozen such notes in four years, never more than a few words each, never signed. He broke the wax with his thumbnail and read it standing at the desk with the cold tea in his other hand.

*A reconciliation auditor has been asking after Sennar entries. Three days, perhaps four. Not from the prefecture. From the center.*

He read it once. He set it down on the desk, face up, and drank some of the tea, which had gone past warm into the flat lukewarm he disliked, and he read it again.

Then he finished the tea, because the tea would be no better for waiting and the note would be no worse for his having finished it, and he sat down.

A reconciliation auditor asking after Sennar entries was, in itself, nothing. Sennar was contested ground. A petitioner had brought a magistrate's ruling under question some quarters back, and a contested magistracy drew auditors the way standing water drew insects; it was the office working as the office was built to work. He had watched three such audits cross the Sennar entries in four years and pass through them without a snag, because the entries were sound on their face and an auditor read the face. An auditor from the prefecture was routine. From the center was less routine. The center did not send a man down over a single contest unless the single contest had grown a tail.

He did not let the thought run ahead of what the note gave him. The note gave him an auditor, a region, and a span of days. It did not give him a name, an office, or a reason, and a man who acted on the part he had imagined rather than the part he had been told was a man who left marks where there had been none. He had survived four years by acting on what was in front of him and no more. He folded the note along its first crease and held it.

What was in front of him was this. Of his entries across four years, the Sennar cluster was the oldest and the densest, made in the period before he had learned to thin his work across jurisdictions, and four of the carry-forward entries in the original commission record traced to it. The four were sound. But sound on the face was a defense against an auditor who read the face, and an auditor sent from the center over a contest that had grown a tail might read deeper, might pull the prior commission's own minutes and lay them beside the carry-forward citations and count. If he counted, the count would hold for thirty-nine entries and would not hold for two, and the two were not in Sennar. The two he had initialed this morning were in jurisdictions east. But the method that found two would find them wherever they sat, and the method began in Sennar because Sennar was where a man would begin.

He rose and went to the wall of pigeonholes.

He made two adjustments before the morning session, and both were small, and both were the kind of thing a deputy registrar did a dozen times a week in the ordinary course of the office, which was why he had chosen them. The first was to the routing of the carry-forward schedule he had just initialed. He drew it back from the assessors' tray, where it would have gone up the line that day, and he reentered it on the supplementary list, the list of matter held for the close of the session pending a point of order, citing the standardization circular from New Terra as his ground; until the attestation forms were confirmed, he wrote, prudence held that fresh carry-forward entries wait. It was a defensible note, the note of a careful man, and it meant his two newest entries would not enter the standing record this quarter, would not exist for any auditor to find this quarter, would sit on a held list among a dozen others held for the same plausible reason, and could be quietly withdrawn entire if the days the note had warned him of turned into something worse.

The second adjustment took longer and showed less. He drew from the pigeonholes the working folder on the Sennar reconciliation, the one that held his own marginal notes from four years of entries, the small private record by which he kept his work straight in his own mind. None of it was evidence of anything; it was a registrar's working notes, and any registrar kept them. But his notes on Sennar were denser than his notes elsewhere, because Sennar had been first, and density was a thing a careful reader could see. He took the folder to the side table, and over the cooling kettle he separated the Sennar sheets from the rest, and he did not burn them, because a burned thing was a fact and ash in a registrar's office was a question, and he had no answer prepared for the question. He folded the Sennar sheets into the standardization circular instead, the genuine New Terra circular he had every reason to keep and to study, and he filed the whole back into the pigeonhole under the circular's reference, where the sheets were no longer Sennar notes at all but study material on attestation forms, and where they would draw no eye because the eye that found them would find them filed exactly where a diligent man would file them.

He washed his hands at the side table because the old paper had left its dust on them, dried them on the cloth he kept there, and put the kettle on again out of habit before he remembered he had drunk his two cups.

A knock. Okonjo, the assessor, with the morning list and a question about the order of the Lomé contests, and Sael answered it, and they spoke for a few minutes about nothing that mattered, the assessor leaning in the doorway with his thumbs in his belt, complaining about the standardization circular as every assessor in the building was complaining about it that week, and Sael agreed that it was a nuisance and that the forms had been fine as they were, and meant the agreement, because the circular had this morning done him a service the New Terra office had not intended. Okonjo went. The corridor outside was filling now, the day's voices rising in it, a trolley of folios rumbling past toward the assessors' room, the day's heat beginning to come up off the courtyard stone and in at the window with the smell of the dust the floor-keeper had not reached.

Sael Mokoena did not think of himself as a man with a network, because the word made it sound like a thing he commanded, and he commanded nothing. He had relations. A courier who carried what the registry could not carry and asked no questions because the questions were not his to ask, a man called Rashid who had moved paper for the office for years and moved a little more on the side because the side paid and the side was discreet. An attesting clerk two prefectures over who owed him a kindness done long ago and repaid it in attestations that did not look twice. The hand that had written the unsigned note, which was the only one of his relations that frightened him, because it was the only one above him, and a man's relations above him were the ones he did not get to manage. He did not let himself think long about the hand. The hand had warned him. A hand that warned him was a hand that wanted him intact, and he took the comfort of that and set the rest down.

The seat he had been passed over for, eleven years gone now, he thought of less often than he once had. It had been the prefectural registrarship at Adwa, the room he should have had, and the board had given it to a younger man with a louder family, and Sael had congratulated the younger man and meant the congratulation to be seen to be meant, and had gone back to the deputy's room he still occupied and understood, with the clarity that comes once or twice in a life, that the empire would never give him what his work had earned and that he would therefore take it by the means the empire had left lying about unguarded. The understanding had not been bitter. He had let the bitterness pass the way he let most things pass, and kept the understanding, which was the useful part. The younger man had since moved on to New Terra and a title. Sael had stayed, and learned the office to its roots, and the roots had turned out to be worth more than the title.

The session bell sounded from the assessors' floor, two strokes, the quarter-warning. He gathered the morning list and the banded contests and the initialed remainder of the schedule, the thirty-nine that would go up and the two that would wait, and he squared the stack against the desk's pale edge. He left the held list in the tray for the clerk to carry. He left the standardization circular in its pigeonhole with the Sennar sheets folded inside it. He left the unsigned note in his breast pocket, where it would stay until the walk home, when he would put it through the third terrace's public incinerator with the day's other private paper, one folded sheet among many, no ash anyone could question.

He stood at the window a moment before he went down. The courtyard below was in full sun now, the floor-keeper gone, a single clerk crossing it at an angle with a folio case under his arm and his head down against the brightness. Sael Mokoena watched him cross and reach the far arcade and pass out of the light into the shade of it, and he turned the warning over once more, and found it manageable, the way he had found such things manageable before. He had done this before. He did it now.

He picked up the stack and went down to the session.

# Chapter 11 — Asha's Finding

The financial records had come up from the vault in the wrong order, and Asha had been three hours putting them right before she let herself read a line of them.

That was the work no one credited and she had stopped expecting them to. The cross-reference clerks held the appointment files and the commission rosters and the prefectural registries, each in its own bureau's hand, and held them apart by long discipline so that no single office could read all of them together and learn what the reading showed. When a thing had to be read together it came to the Quarter, and it came in the order the clerks had pulled it, which was the order of the shelves and not the order of sense, and so the first labor of any such night was to take a stack that meant nothing in the order it arrived and lay it into the order that would let it speak. She had a working room for it now, a second room, smaller than the one Kaviel ran under the north wall and given over to the financial side because the financial side had grown past the table it began on. The table was full. It had been full by the second hour, the appointment ledgers squared along its length and weighted, and so the rest had gone to the floor, the disbursement registers laid out in ranks across the stone in the order she was building, the payment schedules beside them, the warrants of office in their banded files at the wall where she could reach them without rising and lose her place. She worked on her knees among them more than she worked in the chair. A younger officer would have asked for a larger room. Asha had learned forty years ago that a larger room was a room someone could ask the use of, and the use of this one was not a thing she wanted asked, and so she took the smaller room and put the work on the floor and said nothing.

The lamp stood on the table at the edge nearest the floor-work and she had angled the shade down so the light fell across the registers and not up into the room, and that left the upper air dim and the corners gone to dark and the stone underfoot lit in a long warm strip that moved as she moved the lamp. The building had emptied around her by degrees. The kitchens two floors down had banked their fires after the evening meal, and the bread-and-burnt-sugar warmth that threaded the upper floors by day had thinned to almost nothing, so that what reached her now under the closer smell of lamp oil was the dry cold smell of the documents themselves, the disbursement registers most of all, which had sat in a peripheral vault long enough to take the mineral chill of the stone into their bindings and give it back slowly into the warming room. She had eaten nothing since the noon table and did not notice. Somewhere far down the corridor a single watch officer made the round of the administrative floor at the turn of each hour, the even report of boots passing the door and going on, and between those passings the room held no sound but her own, the turn of a page, the dry scrape of a register drawn an inch nearer across the stone, the small click of the lamp's collar when she eased the shade.

The appointments were established. That work was Kaviel's and Zola's, two rooms over and weeks deep, the reconciliation trace running entry by entry through the carry-forward provision and turning up the gaps where an entry rested on a prior authority that did not hold. Asha was not tracing the appointments. She was reading the money under them. A false magistrate seated in the empire's name did not sit for nothing; he drew the salary of the office, the disbursements ran to him through the prefectural treasury on the warrant of his appointment, and the money left a track the appointment alone did not, because the money was handled by a different bureau than the one that confirmed the name, and the two bureaus did not love each other and did not reconcile their books except when forced. Where the appointment was a single clean line on a roster, the disbursement was a hundred small entries across years, each one initialed, dated, posted against a warrant, carried forward at the turn of each fiscal period into the next register. Asha read the money because the money could not be filed once and forgotten the way a name could. The money had to keep moving, and a thing that keeps moving leaves more chances to read it.

She had taken the Sennar disbursements first, because Sennar was where the appointments had gone hot soonest, and she had spent the early hours of the night matching the payment track of each questioned office against the warrant it ran on and the appointment the warrant cited. It was slow and it was clean and it was, she had begun to think by the fifth hour, going to tell her less than she had hoped. The money matched. That was the trouble. Each false magistrate drew his salary in the ordinary track, posted in the ordinary hand, carried forward at the ordinary intervals, and the ordinariness of it was correct because the men who built this had wanted it ordinary, had wanted the money to read as the money of a real office, and so they had made it real money in every respect but the legitimacy of the name at the top. There was nothing wrong with the disbursements. Asha read a hundred entries and found nothing wrong and felt the old patience settle in her, because nothing wrong was a finding too, and a man who could make a fraud's money read this clean was a man worth the reading.

It was past the sixth hour when she found the section that was too clean.

She had moved off the Sennar registers and onto the disbursements of the two jurisdictions beside it, the ones Zola had been tracing, and she had laid the new registers into the floor-ranks below the old and gone down onto her knees to read along them in the lamp-strip, register by register, year by year. And somewhere in the third year of the second jurisdiction the hand of the entries changed. Not the literal hand; the postings ran in the same clerk's writing as before and after, the same initials, the same ink. What changed was beneath the writing, in the bones of the thing, in how the entries sat against one another. Asha did not have a word for it at first and did not reach for one. She knelt over the register and read the section twice and let the wrongness declare itself before she named it, the way she had taught a hundred candidates to do and the way she still did, because the name a person reached for first was the name of the thing they expected and not the thing that was there.

What was there was order. Too much order. A live disbursement register kept across years by working clerks accumulated the small disorders of working clerks, the correction struck through and re-entered, the posting made a day late and dated true, the carry-forward at the fiscal turn that did not quite balance and was reconciled in the margin with an initial and a note. The Sennar registers had all of that, the ordinary friction of real hands doing real work over years, and Asha had read past it without remark because it was the texture of a thing that had grown the way such things grew. This section had none of it. Across three fiscal years the disbursements of the questioned offices ran without a single struck correction, without a late posting, without a margin note, every carry-forward balancing to the coin at the first attempt, every initial set down clean. It read the way a thing reads when it has been copied out fair from a draft. It read the way the candidate's clean finding had read in the assessment room across all the years, the right answer arrived at with no cost, and the no-cost was the tell.

She sat back on her heels and put the lamp closer and read the section a third time, slower, against the years on either side of it. The years before it had the friction. The years after it had the friction. The three years in the middle did not. Someone had gone into a live register that had been kept the ordinary disorderly way, and had taken out the disorder, and had set the entries back down clean. A correction pass. The phrase came to her plainly and she turned it over and did not like how well it fit. Not a correction of an error; the entries before the pass and after it ran to the same figures, the same warrants, the same offices. Nothing had been changed in what the money did. What had been changed was the record of how it had been done, the texture scrubbed out of it and a smooth surface laid down in its place, three years of a working clerk's small honest mess lifted off and replaced with three years that had never made a mistake.

Asha knew what it meant and made herself not yet know it. She rose, because her knee had gone to sleep under her and because she did not want to reach a finding from the floor, and she stood a moment in the dim upper air with the warm strip of the lamp below her and let the cold of the room come back into her attention, the chill the disbursement registers had carried up from the vault still banked in the corners where the lamp did not reach. Then she went to the wall and took down the warrants of office for the three years, the banded files she had set there so she would not lose her place, and she carried them back into the light and knelt again and read the warrants against the scrubbed registers.

The warrants told her the second thing. A correction pass on a live record was not a thing a clerk did on his own word. A register in the standing record was closed at the fiscal turn and could not be reopened and re-entered without authorization, because a register that could be quietly reopened was a register that meant nothing, and the whole empire ran on registers meaning something. So a correction pass left a track of its own, an authorization cited for the reopening, a code set against the reopened entries that said by whose authority the closed record had been touched. Asha found the codes where they had to be, in the margin of the reopened section, set down small and regular at the head of each fiscal year of the pass. She had expected to find the codes of the reconciliation office, the same bureau whose carry-forward provision had carried the false names in the first place, because that would have been the whole fraud closing on itself, the same hands cleaning up after the same hands. The codes were not the reconciliation office's. She read them twice to be sure, and then she carried the lamp to the small shelf where she kept the code tables that let an officer read an authorization back to the category of official who could issue it, and she ran the codes against the table, and she sat for a while with the table open under her hand and did not move.

The codes belonged to a category of authority she could not touch.

There were authorizations in the imperial administration that a Lioness could read straight through, because the Quarter's mandate ran to them, the working codes of the bureaus and the prefectures and the commissions, the hands of clerks and registrars and deputy registrars and the run of officials whose work was the record itself. And there were authorizations the mandate did not reach, set above the working bureaus, held by a category of senior official whose authority over the record was the authority to suspend its ordinary rules, and the codes in the margin of the scrubbed register belonged to that category. To read further, to learn which office of that category had issued the authorization and on whose word, Asha would have to pull the issuing record, and the issuing record sat above the line the mandate drew, and to cross that line she would have to ask for authority from above the Quarter. She knew the shape of what asking meant. Kaviel had told it to the young researcher in the north room the week the room opened, the same telling Asha had given her own juniors for forty years: the mandate was over paper, and the moment they reached past the paper to a hand they were doing a thing the charter did not cover, and a thing the charter did not cover had to be authorized from above the Quarter, and authorization from above the Quarter was a door, and a door could be watched from the other side.

She set the code table down and looked at the floor-ranks of registers in the long warm strip of the lamp, and she did not pull the issuing record, and she did not write a request to pull it, and she did not let herself, even for the length of a breath, frame the question that asking would have asked. The discipline held the way it had always held, the line drawn at the place where reading stopped and reaching began, and she stayed on the reading side of it because the work that crossed the line was work that warned the thing it crossed the line to find. She had spent her life teaching juniors that the rules were not a cage put on them by people who did not understand the work. The rules were the work. And here was the rules turned, the same line that protected the investigation made into the wall the investigation broke against, the category of authority she could not investigate used by someone who had known she could not, and the knowing was the finding. A clerk did not scrub a register. A reconciliation officer did not reach for a category of authority above his own bureau to do it. The correction pass had been run from above the line, by someone whose authority was the authority to suspend the rules, and that someone had cleaned up after the men who built the fraud, which meant the men who built the fraud were not the top of it.

Asha did not exclaim, because she had never in forty years exclaimed at a finding, and she did not bring it to anyone running, because there was no one to bring it to at the sixth hour past midnight and because a finding brought running was a finding half made. She did what she had taught. She logged it. She drew a clean sheet to the table edge in the lamp and set down the entry, the scrubbed section by jurisdiction and fiscal year, and the warrants it ran on, and the codes in the margin, and the category the codes belonged to, and what the category meant for the mandate, in the cramped exact hand that had filled the disposition box of a thousand intake ledgers. She did not set down what she suspected past the codes. The codes were a fact. The category was a fact. That the category could not be reached without escalating the mandate was a fact. That someone with that authority had run a correction pass over the fraud after the fraud was built was a fact, and the facts were the whole of what she logged, because the moment she wrote down what the facts meant about the man at the top she would be building on a thing she could not yet stand on, and she had spent her life refusing to build on that.

When the sheet was done she read it once for what it said and once for what a careful reader would take from it, and then she set it aside and took a second clean sheet and wrote the update for Kaviel. She did not go and find her. Kaviel was two rooms over or in her own office or gone home to the few hours she allowed herself, and a finding of this kind did not travel by a knock on a door at the sixth hour, where the knock itself could be read by anyone awake in the corridor as a thing worth a knock. It travelled the way the Quarter moved everything that mattered, in writing, by the channel, set down plain and left where Kaviel would have it at the first hour and no one else would have it at all. Asha wrote it the way she wrote everything, short, the finding stated and the category named and the wall it ran into marked plainly, with no urging in it and no alarm, because Kaviel would read the urging into the facts herself and would not need Asha to do it for her, and because a written update that carried its own alarm was an officer arguing with the reader on the page. She wrote that the disbursements of the two jurisdictions beside Sennar carried a scrubbed section across three fiscal years. She wrote that the scrub was a correction pass authorized under codes from a category the mandate could not reach. She wrote that the pass post-dated the original insertions, which meant a second hand had come in after the first. She did not write the conclusion. She trusted Kaviel to reach it, and she trusted her to reach the further thing past it, that a hand with that authority did not run a correction pass on a regional fraud out of idleness, that the reach of that category was not regional, and that the wall Asha had hit was no edge to the thing. It was the floor of a story that had more storeys above it than the room they were working in had let them imagine. She folded the update and set it in the channel tray and squared it, and that was the whole of what would leave the room before morning.

The watch officer passed the door again at the turn of the hour, the even boots going down the corridor and away, and the sound of them let Asha know how late it had got and that she had stopped marking the turns an hour or more ago. She gathered the day's work off the floor before she let herself stop, because the work did not get left on the floor of a room that had no use the building could name, and she squared the disbursement registers back into their ranks and banded the warrants and set them at the wall, and she put the scrubbed registers, the two she would want again at the first hour, where her hand would find them and no one else's eye would land on them by accident. Her knees ached when she rose with the last of them and she let them ache. The lamp had burned low and she trimmed it rather than fill it, because she was not going to be much longer.

She sat back into the chair, the first time she had used it that night, and she drew the scrubbed register up onto her knee in the trimmed light and opened it again to the head of the correction pass, to the small regular codes set down in the margin at the turn of the first fiscal year. She did not need to read them again. She had read them enough to carry them out of the room in her head and would. She looked at them anyway, the way a person looks at a thing whose meaning has changed under their hands and who wants to see whether it has changed back, and it had not. The codes sat in the margin clean and small in the same ink as the rest, set there by a clerk who had been told to set them there by a hand above the clerk, and the hand above the clerk had been moving by an authority that the men in the prefectures who built this had not had and could not have got, an authority that came down from a place above the working bureaus, from someone whom the working bureaus answered to and did not question, from someone the record itself was built to trust.

She looked at the codes a long time.

"Someone trusted," she said.

# Chapter 12 — Kaviel and the Man from the Transit Hub

The room had been chosen for what it would not say. It sat off the administrative spine of the Meridian transit facility, behind a door that gave onto a corridor used by no one who had a reason to hurry, and it had the furniture of a hundred such rooms in a hundred such facilities across the empire: a table bolted to the floor and two chairs that were not, under a single light panel set flush into the ceiling that gave a flat even brightness, falling on everything, favoring nothing. There was no window. There was a second door in the far wall, closed, and a man could read that second door as the way he had come in or as the way he would not be allowed to leave, and the room would not tell him which, and that was the point of the room. Kaviel had requested it by its facility code three days before and had given as her ground a records matter touching the transit registry, which was true the way the things she said were true, complete on its face and silent past it.

She was there before him. She always was, in these, because a man brought into a room already occupied read the occupant as the host and himself as the guest, and a guest behaved, and behavior was the thing she had come to read. She had laid nothing on the table. An empty table was worse for a man than a thick file, because a file had edges he could measure and an empty table held whatever he was afraid it held. She sat with her hands folded and the cold of the facility coming up through the floor into the soles of her boots, the air carrying the thin chemical note of a building scrubbed and recirculated and never opened, and she waited.

The operative brought him in and withdrew. That was the whole of the operative's part, to be a body in the doorway for the length of time it took Rashid Beye to cross the threshold and understand that he had been brought somewhere by people who did not explain, and then to be gone, the door easing shut behind him so that the closing was a sound and not a slam. Kaviel had told her to do it exactly that way. A slam was a threat, and a threat gave a frightened man a thing to brace against. A door that merely closed gave him nothing.

She knew his face. She had carried it for four years without a frame to put it in, and now the frame had come up out of the record to meet the face, and the two had locked together in the cold room: the man by the document desk at Sabra Crossing, the courier's satchel across his body, the even unremarkable features a witness would swear they could not recall. He was older than the memory by its four years and otherwise the same, a man built to pass through a crowd and leave no wake, and she let none of the recognition reach her face, because the recognition was hers and not yet his to know she had.

"Sit," she said.

He sat. He did it without protest and without ease, lowering himself into the chair with the care of a man who did not yet know the rules of the room and did not want to be the one who broke them first. His hands he put on the table, flat, where she could see them, and that told her something: a man who put his hands where they could be seen had been in a room like this before, or had imagined it often enough that the imagining had taught him the posture. She filed it. She did not look at his hands a second time.

"You know where you are," she said. It was not a question and she did not let it rise into one.

"A transit office."

"You know what facility."

"Meridian." He had a low voice, level, the voice of a man who had learned that the safest thing to do with a voice was to give it nothing to carry. "The clerks took my transit slip at the lower gate and walked me up. They didn't say why."

"They wouldn't. That isn't their work." She let the silence open after it, a small deliberate space, and watched him decline to fill it. That was the second thing. A courier who knew nothing filled silences, because silence frightened a man who had nothing to hide more than it frightened a man who had everything to hide; the innocent talked to make the trouble go away, and the trouble was that talk was how the innocent convinced you. Rashid Beye sat in the silence and let it be hers, and a man who could do that was a man who had been trained, or had trained himself, in the one discipline a mere courier had no use for.

She turned over the slip of paper she had kept face down on the table, the only paper in the room, and slid it across to him. It was a transit manifest, a copy, the header and the facility chop of Sabra Crossing on it and a date four years gone, and the routing she had written that night in her own small hand, the onward routing of a woman to a seventh gate, was not on it, because that woman's movement had been filed in a place this man would never see. What was on it was the ordinary traffic of the crossing that hour, the southern feeder out of Sennar among it, and a notation she had added in pencil against one of the freight entries, a satchel of attestation matter routed through a peripheral relay, and his initial, B., beside it, in the dry hand of an analyst who had reduced a man to a letter and now sat across from the rest of him.

He read it. He read it the way Mokoena would have read a schedule, she thought, though she did not yet know she was thinking of Mokoena, no slower over the line that touched him than over the lines that did not, his eyes moving down the page at the even pace of a man determined not to teach the page where he lived. When he had read it he did not push it back. He left it where it was, between them, and looked up.

"That's a transit record," he said.

"It is."

"I move paper. That's not a thing anyone hides."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She let that be true in the room a moment, because it was true and because a man relaxed by a degree when you granted him a true thing, and the degree he relaxed by was a reading too. "You move paper between jurisdictions. You've done it for years, for offices that have a reason not to put it through their own registry. There's no charge in that. Half the empire's honest business moves the way you move it, because the registries are slow and the work is real and someone has to carry the thing that can't wait for the slow channel. I'm not here about a satchel."

"Then I don't know what you're here about."

"You will." She left it there, and did not lean toward him, and did not change the level of her voice, because the thing she had come to do was not done by leaning. "I'm going to tell you what I have, and then I'm going to tell you the only choice in it that's still yours, and you're going to listen to both before you say anything, because the first thing a man says in a room like this is the thing he prepared on the walk up, and I have no use for it."

He said nothing. The light panel hummed faintly overhead, a register she felt behind her eyes more than heard, and the cold went on coming up through the floor, and somewhere beyond the far door a cart rolled across stone and faded, the building running its night traffic indifferent to the room inside it.

"You carry attestation matter," she said. "Carry-forward attestations, among other things. Prior-authority citations, originating-office attestations, the paper that lets an appointment enter the standing record on the word that an older commission already confirmed it. You carry it between the office that originates it and the offices that need it, and you carry it by hand and outside the registry, because the registry leaves a route and your offices would rather there were no route." She watched his hands. They did not move. "I can show that you carried it. I can lay your transit slips beside the dates the attestations entered the reconciliation, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and the slips and the dates will agree, because they always agree, because you're careful and careful men leave the cleanest trail of all. A clean trail is still a trail. I have it."

"Carrying paper isn't a crime," he said again, but he said it more slowly this time, and the slowness was the third thing.

"It isn't. You're right. And if all you carried was the slow channel's honest backlog, I'd have no reason to spend a transit room on you." She let the room hold the next part before she gave it to him. "But you didn't carry only that. The attestations you moved into Sennar, and east of Sennar, cite prior commissions that sat and confirmed appointments other than the ones the attestation claims they confirmed, and prior commissions that in two instances never sat at all. The appointments those attestations let into the record are false. The magistrate they seated is false. He rules in the empire's name on the strength of a paper you carried, and I can trace the paper to your hand, and the hand to the satchel, and the satchel to this date and this crossing, four years back, where I first saw you stand by a document desk and find a bench with your eyes that you had no errand at."

That reached him. It did not show in his hands and it did not show in his face, which stayed the careful nothing he had brought into the room, but it showed in the breath, the small held beat where a man recalculates the floor he is standing on, and Kaviel saw it and did not name it and did not press on it, because a man who is recalculating must be left to finish or he answers from the old picture and not the new.

"I didn't read the paper," he said at last. "A courier doesn't read the paper. That's the work. You carry what's sealed and you don't break the seal, and if the thing inside is false you've no way to know it and no business knowing it. I carried a sealed satchel. That's all I did."

"I believe you," Kaviel said, and meant it, and let him hear that she meant it. "I think you didn't break a seal. I think you carried what you were given and asked nothing, because asking is not the work and a courier who asks doesn't stay a courier. If that were the whole of what you knew, you'd be a thing I note and set aside, because a man who carried a sealed lie in good faith is not the man I came up these stairs for."

She let the room go quiet again, and this time she let the quiet do the work, because she had laid the first half of the trap, which was to tell him he was nothing, and now she would watch whether he believed it, because a man who was nothing accepted being nothing with relief, and a man who was more than he had said could not quite manage the relief, and the failure to manage it was the thing she had come the whole way to read.

He did not manage it.

It was small. A man who had only ever carried sealed satchels in good faith, told by the authority in the cold room that he was therefore not the quarry, would have eased, would have let his shoulders come down off the brace he had walked in with, would have reached for the next true thing to say to get himself out the door he could now believe was the way out. Rashid Beye did not ease. He held the brace. He held it because he knew something the courier's account did not cover, and he was waiting to learn whether she knew he held it, and the holding was its own confession, legible to her in the cold light as a written line, and she read it and gave no sign that she had.

"You knew the routing," she said.

He was still.

"A courier carries what he's given between the points he's told. He doesn't choose the route. He doesn't know why this satchel goes to Sennar this quarter and east of Sennar the next, or why the one that fed Sabra Crossing came up off the feeder that didn't post to a board. He carries point to point and the why stays with the hand that loads the satchel." She watched him hold the brace. "But you knew the routing changed. You knew it thinned. Four years ago you carried into Sennar, dense, the same crossing twice in a quarter, and then the work spread out across jurisdictions that don't connect, fewer to each, and a man who only carried wouldn't have felt the spreading, because to a man who only carries each run is its own errand. You felt it. You felt the work being thinned across jurisdictions on purpose, the way a man thins a thing he doesn't want counted, and a courier who feels that is a courier who's been told more than the work requires, or has been carrying long enough for the same hand that he's stopped being only a pair of legs and started being someone the hand trusts with the shape."

His hands moved then, for the first time, the smallest withdrawal, the fingers drawing in off the flat of the table by a hair as if the table had gone cold under them, and he stopped the movement before it finished, and the stopping was as loud to her as the movement.

"You're guessing," he said.

"I'm reading," she said. "I do it the way you carry. It's the work, and I've done it long enough that I don't have to break a seal to know what's in the satchel." She did not raise the voice. "I'm not going to tell you what I'll do with what you've shown me, because that isn't the choice in the room. The choice in the room is smaller than that and it's the only thing left that's yours." She let him have a breath of the silence, one, and no more. "You can stay the man who carried a sealed satchel in good faith. That man walks out of here tonight on his own transit slip, and what I have on him goes into a file, and the file waits, and one day the file is opened by someone deciding what the man who carried the satchels was, and there is no one in the room that day to say he was anything but the legs. Or you can be the man who knew the shape, who felt the work thin and understood why, and that man tells me the hand. Not the office. Not the registrar two prefectures over who owed a kindness. The hand above the registrar, the one that loads the routing and thins it and trusts a courier with the feel of it. That man's file reads differently, because that man chose, while the choosing was still his, to be the one who said the shape out loud."

She did not say the rest of it, that she did not yet know whose hand it was, that the whole of her case ran up to a ceiling she could not pass and stopped there, that this man across the cold table might be the one rung on the ladder she could reach who had ever stood close enough to the hand to feel its weight. She did not say it because saying it was showing him the floor of her own position, and the floor of her position was the one thing in the room she would not lay on the table.

"I don't know a name," he said.

"I didn't ask for a name." She let that land, because it was the truest thing she had said and the most useful. "A courier who knew a name would be a courier already dead, and you're alive, so you don't know a name, and I haven't asked you for the thing your being alive proves you don't have. I asked you for the hand. You've carried for it. You know how the routing reaches you, who hands you the loaded satchel, who changes the points, who sent you to a crossing four years ago to stand at a document desk and watch a bench. You know the shape of the hand the way a man knows the weight of a thing he's carried a long time without ever seeing it whole. That's what's yours to give. The name belongs to someone above you and I'll get it another way. The shape is yours."

He looked at the manifest on the table, the four-year-old paper with his initial penciled beside the freight line, and he was quiet a long time, and Kaviel let him be, because the thing she had built in the room was built and pressing on a man did not make a structure hold faster, it only taught him she was anxious, and she was not going to teach him that. The cold came up through the floor. The light hummed. Beyond the far door the night traffic of the facility rolled and faded and rolled again, and the chemical scrub of the recirculated air sat at the back of her throat where it had sat the whole hour, and she breathed it and waited and gave the man the one thing the room could afford to give him, which was time enough to count.

"If I tell you the shape," he said slowly, "I'm a man who told. There's no file that reads well enough to fix that."

"No," Kaviel said. "There isn't. I won't lie to you about the room you'll be in after this one, because a lie now is a thing you'd catch later and it would cost me the only thing I'm asking you to believe, which is that the two doors are real and one of them is worse." She held his eyes. "I'm not offering you a good outcome. I don't have one to offer. I'm offering you the difference between a man who chose and a man who was chosen for, and that difference is the whole of what's left in your hands, and it's small, and it's real, and in a year it will be the only thing about tonight that was ever yours."

He gave her the shape.

He gave it carefully, the way he did everything, in pieces that each on their own were nothing and together were the thing she had come for: how the loaded satchel reached him from a relay clerk who never spoke past the handoff, never from Mokoena's own hand; how the routing came written on a slip he was to burn, and how the slips had changed, four years back, from a single repeating point to a spread that grew with each quarter; how once, only once, the routing had come from higher and not from the relay at all, in a script the relay clerk handled as a man handles a thing he is afraid of, and how after that the spread had widened all at once across jurisdictions that touched three Thrones' worth of administrative ground, more than any one registrar's office reached, more than Mokoena reached, a reach that had to belong to someone whose authority ran where Mokoena's stopped. He did not have the name. He had never had the name. But he had felt the weight of the hand through the paper for four years, and he laid the weight of it on the table now, piece by careful piece, and Kaviel took it the way she took everything, without exclaiming, filing each piece against the gap in her case where it fit, and finding that it fit, and finding that the gap it fit was the one above Mokoena, the ceiling she had not been able to pass, and that this man had carried the feel of what stood above it without ever once seeing its face.

It was partial. She knew it for partial as she took it, a shape and a weight and a reach, and no name, and she did not press him past the partial, because pressing a man past what he had would only teach him to invent, and an invented piece was worse than a missing one. She took the shape he had and accepted that it was the shape he had, and she set the rest down where she set the things that were not yet hers to chase, in the file she might open later, with a hand she would have to find another way.

When he had finished there was nothing more in him to give and they both knew it, and she did not thank him, because thanks would have made the room a transaction between equals and the room had never been that, and a man who had just told would read thanks as a thing said to ease her own part in what she had done to him. She stood. The chair was bolted and hers was not, and the small scrape of it against the stone was the loudest sound the room had held, and he flinched at it by a degree, the first thing in the hour he had not managed.

"The operative outside will see you to your transit," she said. "Your slip is good. You'll make your routing."

He looked up at her, and there was a thing in his face now that had not been there when he came in, the look of a man who has understood the size of what he has done and cannot take it back and has nowhere to put it, and Kaviel had seen that look on the faces she carried and would carry his with the rest, into the file she might never open and would never forget she had.

She left the manifest on the table. She had no further use for it and taking it would have looked like collecting a trophy, and she did not collect them. She crossed to the door she had come in by, the one of the two that had been hers the whole hour, and she opened it onto the cold corridor where the operative waited, and she went out into the recirculated air and the flat continuous light of the transit spine, and she did not look back at him, and she heard the door ease shut behind her on its own weight, a sound and not a slam.

# Chapter 13 — The Courtier's Position

The apartments Councillor Hadi Marun held in the imperial complex had been allotted to his office and not to him, and a man who had lived in them eleven years could tell the difference on any afternoon he cared to. The rooms came with the seat. The furniture was the furniture the household provided to a councillor of his grade, the long couch and the writing table and the two chairs of pale upland wood set at the angle the household set them in every apartment of the grade, and when Marun had taken the seat he had moved nothing, because a man who rearranged the household's furniture announced that he meant to stay, and a man who announced he meant to stay invited the question of whether he should. So he had left the couch where the stewards left it and added to the room only the things a man was permitted to add, the small things that read as taste and cost little to abandon. A brass water clock from the river country, a good one, that he kept filled and running because the sound of it filled the silences a councillor learned to leave in a conversation. A wall of bound petitions he had argued and won, spined in red, set where a visitor's eye would find them without being led. A single low table of black river-stone that had been his mother's and was the only object in the apartments he would have carried out himself if the seat were ever taken from him, and which a visitor read as a councillor's affectation and never as the one true thing in the room.

It was the slack hour of the afternoon. The court had recessed its committees until the evening session and would not want him until the lamps were lit, and he had told his clerk he would read in his rooms, which the clerk understood to mean he was not to be disturbed and did not understand to mean anything else. Through the shared ventilation that ran the wall behind the writing table came the smell of someone else's late meal cooking two apartments down, a southern dish heavy with the burnt-sweet of caramelized onion and a fish stock under it, and the smell came and went as the draught in the duct shifted, so that the apartment was by turns his own and someone else's, his own dry air of paper and brass-polish and then, on the turn of the draught, the kitchen of a man he had never met cooking a supper Marun would not be invited to. He had stopped noticing it years ago and noticed it today, which told him something about the afternoon before he had let himself know it.

The message had come at noon.

It lay on the writing table where he had set it down, a single folded sheet that had come through the ordinary correspondence and read, to any clerk who handled it, as ordinary correspondence, a note from a colleague about the seating of a minor review body in the river prefectures, the kind of note that crossed the complex by the hundred in any week. He had read it standing, the way he read most things, and then he had sat down, which he had not meant to do, and he had read it again sitting, and he had been sitting with it for the better part of two hours now while the water clock measured the afternoon out of the brass vessel into the lower bowl one slow bead at a time.

The note was not about a review body. The colleague who appeared to have sent it had not sent it; the colleague did not exist, or existed only as a name a man could write at the foot of a sheet, and the body of the note carried under its dull official surface a second reading that opened only to a reader who knew the river prefectures had no such review body seated this season. Marun knew it. He had voted the river rosters himself. So when the note told him that the seating would proceed and that his concurrence would be valued and that the matter would close cleanly before the cold session, he read what it told the man who knew there was no seating, which was that he was being asked again, more plainly than before, and that the asking had reached the stage where the form of it had stopped pretending to need his agreement and had begun describing what his agreement would look like once it was given.

He set the kettle of the water clock straight, which it did not need, and watched a bead of water gather at the lip of the upper vessel and hang and fall.

The arithmetic was not hard. That was the thing he kept arriving at and turning away from across the afternoon, that the arithmetic was not hard and never had been, and that a man told himself a choice was hard when what he meant was that he did not like the side the arithmetic came down on. He owed in two directions. He had always owed in two directions; a councillor who owed in only one direction was a councillor who had not yet been in the seat long enough, and Marun had been in the seat eleven years. He owed the throne the form of his service and the throne had been, in its exact cold way, fair to him, had heard his river prefectures and ruled against the feeling and for the law and had told him to his face that it would read the instrument and not the sentiment, which was as much as a man in his grade could ask and more than most received. And he owed the other direction the substance of a debt that had begun small, the way these debts began, in a favor done him years ago that he had taken without weighing because it had cost him nothing to take and would have cost him standing to refuse.

He let himself think about the favor, because he had spent the afternoon not thinking about it and the not-thinking had grown heavier than the thing itself.

The seat on the river commission. Eight years gone now. He had earned it, by any reading of the rosters he had earned it, the senior councillor of the river caucus with the longest unbroken service and the cleanest record of any man in line, and the seat had gone instead to a younger man out of a northern house whose grandfather had sat where the younger man's father now sat, and Marun had swallowed it the way a courtier swallowed such things, in public with grace and in private with a long quiet rage that he had been careful never to let reach his face. And a man had come to him, not long after, an unremarkable man with the air of a senior clerk, who had said that the matter of the river commission had been noticed by people who thought it ill done, and that such things could be set right, in time, by men who understood the value of setting them right, and that Marun need do nothing now but understand that he was understood. And Marun, who was good at being liked and had built a life on being liked and had felt for the first time in his career the cold of being passed over by men who liked him fine and chose another anyway, had let himself be understood. He had said nothing. He had done nothing. He had taken the favor of being understood the way he had taken the household's furniture, by leaving it where it was set and not asking what it would cost to keep it.

He had told himself across the years that the favor had been the rage's doing and not his own, that a man wronged in the open could be forgiven a private warmth toward whoever offered to set the wrong right, and that the warmth had committed him to nothing. He could see the lie in it now, sitting with the noon note under his hand. The rage had been real and the wrong had been real, and neither had made the choice for him; he had made it himself, in the comfortable dark of doing nothing, which a courtier learned early was its own kind of doing. A man who chose nothing was a man who let the choosing be done by whoever was patient enough to wait him out, and the unremarkable man with the senior clerk's air had been very patient, and Marun had been, for eight years, very comfortable. He turned the brass kettle a quarter on its base, the way Lady Provost Shandt had turned a cup at the autumn dinner without drinking from it, and set it back where it had been, and watched the lower bowl fill.

It had cost a small thing the first time, a year on. A name he had been asked to let pass on a roster he was reviewing, a confirmation he had been asked not to look at twice. He had let it pass. It had been one name among a hundred and the hundred were dull and the one had read no differently than the rest, and a man who let one dull name pass on a long dull roster had done, he had told himself, nothing at all. He had been right that it was nothing and wrong about everything that followed from it, because the men who built these debts built them exactly so, out of nothings, out of single names on long rosters, so that the man who had let the first one pass would find, when the second came, that he had already become the kind of man who let names pass, and that to refuse the second was to admit the first, and that the admitting cost more every season the debt ran.

The southern kitchen turned in the duct again and the onion-sweet came across the room, and Marun sat in his allotted furniture among the spined red petitions of the things he had won and the one black table that was truly his, and understood that the note on the writing table was the second name. Not one name now. A run of them. The note's true reading asked him to do for the cold session's appellate rosters what he had once done for a single confirmation, to carry forward a body of entries out of the Outer Prefectures into the courts he had standing to touch, and to do it as a man of his grade and reputation could do it, cleanly, with his concurrence on the record where his concurrence carried weight, so that what passed under his name passed unquestioned because it passed under his name.

He knew what the entries were. He had not let himself know it in words before this afternoon and he knew it now in words, because the note had stopped pretending and so could he. The carry-forward backlog out of the Outer Prefectures. The thing the young magister had been indiscreet about at the autumn dinner, the books grown fat, the chain in which every link trusted the link before it and no link bore weight. He had been at that dinner. He had heard the talk move along the colonnade the way talk moved, and he had marked it the way a courtier marked everything, and he had understood even then, without letting the understanding surface, that the talk was about the thing he had let pass eight years running, grown now from one name to a weight that could fatten the books of three prefectures.

There was a knock.

It was the soft double knock of the household and then, before he answered, the voice through the door, and the voice was Councillor Imani Tau's, and Marun was on his feet and at the door with the easy warmth already arranged on his face before he had decided to arrange it, because the arranging was eleven years deep and did not wait on his decision.

"Hadi." Tau came in the way she came into every room, without ceremony and without quite waiting to be asked, a spare upright woman ten years younger than Marun with the river-country length of bone and a way of looking at a man as though she had already decided to think well of him and was waiting only to be shown she was right. She held a sheaf of paper against her chest. "Your clerk said you were reading. I told him reading was the one thing you have never once done in these rooms and he should let me by."

"He let you by."

"He is afraid of me. It is my best quality." She set the sheaf down on the long couch, not on the writing table, which Marun noted with a gratitude that surprised him, because she could not have known what lay on the writing table and had kept clear of it anyway, out of the plain courtesy that was how she moved through the world. "The northern water rates. I have the redraft. I have made it worse for the northern houses and better for the guilds and I want a councillor who is not from either to tell me whether I have made it fair or only made it satisfying, because I cannot tell the two apart anymore and you can. You always could."

She sat without being asked and looked up at him, and the afternoon light off the interior court came through the high window and laid a band of it across the couch and across the redraft she had carried up to him because she trusted his reading, and Marun stood over her and felt the two hours at the writing table sitting in him like a stone swallowed whole.

This was the other reading. He understood it standing there with the warmth still arranged on his face, that Imani Tau was the door he was not walking through, that a man could spend the years he had left as the councillor she came to with her redrafts because his reading was clean and his name was good, the councillor whose concurrence meant a thing was fair, and that the price of that door was only the price he had been refusing to pay for eight years, which was to admit the first name, and the second, and to let the seat be taken from him in the open by men who would never know why he had given it up. She did not know any of it. She had carried her water rates up three floors to the one colleague she was sure would tell her the truth, and the truth was the only thing in the room she had come for, and she would have it from him because he had never once given her anything else.

"Read it to me," he said, and sat down across from her in the household's pale wood, and let her read, and listened to the whole of it, the way the throne had listened to the herder, because listening was the one thing left in the afternoon he could do cleanly. She read the redraft through, the rates and the relief schedules and the small careful clauses where she had given the guilds their margin, and her voice had the flat working pleasure in it of a person doing a hard thing well, and the water clock measured it out bead by bead, and the southern kitchen came and went in the duct, and Marun heard every word and held two readings of the afternoon in his hands at once, the redraft on the couch and the note on the table behind him, and knew that he could not hold both past the day.

"It is fair," he said when she finished, and it was, and he told her where it was not yet fair, the two clauses in the relief schedule that read as relief and worked as delay, and she bent over the sheaf and marked them, fast, the way a person marks a thing they had half known and needed only to be told, and she did not thank him because between them thanks had long ago worn down to the work itself.

"You are good for me, Hadi," she said, rising, gathering the sheaf back against her chest. "The others tell me what I want. You tell me what it is." She was at the door. "Come to the river caucus before the session. They have missed you. You have been a stranger this season." And she was gone, the soft latch of the door, her step quick down the corridor, and the apartment closed back around him into his own dry air and the turning kitchen and the slow falling beads of the clock.

He stood a while where she had left him.

Then he went to the writing table and sat down and drew the lamp nearer though the light from the court was not yet gone, and he took a clean sheet from the household stock and uncapped the ink, and he wrote the reply. He wrote it as a note about a review body in the river prefectures, dull and exact, a colleague's concurrence in a routine seating, the kind of note that crossed the complex by the hundred and would read to any clerk who handled it as nothing at all. He wrote the second reading into the first the way it had been written to him, so that the man who knew there was no review body would read what he meant, which was that the matter would close cleanly before the cold session, and that his concurrence could be valued, and that what passed under his name would pass. He wrote it in his own good hand, the hand on the spined red petitions along the wall, and he did not hurry it and did not labor it, and when it was done he read it once to be sure the surface held, and it held.

The southern kitchen came across the room a last time, the onion-sweet gone now to something scorched at its edge, a sauce held too long over the fire two apartments down, and Marun folded the sheet along its crease and pressed the fold flat with the side of his thumb and sealed it. He set it in the collection tray at the corner of the writing table, where the household's runner would take it with the evening's other correspondence, among the routine notes a councillor sent out at the close of any working day. He capped the ink. He did not look at the tray again.

J7. FIELD LOG. Posting under cover of freight audit, Meridian feeder spur. Three weeks. S.

DAY 1. Arrived ahead of schedule, took the spur clerk's room over the weighbridge. Smell of axle grease through the floorboards day and night. Vantage on the lower yard, the attestation shed, the courier gate. Established as auditor's runner. No one asks a runner anything.

DAY 2. Mapped the shed traffic. Couriers in at the half-hour, satchels chopped, out the south gate. Nothing here a manifest would not hold. Pattern is the point. Watch the breaks in the pattern.

DAY 4. Mokoena's man through the gate, southern feeder. Recognized from Meridian. Beye. Carried the attestation satchel, left it at the shed window, did not wait for the chop. A courier who does not wait for his chop is not carrying his own paper. Noted. Said nothing.

DAY 6. Shed clerk drinks at the weighbridge canteen, talks. Says the J7 reconciliation went up clean this quarter, fewer holds than Sennar, faster. Says it the way a man says a thing he was told to be pleased about. The bread here is the flat gray kind, sour, and the tea is worse, and a clerk who buys both for a stranger wants the stranger to stay and listen.

DAY 9. Quiet. Logged the gate counts. Sat the yard from the cold side so the sun would not put me on the glass.

DAY 11. Black transit, no facility chop, in through the freight gate after the day shed had closed. This does not happen on a spur. A man came down from it. Heavy build, deliberate, an aide a half-step behind carrying nothing, which is how you know the man matters. He did not go to the shed. He went to the spur registrar's office, which was dark, and the registrar opened it for him, and the lamp came up behind the blind. Forty minutes. The aide stood at the door the whole forty and watched the yard and saw a runner crossing it with a folio case and did not mark her, because a runner with a folio case at that hour is the yard working as the yard works.

DAY 11, later. Did not log the man's face to the registry. Logged it here. I have seen it. I will set down where. The Bureau circular on attestation forms carried a plate of the New Terra office at its standardization session, the senior table, and his face is on the plate, third from the convening chair. A man whose face is on the plate of the New Terra senior table does not come down a freight spur after dark to sit forty minutes in a dark registrar's office and leave no chop, no entry a ledger could hold. He came to be in the room. Whatever was done in the forty minutes was done by his being in the room, the way Mokoena's entries were done by a true name carrying a false errand. I do not yet know that this means what I think it means. I am not going to write it up until I do.

DAY 12. The spur registrar burned something in the canteen stove before first light. Sour smoke, paper. I was on the cold side and saw the flue take it. A registrar who burns at his own stove and not at the public incinerator on the terrace is burning a thing he does not want carried past his own door.

DAY 14. Beye through again. Same satchel routine. The reconciliation schedule he leaves is thicker by a third than the J7 traffic warrants. The traffic does not warrant it. Something is being moved into J7 that did not originate in J7. Noted. Did not act.

DAY 17. Confirmed the black transit's registry plate against the freight gate's own counter log, which counts axles and keeps no names. One uncharted entry, Day 11, after close. The gate counter does not lie and does not know what it counted.

DAY 19. The aide was at the canteen at midday, alone, no transit in the yard. So he has been left here. A man who matters does not leave his own aide on a freight spur unless the spur is now part of the work and wants a steady hand on it. The work has put down a root here. I have what the records cannot give. I am carrying it out by hand.

DAY 21. Closed the posting. Returned the clerk's room as found. Freight audit filed clean, no irregularity, signed off by the spur registrar himself, who shook my hand at the gate and was glad to see the auditor's runner go.

# Chapter 14 — The Source Eleanor Protected

The residence sat at the end of a lane that went nowhere else, behind a wall too low to mean anything and a gate that had been left unlatched on purpose. It belonged to no one either party would name. A widow of some standing had lived in it once and died in it, and the title had passed through a trust that owned other things in other quarters, and the trust answered to a firm of agents who answered to a deed of administration so old and so dull that following it to its end would take a clerk a season and reward him with nothing. Eleanor had used it three times in forty years. She had chosen it again because a place used rarely and never twice in close succession kept the one virtue a meeting like this one needed, which was that it could not afterward be made to mean what had happened in it.

She arrived first, which was her habit and also her instruction to herself, and she had Sefu's men go through the rooms before she sat. She expected nothing. A room you have not seen searched is a room you are guessing about, and she had stopped guessing about rooms a long time ago. They found dust under the sheets that draped the furniture, and a smell of a house shut up too long, paper and cold ash and the faint sweetness of fruit left to go over somewhere out of sight, and a clock that had stopped at a quarter past some hour years gone and had not been wound since. She had them leave the clock. A stopped clock told a visitor that the house did not run, that nothing here was kept, and that was the truth she wanted the room to say on her behalf before she said anything herself.

She sat in the front room in a chair she had uncovered with her own hands, the sheet folded once and laid over the arm of another. The window gave onto the dead lane. The light came in gray and even, a flat afternoon light with no sun in it, and it laid no bar across the floor and warmed nothing, and she was glad of that too. She had asked Kaviel to come and to come quietly and to take the chair set back from the table, against the wall where the light did not reach, and to say nothing for the whole of it whatever was said, and Kaviel had heard the instruction the way she heard all of them, without asking what it was for, and had taken the chair against the wall, and had not moved since.

Eleanor did not look at her daughter. There was nothing to look at. The room held a table and three chairs and a stopped clock and two women who were good at being still, and outside it the afternoon thinned toward the early dark of the season, and they waited for the third without speaking, because the thing they were waiting for did not improve for being discussed and Eleanor had taught the girl that before she could write.

He came on foot. They heard the gate, which had been left unlatched so that it would make its small sound and not a larger one, and then the door, and then a tread in the hall that Eleanor knew before she could have said how she knew it, a careful tread, a man who had spent his life learning where the boards of strange rooms gave and had never lost the habit even where no one watched. She had heard that tread once before, twenty-one years ago, in a different house, at the beginning of the thing that was ending now. She had been a younger woman then in every way that the body counts and few of the ways that mattered. She did not rise.

He stopped in the doorway. Senior Magister Aurelio Saad was an old man now, older than she had let herself picture across the years of the channel, because she had built the channel so that she would never picture him, never hold a face for the name, never give the apparatus the one thing a captured mind could be made to surrender. She had known his age as a figure in a file and had refused the image, and the image stood in the doorway now and corrected the figure downward and upward at once, a tall man gone spare, the skin at his throat loose over a frame that had carried more, the hands very still at his sides, the eyes steady in a way that did not ask anything of the room. He wore the plain dark of a senior official off his hours, good cloth worn past its season, the dress of a man who had money and no use he would put it to. He looked at her a moment. Then he looked at the chair set for him, and the one against the wall, and the woman in it, and he did the arithmetic of the second chair in the time it took to draw a breath, and his face did not change.

"You brought a witness," he said. His voice was lower than the channel had let her imagine, dry, unhurried.

"I brought my own," Eleanor said. "She will not speak. She will not write. She is here because I will not carry this alone and there is no one else I would let carry it."

He took that in. He looked at Kaviel once more, longer, and Kaviel met the look and gave it nothing, which was correct, and Eleanor felt the correctness of it pass through the room like cold off a pane. Then he crossed to the chair and sat, slowly, his hands finding the table and resting there flat, an old man's care for his own weight on unfamiliar furniture.

"Twenty-one years," he said, "and I have never seen your face until today."

"No."

"I told myself, at the start, that it was a clerk. That I was writing into a drawer." He turned one hand over on the table, looked at the palm, turned it back. "I knew it was not a drawer by the third year. The things that stopped happening. A man who should have risen and did not. A schedule that should have gone through and was held. You cannot hide a drawer's work that well. A drawer does nothing. Someone was doing something with what I sent, and doing it carefully, and so I knew there was a hand, and I went on telling myself it was a clerk because the clerk was the thing I could live beside."

Eleanor said nothing. He had not asked her anything, and the silence was a kind she knew, the silence of a man laying down a thing he had carried so long that the setting of it down was the whole of what he had come to do, and you did not help a man set down a weight by reaching for it before his hands had opened. She let him hold it the last moment he would hold it.

"It is no longer a clerk you can pretend me to," he said. "And I am no longer a man you can run at the end of a channel. That is why I am in a chair in a dead woman's house instead of three intermediaries away from you where I have been safe and useful for twenty-one years. You know this. I will not waste the afternoon telling you what we both carried in with us."

"No," Eleanor said. "Tell me what I do not have."

He drew a breath. The light from the dead lane did not move on him. Somewhere in the back of the house the fruit she had smelled on the way in went on quietly turning, and the cold came up through the floor of the unheated room and found her ankles, and she sat in it and waited.

"The review you stood up," he said. "Four prefectures, routine language, your authority, two years ago now. I knew what it was the day the order crossed my desk, because I was meant to read it and pass it along and I read it the way you taught a man to read without ever teaching him, by reading what it worked to seem. It worked very hard to seem ordinary. I have spent my life among orders that worked to seem ordinary. I knew you had moved. And I knew that if you had moved, the thing I had been feeling under the paper for years had reached the size where it moved you, and that frightened me more than anything I had sent you, because I had hoped, the way a man hopes, that the size I felt was a thing I was imagining out of guilt."

"It was not."

"It was not." He was quiet. "I have the authorization category. The one your people will have found and will not have been able to follow, because following it requires the standing to question the office it issues from, and the office it issues from is high enough that questioning it is itself a report filed about you to the man you would be questioning. Your investigators will have hit that ceiling. They will have called it a ceiling. I have spent twenty-one years on the other side of it. I can tell you whose ceiling it is."

Eleanor did not move. Against the wall, in the chair the light did not reach, Kaviel did not move either, and the room held the three of them in the gray even light with the stopped clock between them, and Aurelio Saad said a name.

He said it without weight, in the same dry, unhurried voice he had used for all of it, the way a man says a thing he has carried so long that saying it costs him nothing more than the breath, and he set beside the name the three offices it sat above, and the reach of those offices across the Outer Prefectures and the jurisdictions that touched them, three Thrones' worth of administrative ground, and the years the man had held that reach, and the one structural fact that turned a coincidence of access into a certainty, which was that the authorization category Asha had struck against could be issued from exactly one chair in the imperial establishment and that chair was his.

Eleanor knew the name. She had known the man across the better part of forty years, had sat at tables with him, had taken his measure in a dozen quiet rooms and had filed the measure and found it sound and had been, in the long accounting of her trust, wrong. She felt the thing settle that she had carried into the room as a possibility and would carry out of it as a fact, and it settled the way a load settles onto a back that has been braced for it a long time, all at once and without surprise and with no relief in it at all. She did not let it show on her face. The face was sixty years of work and it held. But she was old, and the room was cold, and the man across the table was watching her with eyes that had spent a career reading exactly what she was now refusing to show, and she understood that he saw it land, because he had needed to see it land, because a man who has carried a thing twenty-one years needs to know the moment it has finally been received by hands that can hold it.

"You are certain," she said. It was not a question. She asked it as one so that the record of the afternoon, the record that lived only in three skulls and would never live anywhere else, would show that she had asked.

"I am certain of the chair," he said. "I am certain of the category. I am certain the category and the chair are the same. I am not certain of the man's whole intent, because I have never sat in the room where his intent is spoken, and I will not invent for you the part I do not have. You taught me that, through three men I never met. Do not hand them what you do not hold. I am handing you what I hold. The rest is yours to find, and you will find it faster than I could tell it, and you will find it cleaner, because nothing I have given you points back along the path I came."

"It points back along the path of who could have known to give it."

"Yes." He said it as if it weighed nothing, and then, because the word he had used asked too little of the thing, he said the rest. "The moment I stand up from this chair, the channel is over. Not paused. Over. The intervals will not be kept because I will not be there to keep them, and the absence of the intervals is itself a message, and the man whose chair it is will read the absence within the season, because reading absences is what his office is for and what I have spent twenty-one years helping you read in others. He will know a source went dark. He will draw his short list. My name will be on it, and it will be a short list, and I have made my arrangements for what comes after a short list."

Eleanor looked at him then, fully, for the first time since he had sat, and she let herself look at the face she had refused for twenty-one years, the spare old face of the man who had fed her apparatus from inside the establishment that was now eating itself, and she did not soften it for him and she did not harden it, she only looked, and took the face into the same place she kept the two names she had guessed wrong on, and the dish where the key sat in plain view, and the figures she had let climb in a drawer eleven days while a man stayed alive in the dark she kept him in.

"You came in," she said, "because there was no other way to give it to me clean."

"There was no other way to give it to you at all," he said. "The channel was built to move small things and to move them so that no one could weigh them. This cannot be made small. There is no way to send a name through three men so that none of them knows he is carrying a name. You taught me the channel's limits the day you built it, though you never said a word to me, by what you let it carry and what you never asked it to. I have spent twenty-one years inside the limits of a thing you designed. I know exactly what it cannot do. It cannot do this. So I am doing this." He moved his hands off the table at last and set them on the arms of the chair, the gesture of a man preparing to rise. "I am not asking you to protect me. I know better than any living man what you can and cannot protect, because protecting things at the edge of what can be protected is the trade we have shared without sharing a room. You held a report eleven days for me once. I know you did. I felt the eleven days from the inside as a quiet I could not account for, and I accounted for it later, and I have never told you and I am telling you now so that you will not think the years were spent on a man who did not understand what they cost you to spend. You bought me a year that became twenty. I am giving the year back. That is the whole of the accounting and it is even, and I will not have you treat it as a debt in either direction."

Eleanor was silent a long moment. The light had not changed because there was no sun in it to move. The cold sat in the room and the stopped clock said its quarter past nothing and the fruit turned in the back of the house.

"Then it is even," she said.

He rose. It took him a moment, the old frame negotiating the unfamiliar chair, and neither woman moved to help him, because help would have made him a man being helped in his last act of standing and he had not come to be that, he had come to set a thing down and walk out from under it, and the dignity of walking out from under it was the one thing left in the room that was wholly his. He gained his feet. He squared the dark coat. He looked once more at Kaviel against the wall, the witness who had not spoken and would not, and he inclined his head to her a small degree, an old official's courtesy to a younger one whose name he did not know and would never learn, and Kaviel returned it, the same degree, no more, and that was the whole of what passed between them.

"I will go out the way I came," he said to Eleanor. "Do not have your men walk me. A man walked out of a house is a man someone watched leave. I would rather be a man who was never here."

"You were never here," Eleanor said.

He looked at her a last time. Whatever he had come to find in her face he seemed to have found, because he did not search it further. He turned and crossed the room with the careful tread that knew where strange boards gave, and the door of the front room let him into the hall, and the hall let him toward the door to the dead lane, and Eleanor sat and listened to the tread go, the gate's small sound, and then the afternoon with nothing in it but the cold and the turning fruit and the clock that had stopped years before either of them was born to this.

She did not move for a while. Against the wall, Kaviel waited, having said nothing for the whole of it as she had been told, and she went on saying nothing now, because the instruction had not been lifted and because she had spent enough years across tables from her mother to know that the thing Eleanor did in the minute after a door closed was a thing done alone even when another person was in the room, and that the kindest and the most correct course, which in Eleanor's house were the same course, was to let her have the minute and to mark it and to never once mention having marked it.

So Kaviel watched. She watched her mother sit in the gray light with her hands flat on the table where the old man's hands had been, and she watched the line of the old woman's back, which did not bow, and the set of the old woman's jaw, which did not move, and she watched Eleanor look at the stopped clock for a long time as if it were a document she was reading for what it was built around. Nothing in the face gave. Nothing in the hands. But Kaviel had read documents her whole life for the part the writer did not think to guard, and she read her mother the same way now, against her own will and without meaning to, and she saw in the stillness of the hands flat on the cold table a thing she had no word for and did not try to find one for, and she filed it the way she filed everything, against the gap where it fit, and found that it fit a gap she had not known was in her, and she did not look away from it, because she was her mother's daughter and looking away was the one thing neither of them had ever been permitted.

The light thinned. The early dark of the season came down over the dead lane and the low wall and the unlatched gate, and the room went from gray to grayer, and still Eleanor sat with her hands on the table and Kaviel sat against the wall, and the distance between the two chairs was the whole width of the room and the whole length of the years that had built both women to sit in it without crossing it.

Then Eleanor moved her hands off the table at last, and folded them in her lap, and turned her head toward the chair against the wall where her daughter sat in the failing light.

"Now we know," she said, quietly.

# Chapter 15 — The Junior Researcher's Discovery

The knock came at the eleventh hour, and Kaviel knew before she opened the door that it was Zola, because the knock was wrong.

It was not loud and it was not soft. It was even, three taps spaced the way a person spaces a thing they have rehearsed in a corridor on the walk over so that the hand will not betray what the rest of them is doing, and Kaviel had heard that exact evenness once before, weeks back, from a junior who had been taught to be calm and had not yet learned that the teaching showed. She set down the report she had been reading by the single lamp, the only light she kept in the office past the eighth hour, and she crossed the small room and drew the door open, and Zola Adimu was standing in the corridor with a folder held flat against her chest in both arms the way a person holds a thing they have decided no one will take from them between one room and the next.

"You should be asleep," Kaviel said. It was not a reproach. It was the first reading, the thing she said to learn from how the other person answered it.

"I know the hour." Zola's voice was steady and her eyes were not. There was a brightness in them that Kaviel had seen in the eyes of officers coming off a long observation, the brightness of a person who has not slept and has stopped being able to tell that the not-sleeping has begun to cost them. "I could not leave it in the room. I could not log it and go home and have it sit there until the first hour with no one knowing it was there."

"Come in." Kaviel stepped back and let her through, and she looked past Zola down the corridor before she shut the door, the long stone run of it dim and empty, the watch not yet due to pass, no one at the far end where the stairs went down. She shut the door. She did not lock it, because a locked door in the Quarter at the eleventh hour was a thing a watch officer noted, and she crossed back to the table and turned the lamp's shade up a quarter so the light spread off the desk and onto the part of the floor where the second chair stood.

The office was small and she kept it that way on purpose, the same reasoning Asha kept her room small and the appointments room had been a store. There was the desk, old and chosen for the size of its surface and nothing else, and the two chairs, and the window she cracked for moving air even in the cold, the way she did, so that the room never went stale and never held the smell of a person who had been in it too long. The window was cracked now and the night came in through the gap, the cold of it and the faint reach of the compound after dark, a cart somewhere on the lower yard, the bang of a kitchen door two floors down, the air carrying the last of the evening bread off the banked fires and under it the dry paper smell that lived in every room in the Quarter and never left.

"Set it down," Kaviel said. "On the desk. Lay it out the way you would lay it for Asha."

That was deliberate, and Zola took it the way it was meant, as an instruction to do the thing she had been trained to do and not the thing the hour wanted her to do, which was to talk. She came to the desk and she set the folder down and she opened it, and she did not pour the contents out. She laid them. Kaviel watched her hands and the hands were better than the eyes, the hands were trained and the training held even now, lifting each sheet and squaring it and setting it where the next would follow from it, building the thing on the desk in the order a reader would need it and not the order it had come to her in. The ruled sheets first, the three-column sheets, dozens of them, the columns filled in the slow clean hand Kaviel had watched her learn. Then the source pages drawn against them, the carry-forward entries and the prior rosters, paired, each entry beside the older authority it claimed and did not have. Then at the last, set apart, a single sheet in a hand that was not the work hand, a hand that had been written fast and was the only fast thing in the folder.

Kaviel did not reach for any of it yet. She pulled the second chair around so it faced the desk and she gestured Zola into it, and Zola sat on the edge of it the way she had stood in the appointments room the first morning, a person who had not given herself permission to settle.

"Water first," Kaviel said. There was a covered cup on the shelf and she filled it from the jug and set it by Zola's hand on the desk, away from the sheets. "Drink it. Then tell it from the start, in order, the way the work runs, and do not tell me the end before you have told me how you got there, because I will not be able to use the end if I do not first see the road."

Zola drank. Her hand was not quite steady on the cup and she set it down with care so it would not knock the sheets, and Kaviel saw her take the breath, the long one, the one that put the hour back outside the door and the work back in front of her, and when she spoke again the steadiness in the voice had stopped being a thing she was holding on by will and had become the steadiness of the work itself.

"I finished the easy stacks two weeks ago," Zola said. "Sennar and the two beside it and the four that had already gone hot. You had the count from those. I logged them and brought you the twelves the way you taught me, and you took them to wherever you take them, and I started on the cold stacks, the jurisdictions where nothing had been pointed, where I was running the trace blind because no one had told me a single entry there was bad." She drew the first ruled sheet a little toward Kaviel without lifting it off the desk. "You told me to trace them all. You told me the man who built it salts the clean ones among the bad. So I did not skip the cold jurisdictions because they looked cold. I ran every carry-forward entry in them back to the prior authority it named."

"And."

"And in the cold jurisdictions the entries are not clean." Zola's voice did not rise. The not-rising was the discipline holding and Kaviel marked it and was glad of it. "They are bad the same way. An entry cites a prior commission and a session and a folio, and I go to the prior roster, and the session sat and confirmed eleven names and the name in the entry is not one of them. Or the prior commission the entry names never sat at all in that jurisdiction. The same gap. The same shape. In jurisdictions where nothing was ever pointed and no one was looking and no field report ever came up, the carry-forward provision carries names that no commission confirmed, and it carries them the same way it carries them in Sennar."

Kaviel let that sit a moment in the cold air of the room. She had expected the cold jurisdictions to give her something. She had not let herself decide how much, because deciding in advance was the thing that built a shape out of a wish, and she had learned the cost of that long ago. But she had carried, under the not-deciding, a quiet expectation that the cold jurisdictions would give her a few, that the fraud would thin as it spread from the watershed where it started, that there would be a falling-off at the edges that let her draw a line around it and say it ran this far and no further.

"How many of the cold jurisdictions did you run," she said.

"All of them in the Outer Prefectures." Zola's finger moved down the squared edge of the ruled stack, not touching, counting in the air. "And then I did not stop at the Outer Prefectures."

Kaviel looked up from the sheets to the young face.

"You told me the provision had cousins," Zola said. "The first morning, at the map. You said the carry-forward had cousins under other commissions beyond the Outer Prefectures, and that you had not yet thought to pull them. I wrote it down. I have written down everything you have said to me at that table since the first morning, because I did not know yet which of it was the method and which was you thinking out loud, and I could not afford to lose the method by guessing wrong." She said it without apology and without pride, as a thing a careful person did. "When the Outer Prefectures gave me what they gave me, I went and found the cousins. The reconciliation provisions under the adjoining commissions, the ones seated for the jurisdictions that border the Outer Prefectures on the spinward side and the ones under the river commissions to the south. The clause is not worded the same. But it does the same thing. It lets an appointment into the standing record on the word that a prior authority confirmed it, without the present commission looking again. And it is being used the same way, in those jurisdictions too."

Kaviel did not move. The lamp threw Zola's shadow up the wall behind her and the cracked window let in the bang of the far kitchen door again, and Kaviel sat with her hands flat on her knees and made herself reach for nothing on the desk, because the road was not finished and she had told the girl to walk it to the end and she would walk it.

"Show me the reach," she said. "Not the count. The reach. Lay me the map."

Zola reached into the folder for the thing she had set apart, and it was not the fast-written sheet, it was a second map, hand-drawn, the printer's grid of the Outer Prefectures copied out in Zola's slow rule and extended past its own edges where no printed map went, the adjoining jurisdictions sketched in beyond the border with their numbers and the river commissions' districts pencilled below, the whole of it larger than any single sheet so that she had joined three sheets at the back with paste and folded them, and she unfolded the thing now across the desk over the squared stacks, weighting two corners with the water cup and the lamp base the way the work taught, and on it, in the small flat dots that were Kaviel's own mark, the mark Kaviel had set against three jurisdictions on the first night and Zola had learned from watching her do it, there were dots everywhere.

Not everywhere. Kaviel made herself read it true and not read it for the size of the shock of it. There were jurisdictions with no dot. But the dots ran across the Outer Prefectures in a spread that no watershed held and no single office reached, and they crossed the border into the adjoining jurisdictions where a different commission sat under a different bureau seat, and they ran down into the river districts under the southern commissions, and the spread of them was not the spread of a thing that had leaked from one bad office into its neighbors. It was the spread of a thing that had been placed.

"Three commissions," Kaviel said. She was reading the dots and she said it half to herself. "Three commissions under, what. Tell me the seats. You will have the seats."

"The Bureau of Establishments holds the Third Appointments Commission for the Outer Prefectures." Zola's finger went to the printed grid, to the dots she had set there over weeks. "The adjoining jurisdictions to spinward are confirmed under their own commission, seated separately, but it answers up through the same bureau. The river commissions to the south answer up a different line on paper. On paper they are three commissions under two reporting lines across what the old administrative tables count as three Thrones' worth of jurisdiction." She lifted her finger off the map and set her hands in her lap and looked at Kaviel, and the brightness was back in her eyes and there was something under it now that the work had been holding off for the length of the telling and could not hold off any longer. "But they are not being run as three. They are being run as one. The same gap in every one of them. The provision read the same way. The names placed by hands that had to have been pointed at the provision by the same understanding. I traced the reconciliation back the way you taught me, all the way back, and where the prior authority does not exist, I traced who would have had to authorize the entry to be made at all despite the gap, who sits above all three commissions on a line where the three reporting lines come together." She stopped. "There is one office where they come together. There is one authority that reaches all three."

"Do not name it." Kaviel said it fast and flat and it landed in the room like a hand laid on a moving thing to stop it, and Zola's mouth closed on the word she had not yet said. "You have it on the page. Show me where it is on the page and do not say it in this room out loud. The room is not watched, I do not think the room is watched. You are going to learn tonight to hold a name in your head and never let it past your teeth, and the time to learn that is the first time you have a name to hold and not the tenth."

Zola's hand moved to the fast-written sheet, the one in the hand that was not the work hand, and she turned it face down on the desk without Kaviel asking her to, and Kaviel saw her understand, saw the understanding go into her the way the first morning's distinction had gone in, set where it belonged, not shown on the front of her face.

"It is on that sheet," Zola said quietly. "I worked it last. I worked it at the eighth hour tonight and I checked it twice and I wrote it down because I was afraid I would talk myself out of it, and then I turned the lamp down and I sat in the room with it for an hour and I could not stay in the room with it, and I came here."

You did not look for whether the junior was right. You looked for the place where the junior was wrong and found it before anyone else did, because a finding this size would be torn at by everyone it reached and the place it tore was the place a careful reader found first and shored up first. She read down the columns. The entry. The claimed authority. The gap. The discipline she had taught the girl was a discipline she had not stopped keeping, and she kept it now over a thing that was the largest finding she had read in her life, kept her hands steady on the pages and her face still and her breathing even, because if she broke the discipline in front of the junior at the hour the junior most needed to see it hold, she would teach her in one night to undo everything the weeks had built. So she read, and the room was quiet around the reading, the cracked window and the cold and the far sounds of the compound running on through the night, the watch's boots passing somewhere on a lower floor and not on this one, the dry whisper of the source pages as she squared each one and set it back.

The cold jurisdictions had been run blind and the gaps had stood up out of them on their own. The cousins under the other commissions had been worded differently and read the same and the reading was sound. The map was drawn from the dots and the dots were drawn from the gaps and the gaps were facts. And the one office where the three reporting lines came together had not been named on any work sheet, had been kept off the columns and set on a single page apart and turned face down, because the girl had understood, somewhere in the long hour she could not stay in the room, that the name was the part you did not write into the body of a thing that would be read.

"The second thing," Zola said. She had stood when Kaviel stood.

Kaviel came around the desk and stood in front of her in the spread of the lamp, close, the way she did not usually stand to a junior, close enough that the thing she was about to say would have nowhere to go but into the girl and would not scatter into the cold air of the room.

"You will not speak of this. Not to Asha. Not to your family. Not to anyone, until I tell you." A pause. "You did well."

# Chapter 16 — Message Trace

The room ran in daylight now, and Kaviel had not yet decided whether she liked what the daylight did to it.

For the weeks since she had opened it the north room had been a night room, a single lamp and two people and the cold of the deep masonry, and the work in it had been quiet enough that she could hear a stack squared at the far end of the table. That room was gone. The store-smell had gone with it, beaten out at last by the bodies and the warm air and the lamp oil burned every night, and what filled the room at the second hour of the morning was the sound of a thing at full use. Five people where there had been two. The long table pushed against the north wall to clear the floor, and the floor given over to the racked relay logs Ferran had asked for, the banded volumes set up on their ends in the wooden cradles he had built so that a reader could walk the line of them and pull any one without disturbing its neighbors. The high window gave its flat gray light across the near half of the room and left the racks at the dim end in the shadow they kept all day, and Kaviel had set a second lamp down there, against her own habit, because the daylight that had once been enough for two was not enough for five.

She stood in the doorway a moment before she went in, the way she did with any space she meant to work from, and read it. Asha at the table's near end with the financial side drawn up beside her, the disbursement registers she would not let out of her own keeping. Zola at the racks, slower than anyone and right where the others were fast, working a relay log against a list in her own ruled hand. A second researcher Asha had given up to the room, a careful man named Otieno who did not yet know what he was working and had not asked. And Ferran, at the table's far end, where Kaviel had put him on the first day so that the dim end and the racks were his and the near end with the maps and the names was hers.

Tobias Ferran was the thing in the room she had not built and could not unmake, and she had spent the first day of having him deciding how much of the room he would be allowed to see and the rest of the days holding to it. He was a records-systems specialist out of the transit administration, contracted to the Quarter through a billing line that read as building maintenance, and he had come because the trace needed a thing no Lioness in the compound knew how to do, which was to read the routing record of a private courier message back through the relay points it had passed. He was good. She had known he was good within the first hour, the way she had known Zola was patient, and she had not let the knowing soften the other fact about him, which was that he sat outside the chain. Everyone else in the room had been read and held and made by the Quarter over years. Ferran had been hired. He did not know the names. He did not know the shape. He knew the routing problem she had brought him, and he believed it was a billing fraud in the courier service, a thing of stolen franking and misposted relay fees, because that was what she had told him it was and she had built the cover well enough that the work he did on it was true work and not a lie he could trip over.

"Where are we," she said, coming to the near end of the table.

"Two of three." Ferran did not look up from the log under his hand. He spoke the way he worked, flatly, with the figures first. "The message that went to your registrar in Adwa came in to the office courier service off the private line, not the registry line. Private matter, hand-addressed, no office mark, the franking paid against a standing account. That account is the first point. I have it."

"You have the account."

"I have the account number and the relay it franks through. The account is held in a service name, a forwarding office in Adwa that takes private correspondence for clients who would rather the registry not see it. Lawful. Common. The forwarding office is a wall, not a window, it does not keep who its clients are, it keeps the account and the account pays. But the account is a thing, and a thing routes." He turned the log a quarter toward her, not so she could read it, he had learned in the first day that she did not read his logs, but so the gesture stood for the showing. "The forwarding office did not originate the message. It received it from somewhere and franked it on. Every message it franks carries the relay stamp of where it came in from, because the relay charges the forwarding office and the charge has to post against something. I read the relay stamps back. The forwarding office took your message in from a second relay, a transit hub two jurisdictions north, on the spinward line. That is the second point. I have that one too."

Kaviel set two fingers on the map at the near end of the table, on the spinward jurisdictions where Zola's dots ran across the border, and found the transit hub without being told where it was, because she had read the spinward line for other reasons in other weeks. The second relay sat where the spinward courier lines came together before they ran down toward the Outer Prefectures. North of it the lines thinned and went up toward the bureau seats. She did not say any of that.

"And the third," she said.

"The third is the one that matters and the third is slow." For the first time Ferran looked up, and his face had the small fixed attention of a man who has come to the part of a problem that is worth his time. "The transit hub does not keep client accounts the way the forwarding office does. It is a relay, it moves volume, it posts its charges in bulk against the lines that feed it and reconciles them at the fiscal turn. So I cannot read your message back through it by the account, because at the hub your message is not a message, it is one franking among forty thousand in a posting batch. I have to find the batch your message rode in, and then find what fed the batch, the line that brought that volume into the hub on that day. That is in the hub's own relay reconciliation, and that is what I am pulling now, and it is in those." He nodded at the racks at the dim end, where Zola and Otieno worked. "Three days of the hub's relay reconciliation either side of your message's date. The line that fed the batch is in there. When I have the line, I have the third point, and the third point is where your message entered the courier system to begin with. Whoever wrote it handed it to that line. That is your origin."

"How long."

"Today, if the racks hold what I asked for. Tomorrow if they pulled it short." He went back to the log. "The hub keeps it. The hub keeps everything, it has to, the whole system bills off it. The only question is whether your clerks pulled three days or pulled one and called it three."

"They pulled three," Asha said from the near end, without looking up from her registers, and Ferran took that the way he took most things she said, which was as a fact he did not have standing to argue, and said nothing.

Kaviel left them to it and went down the room to the racks.

This was the part of the trace she had thought about most, the reading of it once he handed her the line. The routing of it was Ferran's and beyond her. She had run the shape of it in her head on the walk down from her own office that morning. The first point was a service name behind a forwarding office, a wall. The second point was a relay batch in a transit hub, a number. Neither of them was a hand. # Chapter 17 — The Protected Individual Returns

The witness facility did not announce itself. It sat below the administrative level of the Lioness compound, down a stair that the watch logged but did not guard, behind a door faced in the same gray panel as the storerooms to either side of it, and a person who did not already know what lay behind it would have read it as one more cupboard in a building full of cupboards. That was the design. Kaviel had walked the approach the day before from three directions, the way she walked every approach, and had satisfied herself that nothing in the route told a watcher where the route went.

The room itself had no window. It had been cleaned that morning, and the cleaning had left its own evidence, the faint chemical sweetness of the scrub the compound used on rooms reserved for this work, the smell that always sat at the back of Kaviel's throat and that she had stopped trying to name years ago. A table, a jug of water and two covered cups, two chairs set out and the others left stacked against the wall. A single light panel flush in the ceiling, even and shadowless. Nothing on the walls, nothing on the table but the water. She had brought no file in, because a file told a witness what you already had and taught her where the gaps were, and a witness who learned the gaps began to fill them, and a witness who filled gaps was no longer a witness.

Meret stood inside the door. She had been there since before Kaviel arrived, and she would be there until the witness had gone, and in the hours between she would say nothing and ask nothing, because that was the work Kaviel had asked of her and Meret did the work she was asked and not the work she imagined. She was in the room so that the room was secure, so that nothing came through the door that Kaviel had not let through, and she stood with her hands loose and her weight even and her eyes on the door, a presence the witness would feel and would not have to look at.

"They are bringing her up," Meret said. It was the second thing she had said since Kaviel came in. The first had been the room's condition, given in three words, which was how Meret gave conditions.

"Slowly," Kaviel said. "I want her settled before I am in front of her."

Meret put her knuckle to the bead at her collar and passed it on, two words, and they waited.

Kaviel had read the intake the way she read everything, once for the facts and once for the seams, and the seams in this intake were the part that had kept her awake. The witness had a name now. She had been a woman on a bench at Sabra Crossing with a document case held in both hands, a protected individual onward routed to a seventh gate and filed closed, and the file Kaviel had filed that night had been clean and defensible and silent on the one thing that mattered, which was that the woman Kaviel moved had come up out of Sennar on a feeder that posted to no board, under a name no one upstream had passed to Kaviel, by a route that the advance brief had gotten wrong in two of its three details. The file did not say the brief had been bad, because Kaviel had not been able to prove it, and a thing she could not prove did not go into a file. She had carried it instead, the woman's face and the bad brief and the man at the document desk with the courier's satchel, into the place she kept things she could not yet do anything with.

The door opened. An operative brought the witness in and withdrew, the way the operative at Meridian had withdrawn, a body in the doorway for the length of time it took and then gone, the door easing shut behind on its own weight.

Kaviel stood. She did not come around the table. A table between them was a thing the witness could keep, a width of it that was hers, and Kaviel left it to her.

Naila Osei was older than the memory by four years and otherwise the woman from the bench. Middling height, a record-office stillness in the way she held herself, hands that wanted an object and found none and folded instead. She had the look of a person told she was safe enough times that she had stopped believing the telling did anything, who came into a windowless room and read the door at her back the way a person reads a thing they have learned to read. She looked at Meret once, took the measure of her, and looked away. She registered the chairs left stacked against the wall, the two set out and no third for an interrogator. A records officer of the peripheral grade missed very little; the work had shaped her the way Kaviel's work had shaped Kaviel.

"Sit, if you would," Kaviel said. "There is water. The room is ours for as long as we need it and no longer than that."

Naila Osei sat. She poured herself water without being asked and did not drink it, set it where she could reach it, a record officer's instinct to have the thing in front of her placed before she needed it. "I know your face," she said. Her voice was low and even, a voice given nothing to carry. "I have spent four years not letting myself know it, and now they bring me to a room and there it is, and I find I knew it the whole time. You sat down beside me at Sabra and told me about a re-route that was overbooked, and you put a voucher on the bench, and you said seventh gate, and you walked away and did not look back."

"I did."

"I have wondered since whether you were real."

"I was the movement," Kaviel said. "I am something else now, and the something else is why you are here, and I am going to be plain with you, because you would catch me if I were not and it would cost us both the hour." She sat, finally, across the width of the table the woman had kept. "You asked, through the channel that kept you, to give what you have. No one sent for you. I want that said first, because what you give me you give freely, and that will matter later in a way I will tell you about before you leave."

"I know what I am here to give." A record officer's flatness, the flatness of a person stating the contents of a folio. "I worked the spur registry at the Meridian feeder out of Jurisdiction Seven for eleven months before they moved me. I was a reconciliation clerk. I entered the carry-forward attestations into the standing record. I am here to tell you that the attestations I entered were false, and that I knew it, and that I entered them anyway, until the day I understood what I was inside, and then I went to the channel and asked to be taken out."

Kaviel let that sit without reaching for it. It was a great deal, given fast and whole, and a witness who gave a great deal fast at the opening was a witness either telling the truth or performing it, and the difference was the thing the next hour was for.

"Tell it in order," Kaviel said. "From the work. Not from the day you understood. Tell me the work first, the way it ran on a day that was nothing, and let the day you understood arrive where it arrived. I will not be able to use the understanding if I have not first seen the floor it stood on."

Naila Osei told it the way Kaviel had hoped she would, in the dry registry voice of a person describing a process she had performed a thousand times, and the dryness was the thing that made it land. The couriers came in at the half-hour through the south gate with the attestation satchels, chopped at the shed window, and the schedules went up to the registry where she sat, and she keyed each carry-forward entry against the prior authority it cited. Where the authority checked she confirmed it into the standing record. Where it did not check she was to enter it regardless, on a separate instruction that came down with the schedule and that she was to burn.

"Who handed you the instruction to enter the ones that did not check," Kaviel said.

"The schedule carried it. A slip folded into the schedule, in a hand that was not the registrar's hand. I was to read it, enter against it, and burn it in the registry stove before the schedule went to file." She turned the water cup a quarter and did not lift it. "I burned a great many slips."

"And the courier."

Something moved in the woman's face, the first thing in it that the registry voice did not cover. "The same man, most quarters. Even features, a build you would not describe. He left the satchel at the shed window and did not wait for the chop, and a courier who does not wait for his chop is carrying paper that is not his to answer for. The shed clerk called him Beye. I never spoke to him. The schedule he left was thicker by a third than the spur's own traffic could ever have warranted, and I keyed it, and I burned the slip."

Kaviel kept her face still over the name and the satchel and the third again, because all three had come to her once already from another hand in another cold room, and a thing that arrived twice by two roads that had never touched was a thing that began to be evidence. She gave nothing, because a witness who saw recognition began to aim for it, and she let the silence open.

"You said there was a day you understood," Kaviel said. "Tell me the day."

Naila Osei was quiet for a moment, the quiet of a person walking back to a room she did not want to stand in again, and then she went in. "A black transit came down the freight spur after the day shed had closed. There is no facility chop on a transit that comes in after close; it enters the gate counter as nothing more than axles. "He did not go to the shed. He went to the registrar's office, which was dark, and the registrar opened it for him and lit the lamp behind the blind, and they were in there the better part of an hour, and the aide stood at the door the whole time and watched the yard."

"Where were you," Kaviel said.

"At my window. I had stayed late to finish a schedule, which the registrar did not know, because the registry sits above the office and a person at its window after dark is not seen from the office door." The flatness held, and under it now there was a thing the flatness was working to hold. "I saw his face when the lamp came up. I had seen it before. The Bureau sends down a circular every year on the standardization of the attestation forms, and the circular carries a plate of the senior table at the New Terra office, the men who sit the session. His face is on that plate. He sits near the convening chair. I had keyed forms standardized at that table for eleven months, and the man whose table standardized them came down a freight spur in the dark to sit an hour in my registrar's office and leave no chop, no entry any ledger I kept could hold, and I understood, sitting at the window in the cold, what I had been entering and whose weight had been behind it."

The room was very quiet. Meret had not moved at the door. The light panel hummed in the register Kaviel felt behind her eyes more than heard, and the scrubbed sweetness of the cleaned room sat at the back of her throat, and across the table the woman who had keyed a fraud for eleven months and gone to the channel and asked to be taken out sat with her hands folded over a cup of water she would not drink, looking at the middle distance of a registry window four years and many jurisdictions away.

Kaviel did not reach for the thing she had just been given. She had it. She had read Silva's field log out of Jurisdiction Seven until she could have set it down from memory, the black transit and the heavy man, the aide who watched the yard, the dark office and the lamp behind the blind. Silva had stood in the lower yard with a folio case and watched the outside of an hour that this woman had watched from the inside, from a registry window above the same office, and the two accounts had locked together across four years and a great distance the way the face and the frame had locked together in the Meridian room, and the locking was the thing the investigation had needed and had lost when the third relay went dead.

The canteen stove, in the dark, before the shift came on. A man who burns at his own stove and not where it is logged is burning a thing he does not want anyone to know was ever paper." # Chapter 18 — Eleanor's Decision

The room had not changed, and that was the first thing Eleanor read when she came into it, the way she read everything, against the version of it she carried in her. She lifted the pot herself, because the absence of servants was the meaning of the room, and the steam came off the cups and broke against the cold of the walls and was gone before either of them touched the porcelain. The smell of it reached Eleanor faint and bitter and herbal, the same leaf they had drunk and not drunk the last time, and she took the cup when Amira set it an inch nearer her, wrapped both hands around it and did not drink, and let the small warmth last the minute it would last in a room this cold.

"You asked for the room," Amira said. "You have not asked for the room since the spring."

"No."

"The last time you asked for it, you were buying time you would not name. You bought two weeks. You brought me the report whole on the thirteenth day, a day under, and I have not forgotten that either."

"You flinched at none of it," Eleanor said. "I told you most rulers cannot do that. You did it. I have weighed the doing of it ever since, and it has changed what I am willing to bring into this room."

Amira drank, the cup raised and lowered with nothing on her face, and Eleanor watched the absence of expression and filed it the way she filed all such absences, as a kind of information about a person who had learned, from her, that the giving of nothing was itself a discipline. The first exchange had been made and answered, and both of them knew it for one exchange and not two. Amira had told Eleanor that she remembered the cost of the last trust she had extended. Eleanor had told Amira that the extending of it had earned her something she was about to spend.

"I have the whole of it now," Eleanor said. "Not the shape. The body. The list, and the mechanism that kept the list invisible, and the reach of the office that issued the protection. My people have it documented to the standard the throne would require if the throne were ready to require it. The scope is larger than I brought you in the spring. A thing built to seem ordinary is built wide, because width is what lets each piece of it pass for routine. This one runs across the Outer Prefectures and into the jurisdictions that touch them. Three Thrones' worth of administrative ground answers, in the end, to one chair."

Amira set the cup down. The small sound of porcelain on wood was the loudest thing the room had made. Outside, past the garden no one watched, a bell in the administrative wing marked an hour, the morning hour this time and not the evening one, a higher and busier sound with the day's work moving under it, and it faded and left the stillness in the room heavier than the noise had been.

"A chair," Amira said.

"A chair, and the man in it, and I know the man." Eleanor turned the cup a quarter in her hands. "I have known him the better part of forty years. I took his measure in rooms quieter than this one and found it sound, and I was wrong, and the being wrong is mine to carry. You do not need his name this morning. You will have it when you need it, and the having of it will be a weapon, and I will not put a weapon in the room before I have told you what I mean to do with the hand that would wield it."

Amira's eyes came up at that and stayed. She had a way of going still that was not the stillness of waiting; it was the stillness of a mind that had heard a thing arrive ahead of its meaning and was clearing room for the meaning to land. The garden window let the gray light fall across the side of her face and gave the cheekbone no shadow, and she let the quiet run, because she had learned from the woman across the table that the quiet after a sentence was where the sentence finished itself.

"You have decided something," Amira said. "You came in deciding it, the way you came in deciding the two weeks. You let me open the conversation so the thing you want will arrive as the answer to a question instead of a demand into silence. You taught me the maneuver, and I use it on petitioners who do not know they are being used by it, and it is strange to sit on the near side of it and recognize the hand."

"Then I will not maneuver," Eleanor said. "It is too important to waste the hour on technique, and you are too able to make it cost more than it earns. I am going to ask you for a thing, and ask it plainly, and the plainness is the only assurance I can offer that I am not handling you."

"Ask."

"Three days. Before any formal action. Before the name moves out of this room, before a warrant is drawn, before a single office of the establishment learns that the throne has seen what I have seen. Three days in which the throne does nothing and instructs no one and signs nothing. After the three days, the timing is yours, and the name is yours, and the apparatus I have built is yours to point wherever you point it. I will not ask for an hour past the three days. I have never asked you for time I wasted. I am not asking now."

Amira did not answer at once. She poured into her own cup, though the first was not finished, the way a person pours to fill the space where a decision is being made, and the fresh liquid steamed and the steam died against the cold and the bitter smell of it hung a moment and thinned. She set the pot down and looked at the cup and not at Eleanor, because to look at Eleanor while deciding was to hand Eleanor the decision early, and she had learned that from Eleanor too.

"In the spring," Amira said, "you told me what the two weeks were for. You told me there was a person whose continuance depended on the matter being handled in a way that did not announce how you had learned of it. You named the cost without naming the person. You bought the time to protect a source. I extended the trust because the request had a shape I could see the back of, even where I could not see the face."

"Yes."

"This request has no back to it." Amira turned the cup a half on the table, the porcelain dragging faint on the wood. "You are asking me to do nothing for three days, and you are not telling me what you will do in them. You are not telling me what the three days buy. You are not telling me whether you mean to act inside them or only to wait. You are asking for the time and withholding the use of the time, and that is a different request than the spring, and we both know it is, and you have come in this morning knowing I would know."

"I have."

"Then tell me why I should grant it."

Eleanor was quiet a moment. The cold of the room had reached her hands fully now, and the cup gave back nothing, and she set it down on the tray with both hands so that it would not rattle, and folded her hands again over the cane.

"Because you have granted it before," she said, "to a harder version of me, in this room, for a use the throne was never told. Your grandmother granted me time once whose purpose I never gave her and never will. She did not like granting it. She granted it because the alternative to trusting the house was governing without the house, and she had measured both, and the house was the colder comfort and the surer one. I watched her decide it. You are sitting this morning where she sat. I am asking you to do the thing she did, and to like it as little as she liked it, and to do it anyway, because the arithmetic has not changed in two hundred years and will not change for our convenience."

"My grandmother granted you time to protect a source," Amira said. "That is the work the compact is for. I understood her the first time you sat in that chair. You are asking me for time to do a thing you will not describe, and I cannot tell, from where I sit, whether it serves the throne or serves your house or serves only you, and you are asking me to grant it without resolving which."

"I am."

"Eleanor."

The name, plainly used, was a pressure laid at a point, the way it had been the last time, and Eleanor felt the point and did not move from it. Amira did not use the given name often. In the throne room she said De Grosvenor, or she said nothing and let a look carry it. Here, with no scribe to read familiarity into it, the name was an instrument, and the woman wielding it had learned the wielding from the woman she aimed it at.

"You are afraid," Amira said, "that if you tell me what the three days are for, I will forbid it. You have measured the odds that I would, and found them high enough that you would rather ask for the time blind than ask for it whole and be refused. You have decided to do something I would not permit if I knew it, and you have come to buy the room to do it in. That is the request. Say whether it is the request."

Eleanor looked at her across the low table and the two cups going cold, and the gray light lay on both of them and gave neither a shadow, and the seams in the floor showed faint under the polish where the old breaks had been mended to look whole.

"It is near enough that I will not insult you by correcting the margin," she said. "Yes. There is a thing I mean to do that you would forbid, and you would be right by your own measure to forbid it, and the forbidding would cost the throne more than the doing, and the judging is mine because it is the work my house exists to do. I am asking you to lend me three days of doing nothing while I take onto my house a risk the throne should not be seen to take and cannot be made to wear. The compact was drafted by people who did not trust each other and needed each other regardless. This is the use it was drafted for, the ugliest and the rarest, the reason the terms were written so they could be honored without ever being comfortable."

Amira rose then. She did it without haste, smoothing the front of her robe with one hand, and crossed to the window she had been ignoring, and stood before the glass with her hands joined behind her at the small of her back in the posture she took on the dais when a petition ran long and she wished to weigh it without a hundred faces reading her. The pane had fogged faintly where the cold of the glass met the warmer air, and her breath added to it, and through the blur the dead path and the rusting tool and the heavy hedge lost their edges and became a gray field with shapes in it.

She stood a long time. The bell in the administrative wing marked another hour, the day deepening under it, the ordinary traffic of the establishment moving through corridors that did not know what was being decided in a room without a scribe. Eleanor did not fill the silence. She had taught the Empress that a ruler who fills her own silences has surrendered them.

"If I grant the three days," Amira said to the glass, "and the thing you do inside them goes wrong, I will have granted the time, and the granting is the throne's act even where the doing is yours. You are asking me to put my name to a blank page and trust the house to write nothing on it I would be unable to stand behind."

"No," Eleanor said. "I am asking you to put your name to nothing at all. There is no page. The three days exist only as an absence, an action the throne declines to take, and an absence cannot be read back to you as authorization, because you will have authorized nothing, signed nothing, instructed no one. If the thing I do goes wrong, it is a thing the head of the De Grosvenor house did on her own standing, and the throne's defense is the truth, that it knew nothing and was governing in good faith on the information it had. I have built it so the absence protects you. That is the half of this you have not yet seen. The three days are a thing I give you, dressed as a thing I ask."

Amira turned from the window. The fogged glass had not cleared, and behind her the gray field of the garden held its blurred shapes, and the light came in flat and laid no shadow on the floor between them.

"You have built it so that I am protected whether I grant the time or refuse it," she said. "If I refuse, the throne moves in the open and you are not a factor in it. If I grant, I do nothing, and the doing of nothing cannot be turned against me, and whatever you do inside the three days is yours to wear alone. You have built a request I cannot lose by answering, which means you have built it so that the only loser available is you."

"I have weighed who can afford the loss," Eleanor said. "I am old. The house outlives its heads; that is the point of a house. The throne does not outlive a misstep taken in the open at the wrong hour. I am the cheaper thing to spend, in any accounting that runs longer than a life, and I have run my accounting longer than my life since before you were crowned."

Amira came back to the chair and sat. She had not meant to sit again; standing had been the closing posture, the signal that the meeting neared its end, and to sit again was to reopen what she had begun to close, and Eleanor marked the reopening and gave no sign of marking it.

"There is a version of this," Amira said, "in which the three days are for your house, and the protection you describe is the wrapping you put on it so I would take it. I cannot rule it out from where I sit. The man at the top of the thing has sat at tables with you for forty years, and the shape of your request is also the shape it would take if you meant to reach him for a reason of your own."

Eleanor did not look away from it. "You would be a poor ruler if you did not say it. If my house were the danger, I would not spend my standing to buy time I could have taken without asking. I would have brought you the list incomplete and steered you past the large part of it while you congratulated yourself on the small. That I have brought you the whole, and built the three days so the cost falls on me and the protection on you, is the behavior of a house that is not betraying you. It is not proof. There is no proof. It is the behavior, offered in the place where proof does not exist, and you will weigh it as that or you will not grant the time."

The two of them sat with the cold cups between them and the gray light on the mended floor, and the bell in the wing did not ring, and the garden held its blurred shapes behind the unwiped fog on the glass. Amira looked at her hands a while, and then at the woman across the table, and the looking was the decision arriving and being read in the same moment, because there was no longer a use in concealing it from each other.

"Three days," Amira said. "From this hour. The throne does nothing, instructs no one, signs nothing. I will hold the morning's work as though this conversation had not occurred, and I will be, in the matter, a ruler governing in good faith on the information she has, which will be true, because I will refuse the rest of the information until the three days have run. When they have run, you bring me the name, and you bring it to this room, and there is no scribe, and after that the timing is mine again and you do not ask me for an hour past it."

"After that the timing is yours," Eleanor said.

"I am not granting this because I trust the thing you will do. I do not know the thing you will do. I am granting it because in the whole of my reign your house has not failed the throne in a way I could prove, and because the request is built so that the only person it can ruin is the person asking for it, and a house bent on betrayal does not build the trap so that it springs on itself." Amira set both hands flat on the low table, the gesture deliberate, the way a person sets down a weight she has chosen to carry rather than drop. "And because my grandmother granted you the same and the throne survived it. I did not understand her then. I understand her this morning. I dislike understanding her. I am doing it regardless."

"That is the whole of what the compact ever asked of either of us," Eleanor said. "To do the thing we dislike, regardless, because the disliking is cheaper across the years and dearer in the hour. We have not improved on the people who drafted the terms. We have only kept the house they built, where the roof leaks at the windows they wanted, and learned to set the buckets without complaint."

Amira rose again, and this time the rising held, and the room came back to its proper order, the Empress on her feet and the head of the serving house seated, the given names set down and the titles taken up. She moved toward the door that led to her quarters, and at it she paused, not turning fully, her profile lit cold and flat from the garden window she still had not looked at, the fog of her own breath gone from the glass behind her.

"The thing you mean to do," she said. "When you bring me the name. I will want to know whether you did it because the compact required it, or because you chose it and called it the compact."

"You will not like the answer," Eleanor said.

"I rarely do."

Eleanor rose then, slowly, the cane taking weight for the first time since she had entered, and she set the untouched cup back on the tray with both hands so that it would not rattle, and she inclined her head the precise depth that the head of the De Grosvenor house inclined it to the Trinitarian Throne, and no further, and the gray light lay even on the mended floor between them, and the door to the Empress's quarters closed without sound.

She told Kaviel in a corridor.

It was not the office, where the single lamp burned past the eighth hour, and it was not the investigation room, where the team now worked the days at full capacity. It was a corridor in the administrative wing, a length of cold stone between two doors that led somewhere else, the kind of place a person crosses without staying, and Eleanor stopped in the middle of it where the light from a high window fell in a flat band across the floor and did not warm it, and Kaviel stopped because her mother had stopped.

Eleanor did not turn to face her fully. She stood with the cane set and her weight even on it and looked at the band of light on the stone, and she said it in the voice she used for instructions, the voice that asked nothing of the room.

"I am going to the man myself," she said. "Alone. Before any formal action. The Empress has granted three days. They begin this morning. On the third day I bring his name to her and the timing returns to the throne. Between now and then I will see him, and you will not be present, and you will not arrange a presence, and you will hold the room and the list and the team exactly as they are and move nothing. If anyone asks what the house is doing in these three days, the house is doing what it always does, which is waiting."

Kaviel said nothing. She had been trained from before she could write that the thing her mother did in the minute after she set a weight down was a thing done alone even when another was in the room, and she gave the minute now, and held it, and marked the holding and meant never to mention it.

She did not ask why. She knew her mother would not have built the three days, would not have spent the standing in the reception room, would not have stood in a cold corridor to say it in the instruction voice, if there had been a version of the thing that ran through the apparatus and stayed safe. She knew the man's name; she had been against the wall in the dead woman's house, in the chair the light did not reach, and had heard the old magister say it without weight. She knew that a person who carries a name like that into a room alone, before the throne has moved and before the apparatus can stand behind her, does it so that whatever happens in the room falls on one person and not on the house and not on the throne. She knew her mother had built the three days the way she built everything, so that the cost would land where Eleanor had decided it should, which was on Eleanor. And she knew there was no instrument she could offer that her mother had not already weighed and set aside, because an instrument would only move the cost off the place Eleanor had chosen for it, and to argue the choice was to ask the old woman to be someone other than the one who had raised her to recognize exactly this and not look away.

"Three days," Kaviel said.

"Three days."

"And nothing moves until you bring her the name."

"Nothing moves."

Eleanor turned her head then, the smallest degree, enough to bring her daughter into the edge of her looking and no further, and whatever she found in Kaviel's face she did not search, and Kaviel gave her the same nothing she had given the magister in the dead woman's house, the correct nothing, the only form of agreement that did not insult the gravity of the thing. Eleanor inclined her head a fraction, a smaller depth than she gave the throne, one that had no name and was kept only for the girl, and she turned and crossed the band of cold light and went out through the far door, the cane taking its even weight on the stone, and the sound of it faded by degrees and was gone.

Kaviel stood alone in the corridor. The high window let the flat light down across the stone and the band of it lay where her mother had been standing, and from somewhere below in the administrative wing came the smell of the day's first meal carried up through the shared vents, bread and something cooked too long, the smell of a building that did not know, and a clerk's footsteps passed in the corridor beyond the far door and did not slow, and the cold came up through the floor and found her where she stood without moving.

She had read documents her life long for the part the writer did not think to guard, and she read this the same way, against her will and without trying, and she saw what her mother meant to do and why it had to be done alone and why the doing of it placed the old woman at the end of a thing from which the house could not reach her, and she stood in the cold band of light in the empty corridor and understood the whole of it, and wished, with everything she had been trained never to show and showed now to no one because there was no one to show it to, that she did not.

# Interlude 4 — Historical Note on the De Grosvenor Compact

I set down here what the record permits me to establish about the agreement under which the De Grosvenor house has served the Trinitarian Throne, because the decision Eleanor De Grosvenor took in the months this account concerns cannot be weighed without it. A reader who does not know the terms of the compact will read her choice as recklessness. A reader who knows them will read it differently, and I believe the second reading is the truer one. I will keep to what the documents support and mark where they do not.

The compact predates both the Empress Amira and Eleanor by a wide margin. Its founding instrument survives in the standing archive in two copies, one held under the Throne's seal and one under the seal of the house, and the two agree in every clause, which is itself a thing worth noting, since instruments of that age usually drift apart in the copying. It was formalized under the administrative reforms that followed the reign of Lucius Luceron II, the Soldier Emperor, who left behind a Throne that governed by process where his predecessors had governed by presence. The compact belongs to that work. It was drafted by clerks, not by heroes, and its language is the flat language of an establishment instrument. That flatness has preserved it. Three imperial generations have passed under it without amending a word.

The terms are short. The house places its intelligence and administrative apparatus at the disposal of the reigning sovereign. The sovereign, in turn, grants the house a latitude no other instrument in the imperial body grants to anyone: the head of the house may act on the sovereign's behalf without prior authorization where seeking that authorization would defeat the action, and the sovereign agrees in advance to be bound by what is done in good faith under that latitude, whether or not the sovereign would have approved it had there been time to ask. I have read the clause perhaps two hundred times. It is the most dangerous sentence in the imperial archive, and it was written by a clerk who understood exactly what he was setting down. The compact does not ask the sovereign to trust the house. It requires the sovereign to be bound by trust already spent, after the fact, when the cost has already landed.

Such an instrument should have failed within a generation. It did not, and the reason it did not is the part of the record I most want the reader to carry forward. The archive shows three prior occasions on which the latitude was exercised at its full reach, each under a different sovereign and a different head of the house, and each preserved in enough detail to study.

The first came under an early reign I will not date precisely, the founding instrument being then less than thirty years old. An earlier matriarch of the house suppressed a report the sovereign had ordered released, held it through a season of pressure that cost her standing she never recovered, and was vindicated only when the source she had protected by holding it proved to be the single thread by which a succession crisis was unknotted. The sovereign had not authorized the holding. The sovereign was bound by it. The record notes, dryly, that the matriarch and the sovereign did not speak again for two years and that the apparatus functioned without interruption throughout.

The second I can date with confidence to the middle reign of the three, the household accounts of that period being intact. The head of the house in that generation moved imperial funds across a jurisdictional boundary, without the sovereign's knowledge, to settle a debt the sovereign did not know the Throne had incurred, and absorbed the irregularity into the house's own books rather than enter it where it could be traced to the sovereign. The accounts carry the loss to this day, unrecovered. The sovereign learned of it eleven years later, from a man in my own posting, reading the same kind of pages I read now.

The third is recent enough that men still living remember its public face, though almost none of them know its private one. The head of the house in that instance withheld the name of a compromised official from a sovereign who had every right to demand it, withheld it for the length of time required to be certain, and bore alone the risk that the certainty would never come. It did come. The official fell. The reign held.

In each of the three the same shape appears, and the shape is the point. One party extends trust the other has not formally earned and cannot formally compel, and does so under a clause that converts that trust into an obligation the sovereign cannot afterward refuse. The compact does not survive because the parties are loyal. Loyalty wears thin under three generations. It survives because the instrument was built to spend trust ahead of proof and to make the spending binding, so that the question is never whether the sovereign will agree, the question is only whether the head of the house will dare to act knowing the sovereign was never asked.

This brings me to Eleanor De Grosvenor and to the decision she took when she had learned the full reach of the matter recorded in these pages and learned, with it, the name at the top of it, a name she had known across decades. She did not bring that name to the Empress and ask leave to act. She asked instead for three days and gave no reason, and the Empress granted them, and the granting was itself an act under the compact, trust spent forward against a proof not yet shown. What Eleanor intended to do in those three days she told no one, and I have not reconstructed it from anything she said, because she said nothing I can cite. I have reconstructed it from the instrument. By the terms of the compact she was about to exercise the latitude at its full reach for the fourth time in the agreement's history, alone, against an official whose authority spanned three Thrones' worth of administrative ground, before any formal action could shield her, knowing that the moment she moved he would know he had been found.

The wax on the founding copy of the compact has gone the color of old tea, and the seal of the house pressed into it has lost the fine lines of its device, so that I must turn the page to the angled light from the high window to read the matrix at all. It was made by a clerk who knew where an empire is held upright at its worst hour. The weight does not rest with the sovereign, and it does not rest with the house. It rests on the willingness of one of them to act first and be judged after. What follows in this record is that clause being exercised as it was always built to be exercised, by the one person then alive who understood it well enough to pay what it asks.
