## 1. EXECUTIVE ANSWER

**Buy in Guyana. Do not buy in Trinidad and export.**

Four findings drive that conclusion, and all four are independent of any quotation.

**1. Guyana is the resource.** Wallaba (*Eperua falcata*) is the species the world uses for tropical hardwood transmission and light poles, it grows in abundance across Guyana, and Guyana is its principal commercial source. Under Guyana's exported roundwood category, poles account for 24 percent of volume, behind piles at 75 percent. A pole industry of that size is a production industry, not a retail counter.

**2. Guyana buys wooden light poles for its own network today.** Guyana Power and Light has an open bid for construction of a **timber revetment for timber light poles along Lamaha Street** in Georgetown, and Hinterland Electrification Company Inc. has invited bids for the **supply and delivery of wallaba poles and crossarms** for network expansion at Mahdia, with an eight week supply period from contract signing. Wooden light poles are a current, accepted, in service standard in the destination country. That removes the approval risk that normally attaches to a timber pole.

**3. Trinidad is a net importer of the same product.** Trinidad's state forest estate is roughly **10,000 hectares of teak and 4,500 hectares of Caribbean pine**. Its specialist timber merchants stock greenheart and mora, which are Guyanese species, meaning the Trinidad trade buys from Guyana. The only Trinidad producer of treated poles identified anywhere in the literature is **Brickfield Forest Industries**, which produces creosoted posts and poles from teak plantation thinnings, and no current contact, capacity or price for that operation could be confirmed. Buying poles in Trinidad for a Guyana project risks buying Guyanese timber at a Trinidad margin and paying to ship it back to its country of origin.

**4. The freight asymmetry is severe and it runs the wrong way.** Two hundred 30 ft wallaba poles weigh an estimated **63 to 70 tonnes** (calculation at Section 6). That is a break bulk or multi flat rack movement, not a parcel. Guyana sourcing carries road haulage only. Trinidad sourcing carries inland haulage to Port of Spain, loading, export clearance, ocean freight, Georgetown port charges, customs entry and inland haulage at the far end, against a product Trinidad does not natively produce.

## 2. THE MATERIAL SPECIFICATION QUESTION THAT NEEDS YOUR DECISION

The requirement says **treated** wooden poles. The product Guyana sells is **naturally durable** wooden poles. These are two different technical routes to the same service life, and the difference will appear in your tender documents, your certificates and your warranty.

Published wood technology on wallaba states that the species is **self impregnated by nature, so creosoting is unnecessary**, that its natural oily resins and high gum exudates protect it against insect attack and decay, that the heartwood is durable and resistant to subterranean termites, and that service life in ground contact is quoted by the trade at **over 50 years**. Wallaba carries a basic specific gravity of 0.78 and an air dry density of about 58 pounds per cubic foot, and it is described as roughly three times stronger than southern yellow pine. Its one recorded weakness is **low resistance to marine borers**, which matters for tidal and saline immersion and does not matter for a roadway lighting pole set in soil.

So you have a choice, and it is yours to make:

**Route A. Naturally durable untreated wallaba, Guyanese origin.** Lowest cost, shortest chain, proven in the destination network, no treatment plant dependency, no preservative documentation. It will **not** satisfy a specification clause that reads "pressure treated" on its face, and it will not carry a treatment certificate or a retention figure.

**Route B. Pressure treated poles.** Satisfies a literal reading of the requirement, carries an ANSI O5.1 class, a preservative, a retention in pounds per cubic foot and a treatment certificate. It costs more, and in Guyana it depends on whether a commercial pressure treatment plant will take the work. Dun and Bradstreet lists **six sawmill and wood preservation companies in Guyana**, across East Bank Demerara, Georgetown, Linden and Essequibo, so treatment capacity plausibly exists, but no plant was confirmed by name and no treatment tariff was found. Imported CCA treated southern yellow pine or Douglas fir is the fallback, at a materially higher landed cost.

**Recommendation: adopt Route A and write the specification around natural durability**, with a clause permitting an equivalent pressure treated pole as an alternate. Ask each bidder at Section 11 to price both. Do not let a word in a specification force you to import a weaker pole at a higher price into the country that grows the stronger one.

## 3. TECHNICAL SUITABILITY FOR LIGHTING, CATEGORY A VERSUS CATEGORY B

The requirement distinguishes a treated wooden **utility pole** suitable for lighting from an **untreated construction post**. Applying that test to what was found:

**CATEGORY A. Wooden utility pole, technically suitable for a street light fixture or lighting arm.**

* Wallaba round transmission poles and round poles, Guyanese origin. Documented end use includes transmission poles and light poles. Documented in service use on the Guyana network through GPL's Lamaha Street timber light poles and HECI's wallaba pole procurement. Ground contact rated by trade description. Suitable for a bracket or arm mounted luminaire subject to a structural check of the top diameter against the arm moment.
* Greenheart round poles, Guyanese origin. Heavier and stronger than wallaba, conventionally sold as marine piling, and priced accordingly. Technically over specified for lighting duty and financially wasteful for it.
* Imported ANSI O5.1 CCA treated southern yellow pine or Douglas fir poles, classes 1 to 6, from North American producers. Purpose built utility and light poles. Highest landed cost of the credible options.

**CATEGORY B. Untreated construction post or ordinary timber, excluded unless commercially treated.**

* Rough and dressed local pitch pine, teak and boxing board from Trinidad sawmills. These are building materials. They are not poles, they carry no pole class, and they are excluded from this requirement unless a treatment plant takes them to a ground contact or utility pole standard.
* General hardware and lumber yard stock in both countries. Excluded on the same ground.

**Excluded on material, per the requirement:** GuyCrete Inc. of Lot 3 Sandy Babb Street, Kitty, Georgetown, telephone 226-5225 and 600-1767, is recorded as the sole manufacturer of concrete utility poles in Guyana and has supplied street light posts to the Ministry of Public Works and pilot programmes for GPL. Concrete is outside scope and no concrete option is carried forward. The entry is retained only because it explains why part of Guyana's street lighting demand has moved away from timber, which affects how much spare pole capacity a mill may have.

**The CARICOM origin trap, and it is the decisive commercial point on this route.** Intra CARICOM trade is duty free on a CARICOM Certificate of Origin, and the certificate confirms that the goods were produced **or substantially transformed** in the exporting state. **This is the whole argument in one table.** The Guyana route deletes ocean freight, export handling, import duty, two sets of port charges, phytosanitary certification and a foreign trading margin. It does not delete them by a percentage. It deletes the lines.
